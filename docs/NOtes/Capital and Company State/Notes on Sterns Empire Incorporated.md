# Notes on Philip J. Stern, _Empire, Incorporated: The Corporations That Built British Colonialism_

**Belknap Press / Harvard University Press, 2023.**

---

## 1. What the book is and what it argues

A 400-year history (c. 1550–present) of British colonialism told through the **joint-stock corporation** rather than through sovereigns and statesmen. Stern calls his subject **"venture colonialism"** (also "corporate" or "chartered" colonialism): a brand of overseas expansion bound together across four centuries by the conviction that _the public business of empire was best done by private enterprise._

**The central thesis.** The British Empire — including the "official" Crown-and-Parliament empire — was built in large part _by, and out of, corporations_. The empire directly governed by the state was itself an "incorporated empire," assembled by absorbing, regulating, imitating, and co-opting private chartered bodies. This was a process that was "**far more iterative than inevitable**": in every generation new institutions, ideologies, and legal regimes declared chartered colonialism dead, yet it kept being "reformulated and transformed."

**The corporation as a paradox.** Stern's organizing claim is that the colonial joint-stock corporation was never an "inexorable juggernaut." It was a _productive contradiction_ — simultaneously person and group, public and private, commercial and political, mercantilist and capitalist, sycophantic and rebellious, regional and global, immortal and fragile. Its very ambiguity ("jurisdictional evasiveness") is what made it so well-suited to empire.

**Two fused concepts.** The book hinges on the gradual union of two old ideas:

- **Joint stock** — dividing an enterprise into purchasable shares, so capital, risk, management, and information could be drawn from "anywhere and anyone" (a financial _and_ a political/social innovation: a shareholder was an "adventurer," and could become a governor without expertise).
- **The corporation** — a legally fictional/artificial person that could sue, hold property, make its own laws, and live forever. Derived less from commerce than from medieval religious and civic bodies (universities, municipalities, monasteries, the _corpus_ of Christ) and from the "king's two bodies."

**Key conceptual coinages and motifs:**

- **"Portfolio colonialism" / "commodified sovereignty"** — because chartered rights could be bought, sold, bequeathed, and parceled, an individual could own "a share of sovereignty in various places around the world without ever leaving home." Charters often had little real asset backing; company colonialism could resemble "a laundering racket mixed with what might later be called a Ponzi or pyramid scheme."
- **"Limited moral liability"** — joint stocks let shareholders participate in faraway violence at a remove (quoting Herbert Spencer: corporate conscience is inferior to individual conscience).
- **Charters were bad law** — vague, aspirational, inconsistently enforced, superimposed over indigenous sovereignty and rival European and even English claims. Real rights abroad had to be _acquired_ abroad through treaties, purchase, pressure, conquest, or "outright fabrication."
- **A "chaos theory of empire"** — viewed at the right scale, the seemingly random welter of competing colonial projects reveals "ordered patterns." Empire was "uncoordinated but never unintentional."

---

## 2. Method and historiographical stance

- **"Process, not product."** Stern wants to understand empire as something continuously _enacted_ ("always in the process of being enacted" — every ship, treaty, share sale, lawsuit, and pamphlet).
- **"Genealogy, not analogy."** He resists treating early companies as mere mirrors of today's tech giants or "private empires" (Facebook "more like a government," ExxonMobil's "private empire," the Crown as "the Firm"). The present is connected to the past by genealogy and "the faintest of family resemblances," not by neat analogy.
- **Collective biography + narrative history.** The book is "an experiment in collective legal, intellectual, and institutional biography" — aggregating "micro-intellectual" histories onto a macro scale. He explicitly says he is putting a _supporting character_ (the corporation) at the center, **not to replace one master narrative with another**, but to show that imperial history "demands many different, often conflicting narrative possibilities at once."
- **A history that does not "see like a state."** It dissolves the standard taxonomies of imperial history (chartered/proprietary/Crown colony; protectorate/mandate/dependency/dominion/sphere of influence) because corporate colonialism cut across all of them.
- **Self-described as synthesis.** In the acknowledgments he frames the book as "a work of synthesis" and a "tribute" to the long tradition and recent boom in scholarship on colonial companies. So most named historians are cited _as allies_; his arguments are with larger interpretive frameworks.

---

## 3. Where the book argues with other works (historiographical interventions)

This is the through-line of the book's polemic. The disagreements operate at the level of _frameworks_, not usually individual monographs.

### (a) Against the "absence of mind" and "official mind" theses

- **J. R. Seeley** — Stern repeatedly punctures Seeley's famous line that Britain acquired its empire "in a fit of absence of mind," and Seeley's view (in _The Expansion of England_, 1882) that the state always swept up by "supreme control" whatever adventurers conquered. Stern's counter: empire was acquired _not_ in an absence of mind but "possibly in the absence of an **'official mind'**" centered exclusively in the state.
- **Robinson & Gallagher's "official mind of imperialism"** — the phrase is deliberately inverted. Stern decenters the metropolitan policymaking state as the engine of empire.

### (b) Against treating corporate empire as a curious anomaly or a purely commercial sideshow

- Stern insists 19th- and 20th-century European empires were _full_ of corporations, which were "neither the curious anomalies nor purely commercial enterprises some at the time and many since took them to be."
- He pushes back on the standard image of a "muddled," "chaotic pluralism" — what one historian likened to Adam Smith's costly, illusory "project of an empire." Stern reframes chaos and anarchy as _deliberate and effective_ weapons of expansion.

### (c) Engaging Lauren Benton (sympathetically, then extending)

- He adopts **Benton's** "agents in empire" and the "irregular thrust of imperial jurisdiction into extra-European space" and her "jurisdictional" approach — but pushes the corporation to the foreground as the chief such agent.

### (d) The capitalism-and-imperialism debate (the book positions itself among, and against, all the classic theorists)

- **Adam Smith** — the recurring foil. Smith condemned the "exclusive corporation spirit" and "mercantile system"; held joint stocks bred "negligence and profusion" (directors trafficking in "other people's money"); called the government of an exclusive merchant company "perhaps the worst of all governments." Stern notes Smith's _inconsistencies_ (the quiet caveats for banks/insurance/infrastructure, the "tortured logic" exempting the **Hudson's Bay Company**, the concession that a temporary monopoly might be fine "at the outset"). Stern's larger point: the pro-venture-colonialism argument resembles **modern neoliberalism**, yet its supposed patron saint, Smith, thought chartered companies an anachronism — exposing a contradiction at the heart of liberal economic thought.
- **J. A. Hobson** (finance capital as the "taproot of imperialism"), **Lenin** (imperialism as the highest, "parasitic" stage of monopoly capitalism), and **Joseph Schumpeter** (colonialism as a pre-capitalist "social atavism" that capitalism should dissolve) — Stern stages these against one another and argues the corporation's "bivalent nature" _explains but does not resolve_ the dispute. His verdict: venture colonialism was "neither anathema to capitalism nor coterminous with it." If imperialism was an atavism, it was "less a social than a legal one, written into empire's genetics."
- **Cain & Hopkins' "gentlemanly capitalism"** and the literatures of "informal," "business," and "economic" empire — Stern says his actors (gentlemanly capitalists, felons, and foreigners alike) "cannot be so neatly classified" into these categories.

### (e) The "company-state" literature — including his own earlier work

- Stern coined/popularized "**company-state**" (his earlier book on the EIC). Here he explicitly **decenters** it: bodies like the EIC and HBC were only the "dominant species" within a "**vast and diverse ecosystem**" of corporate enterprises — most of them small, failed, or merely imagined. This is a self-correction/expansion: don't mistake the famous survivors for the whole phenomenon.

### (f) The most distinctive intervention — unmasking the inherited Victorian narrative

- Stern shows that the "joint-stock principle" continuity story — the idea that British empire was _always and continuously_ built by chartered companies — was **itself manufactured as imperial propaganda** by the promoters of the late-Victorian chartered companies (Cecil Rhodes's circle, George Goldie, Frederick Lugard, Flora Shaw, James Rochfort Maguire, Verney Lovett Cameron, A. V. Dicey, Alfred Lyall, the North Borneo and BSAC publicists).
- He singles out **George Cawston & A. H. Keane, _Early Chartered Companies_ (1896)** — written to "awaken public interest" in chartered companies and legitimize the BSAC (whose charter it reprints, without comment, as the book's final "specimen") — and notes it "remains a ubiquitous if rarely interrogated resource for historians of early companies today." Likewise **Cecil Carr's** _Select Charters of Trading Companies_ (and Maitland's Selden Society project) reproduced the same "the modern grant follows the old" conclusion.
- **The payoff:** modern historians who narrate a smooth lineage from the Muscovy Company to the EIC to the BSAC are often _unknowingly recycling self-serving imperial mythology._ Stern wants the continuity to be examined critically rather than assumed — empire "advanced around the globe more as a cacophony than a concert."

### (g) Local/empirical engagements (mostly approving, used as building blocks)

Cited as authorities rather than opponents: **Rupali Mishra** (charters required constant "negotiation and interpretation"); **Will Pettigrew** ("statutory deregulation" of the Royal African Company, 1698); **Karen Kupperman** (Providence Island and the Western Design); **Charles Andrews, Owen Stanwood, Mark Peterson, Phil Withington, Carl Wennerlind, Milton Ready, P. J. Marshall, Matthew Birchall**; and for the modern coda **Mircea Raianu** (Tata), **Vanessa Ogle** ("private investors into virtual suzerains" / tax havens), and **Raymond Craib** ("adventure capitalism").

## 4. Chapter-by-chapter notes

The six chapters are organized chronologically, each named with a finance pun and subtitled an "Age."

### Introduction — _Incorporating Empire_

Lays out the thesis above. Builds the conceptual toolkit (joint stock + corporation; the corporation's metaphysical "dilemmas buried in its DNA"; charters as "exemptions from Law" per Hobbes; commodified/portfolio sovereignty). Surveys critics across the spectrum who saw corporations as soulless monstrosities — Hobbes ("worms in the entrails"), critics of "bloodsuckers of the commonwealth," Smith, Marx ("a new variety of parasites"), the Frankenstein metaphor, and pluralist Harold Laski (corporations habitually "escape... the rigid bonds" and "rebel against the state" that created them). States the methodological commitments (process/genealogy/collective biography/chaos theory) and the historiographical positioning.

### Chapter 1 — _Initial Public Offerings: The Age of Discovery_ (16th century)

**Argument:** England's overseas corporation grew out of a **"franchise government"** in which offices were both public service and private property. Overseas charters took the medieval mechanisms of civic and commercial life (guild, livery company, confraternity, municipal corporation, seigneurial lordship, martial law) and projected them into spaces where the Crown had no real authority.

- **From Moscow to Munster:** Humphrey Gilbert as the archetypal Elizabethan projector (Le Havre → Ireland → Atlantic schemes). The **Muscovy/Russia Company** (1553–55) as "in many ways the first" English overseas joint-stock corporation — sought Cathay, found Russia; became the de facto government of Anglo-Russian trade and the gatekeeper of all northern/Atlantic exploration. Irish plantation (the Munster model) as the laboratory; plantation premised on the _expropriation of land_ via legal fictions (rebellion/forfeiture, fabricated deeds, "voluntary" surrender-and-regrant).
- **New Found Lands:** Frobisher and Michael Lok's **Company of Cathay** — the disastrous Northwest Passage / "fool's gold" ventures, which were never actually incorporated, leaving Lok personally liable; creative bookkeeping foreshadows later bubble dynamics. The ventures' real legacy was a corpus of promotional treatises, maps, and travel narratives.
- **Passages to the Indies:** The Levant Company (regulated vs. joint-stock models), the capture of the _Madre de Deus_ and its intelligence windfall, Hakluyt's legal brief (anticipating Grotius's _Mare Liberum_), and the chartering of the **English East India Company (1600).** Companies were "political chameleons" and "transnational institutions," shifting between sovereign and subject; rights abroad came through _farman_, _shuinjō_, treaty, grant. (Cites Mishra on charters as objects of constant negotiation.)

### Chapter 2 — _Municipal Bonds: The Age of Crisis_ (early–mid 17th century)

**Argument:** Companies were still "quite bad at" colonialism — underfunded, over-promoted, over-confident — but the joint-stock/corporate form gave them tools (debt, subsidiaries, mergers, acquisitions, divestments) and bred the strange idea that _"in buying a company, one could buy a colony"_ (the Fife Adventurers / Lewis).

- **Manie Purces of the Well-Publique:** The **Virginia Company** and the London/Irish Society (Ulster plantation) — corporations made of corporations (livery companies conglomerated into consortia). Pocahontas's London tour as a promotional act.
- **Much Suing for Patents:** The monopoly debates — _Darcy v. Allen_ (Case of Monopolies), the **Statute of Monopolies (1624)**, Sandys's "free trade" campaigns. The crucial distinction: a grant to _one_ person is a monopoly, but a grant to a _corporation_ ("many sellers") supposedly is not — which Bacon mocked as "ridiculous." Critics' worry: the "Monopoly in a Monopoly" run by insiders.
- **Costly and Tedious Business / The Corporate Commonwealth:** The Virginia Company's collapse (1624); the Civil War and Cromwellian Protectorate as a "mixed experience." Key claim: the revolutionary state — Commons and Cromwell alike — _continued and even intensified_ the entrepreneurial tradition (Western Design, Navigation Act 1651, Adventurers for Irish Land, the absorption of the Assada venture and the EIC's reorganization). Even radical regimes built empire by corporation. (Cites Kupperman, Andrews, Peterson.)

### Chapter 3 — _Corporate Finance: The Age of Projects_ (Restoration → 1720)

**Argument:** Defoe's "projecting age." Corporate colonialism proliferates (and so does the backlash). The Crown's growing bureaucracy (Councils/Lords of Trade, Edward Randolph) tries to assert control — but these bodies were themselves staffed and steered by company men and driven by petitioners, not by a coherent "imperial agenda."

- **The Corporate Restoration:** Royal Society as a "corporation"/"project for projects"; Robert Boyle as a node connecting the Royal Society, EIC, the New England Company, and the HBC. Connecticut and Rhode Island as corporations; the Atherton Company's land-grab as a "protection racket."
- **The Corporation is Dead, Long Live the Corporation:** Royal/courtly company-founding (James, Duke of York; Prince Rupert) — Royal Adventurers into Africa → **Royal African Company (1672)**, and the **founding of the Hudson's Bay Company (1670)** (see §5). Legal theories developed in the _quo warranto_ attack on the Somers Isles (Bermuda) Company that would let the Crown override charters. The Glorious Revolution shifts power to the Commons but produces _mixed_ results; the rival "old" vs. "new" East India Companies; the Company of Scotland / Darien disaster.
- **A Bundle of Arrows Knit Fast Together:** The argument that charters = property and therefore could not be withdrawn without compensation; the racial/spatial "this side of the line" logic (monopolies are illegitimate among "Christian" Europe but "absolutely necessary" for trade with "barbarous" peoples — Josiah Child, Molloy). Ends with the **South Sea Bubble (1720)** and the (largely toothless) **Bubble Act** — which "did little to arrest the enthusiasm" for joint-stock colonial projects.

### Chapter 4 — _Hostile Takeovers: The Age of Revolutions_ (18th century)

**Argument:** A century of "revolution and evolution." The British state increasingly builds empire by **merger and acquisition**, becoming, in John Sinclair's words, "a great corporation" itself. Yet old corporate forms persist and new ones (even an _abolitionist_ one) appear.

- **Georgia, Limited:** The Trustees of Georgia as a non-profit, philanthropic corporate colony reliant on state subvention.
- **Every State Is a Corporation: British America:** The American Revolution reframed as a crisis _about charters and corporate rights_ (colonies as corporations; _Dartmouth v. Woodward_ later crystallizes the charter-as-property doctrine).
- **Questions Equally Interesting and Uncertain: British India:** The EIC's transformation from trader to territorial sovereign after 1757; Parliament's new centrality; the vicennial charter reviews; **Fox's failed India Bill (1783)** and **Pitt's India Act (1784)** establishing the Board of Control and **"double government."** Regulation here _naturalized and sanitized_ the idea of a company (and then a British) empire in India — the question became not _whether_ but _how much_ to supervise. **Adam Smith's** critique (3rd edition of _Wealth of Nations_) anchors the chapter — including his **"tortured" exemption of the HBC** (see §5). Burke's counter ("the Counting-house gave lessons to the State").
- **Abolition, Inc.:** Corporate colonialism turned to _anti-slavery_ — the **Sierra Leone Company** (govern first, trade second; descended from the corporate-colony tradition but now Parliament-chartered and state-subsidized) and the **Bulama Island Association.** Shows the model's adaptability — the same corporate logic could promote _or_ abolish the slave trade. (The slave-trading African Company is dissolved 1752; the Royal African Company falls while the HBC survives.)

### Chapter 5 — _Corporate Innovations: The Age of Reform_ (early–mid 19th century)

**Argument:** A "Companies Raj." The EIC loses its commercial monopoly (India trade 1813, China trade 1833) and becomes a _purely governmental_ body — even as a new wave of registered/chartered colonization companies floods Australia, New Zealand, and the Canadian West.

- **The Companies Raj:** The 1813 charter renewal; advocates wield "experience over theory" against Smith; the "Territorial/Political" vs. "Commercial" account split (which paradoxically entrenched rather than dissolved the company-empire).
- **The Roaring '20s:** The 1820s company boom; the **HBC–North-West Company war and 1821 merger** (see §5).
- **A Pennsylvania on the South Coast of New Holland / Global Wakefield:** **Edward Gibbon Wakefield's "systematic colonization"** and its diffusion through joint-stock companies (South Australian Association/Company, Western Australia "Australind," New Zealand Company and its nested subsidiaries — Plymouth Company, Canterbury Association, etc.). Léopold I's company-shopping; the HBC's Pacific Northwest ventures (Puget's Sound Agricultural Company; Vancouver Island). **Closing point:** companies _and_ the new charitable/emigration societies agreed on one thing — colonial expansion "was no business for government."

### Chapter 6 — _Limiting Liabilities: The Age of Imperialism_ (late 19th century)

**Argument:** A "new" imperialism "that at times could look quite old indeed." General incorporation and **limited liability** (the Companies Acts) democratized company formation — and instead of _replacing_ chartered colonialism, registered companies worked _in tandem_ with charters. The era's chartered companies (North Borneo, Royal Niger, Imperial British East Africa, British South Africa) explicitly modeled themselves on the EIC and HBC and constructed the "joint-stock principle" continuity myth.

- **The Blowing Up in Leadenhall Street:** The EIC's abolition after the 1857 Rebellion (Government of India Act 1858) and the parallel pressure on the HBC (see §5).
- **The Reign of Registration:** Limited-liability company law; the figure of the "promoter" (Dickens's Tigg Montague) as the new "projector."
- **Empire (Chartered and Limited):** The Falkland Islands Company; James Brooke / Sarawak / the Eastern Archipelago Company; the hybrid "register-then-charter" strategy. Goldie's **Royal Niger Company "(Chartered and Limited)"** — the very name advertising its doubled, reinforcing sources of authority.
- **How to Make Money by Starting a New Empire:** Natal Land Company; Cecil Rhodes, the Rudd Concession, and the **British South Africa Company (1889)** — "to found a company that would become to South Africa what the East India Company had been to India."
- **Statesman, Economist, Philanthropist:** The promoters' self-conscious _use of history_ as argument (Lugard, Flora Shaw citing Seeley, Maguire, Cawston & Keane). This is where Stern most directly exposes the inherited continuity narrative as imperial propaganda.

### Epilogue — _Winding Up_ (20th century → present)

The slow, expensive **"winding up"** of the chartered companies, always snagged on the problem of **compensation** (how to value a body that was simultaneously a business and a government; the IBEAC, Royal Niger, BSAC, and North Borneo buyouts — each "somewhat analogous" to the HBC's 1869–70 surrender). The form mutated rather than died: into smaller concessionary companies (oil, plantations, Palestine, Manchuria, United Fruit) and then into a _new_ species — genuinely **public/state-owned chartered corporations** (BBC, BOAC, Cable & Wireless, Empire Cotton, UKCC, the Colonial/Commonwealth Development Corporation) serving a more integrated, singular "British" Empire. Decolonization made concessionary companies focal points of protest and nationalization (Suez 1956, Anglo-Iranian Oil, etc.). **Survivors:** the HBC (see §5), De Beers, the Australian Agricultural and Van Diemen's Land Companies, the revived "East India Company" retail brand, and the corporate DNA buried in Unilever, GSK, HSBC, Charter Consolidated, etc. **Closing image:** the 1982 Falklands War — where the century-old Falkland Islands Company had lobbied to keep the islands British — bore the British military codename **"Operation Corporate,"** which Stern calls "arguably the final battle in four centuries of British imperial expansion."

## 5. The role of the Hudson's Bay Company (the HBC thread)

The HBC is one of Stern's two flagship "company-states" (with the EIC) and recurs in every period after its founding. He uses it both as a major case study and as a _measuring stick_ for everything else.

**Founding and early character (Ch. 3).**

- Chartered in **1670** as the **"Governor and Company of Adventurers of England Trading into Hudson's Bay,"** out of the same Restoration royal/courtly company-founding nexus that produced the Royal Adventurers into Africa / Royal African Company. **Prince Rupert** was its patron; it grew from a proposal by two North American fur traders to revive the older Kirke–Alexander "Canada Adventurers."
- Empowered to make war, fortify, plant "Colonies or Plantations," and make "Compacts and Agreements with the Natives" across a vast tract — **Rupert's Land.** Stern stresses it had a brief "similar to the African and East India Companies": from the start a _military, diplomatic, and juridical_ enterprise as much as a fur trader, even though its profile in the 18th century rested mostly on trade.
- **Robert Boyle** (Royal Society / EIC / New England Company) was an early HBC "adventurer" — the HBC sits inside Stern's web of overlapping corporate networks.

**Resilience under attack (Ch. 3–4).** The HBC is Stern's recurring example of corporate _survival_:

- After the Glorious Revolution, when critics marshaled the state against monopolies, the HBC **"emerged relatively unscathed."** Its self-justification became a template: the dual threat of "Savage Indians" and "Potent Rivals the French" required arms and infrastructure that "could only be managed but by a Company in a Joynt Stock." It was explicitly a _model_ for proposed Indian-trade companies in Carolina and Virginia (e.g. Cadwallader Jones's "Company of Gentleman adventurers... on the model of the Hudson's Bay Company").
- During the **South Sea Bubble (1720)** the HBC opportunistically floated new stock issues.
- **Adam Smith's exemption (Ch. 4).** Though Smith savaged the EIC and Royal African Company, "by some tortured logic" he **carved out an exemption for the HBC**, arguing it operated on a small enough scale to function like a private "co-partnery" despite being a corporation. Stern uses this as evidence of the _inconsistency_ at the heart of Smith's anti-corporate doctrine.
- **The Dobbs assault (Ch. 4).** Arthur Dobbs (Irish landowner, Ohio Company promoter) led a Northwest Passage-driven campaign that produced a charter review, a 1749 Commons inquiry, and a £20,000 NW Passage prize. The HBC defended itself with the now-standard arguments — only an exclusive company can bear the costs of governance plus indigenous trade; a charter is _property_; revoking it would "ruin British Canada" and trigger an "outcry" from every other corporation, college, and church fearing for its own "properties." **The HBC survived; the Royal African Company did not (dissolved 1752).**

**Re-territorialization (Ch. 5).** This is the HBC's pivotal transformation, deliberately paralleled to the EIC:

- Competition from the Crown colonies of Lower/Upper Canada (1791) and especially the Montreal-based **North-West Company** (allied for a time with John Jacob Astor's American Fur Company).
- The **Red River / Assiniboia colony** — granted by the HBC as a proprietary concession to its largest shareholder, the **Earl of Selkirk** — became the flashpoint: an escalating commercial war culminated in a **bloody battle in 1816**. Commissioner W. B. Coltman charged the HBC with failing both as a government (permitting a private war) and as a company (letting one individual, Selkirk, dominate it) — an offense of "nonuser and misuser" that could have justified revoking the charter.
- Under threat of a _quo warranto_, the **HBC and North-West Company merged (1818–21)** via a share swap, "in a moment reminiscent of the union of the two warring East India Companies in 1709." Reinforced by an **Act of Parliament (1821)** and a 21-year license, the HBC's authority was _vastly expanded_ to the Pacific. Like the EIC, the HBC "emerged reinvigorated as a territorial government" after a period of resting on trade. It absorbed Red River under the Council of Assiniboia and (1839) leased the Alaskan coast from the Russian-American Company.
- **The Pacific Northwest / Wakefield:** under Governor **George Simpson** ("the little emperor"), the HBC pushed into Oregon (the **Puget's Sound Agricultural Company** subsidiary). After the **1846 Oregon Treaty** ceded the territory to the US, the HBC retreated north and secured a chartered subsidiary colony at **Vancouver Island** — promoted as proof "that colonization can be most effectually conducted by corporate bodies." Rival Wakefieldian James Edward Fitzgerald (like Dobbs and the North-West Company before him) attacked the HBC's _charter_; Gladstone questioned whether Charles II could ever have lawfully granted such powers. The HBC won but was criticized for under-investing and hoarding the best land. It "continued to hold out against the Wakefield 'system.'"

**Loss of government and surrender (Ch. 6).** Stern treats the HBC's "winding up" as the Canadian counterpart to the EIC's 1858 abolition:

- The Vancouver colony's failure and the violence/grievances at Red River (and the failed 1849 Sayer prosecution, which broke the trade monopoly in practice) put the charter's legitimacy "front and center." The HBC "narrowly escaped" a _quo warranto_ in 1850 only because the Colonial Office could find no willing complainant.
- In **1857–58** the western license lapsed; the HBC's government west of Rupert's Land was allowed to expire, the Vancouver patent was withdrawn (£57,500 compensation), and the region became the Crown colony of **British Columbia.**
- **A financial takeover (1862–63):** the railway magnate **Edward Watkin** / British North American Association called the HBC's bluff; the **International Financial Society** bought a controlling block of HBC shares (offering 3× par) — acquiring "vast amounts of Britain's Canadian empire" as a _financial_ play, with little interest in actually governing.
- **The surrender (1869–70):** the HBC ceded its governmental rights in **Rupert's Land to the Crown for £300,000**, with government vested in the new **Dominion of Canada (1867)**; it gave up Vancouver and its Alaskan leases (Alaska sold to the US, 1867). It remained a _commercial_ company retaining large property. Robert Brown's 1870 "eulogy" frames it as "the last of the old proprietary governments," whose "soul be long since fled" — "the world is too advanced for monopolies."

**As precedent and benchmark (Ch. 6 & Epilogue).** Crucially, the HBC did not simply vanish — it became an _argument_:

- George Goldie invoked "the Hudson's Bay Company's erstwhile power as a territorial government" as precedent for vesting government in the **Royal Niger Company**, _and_ used the way the HBC was dispossessed as a precedent for demanding **compensation.** Crown lawyers and the Foreign Office pushed back: the HBC's powers had themselves been "hotly contested" and eventually curbed by statute, and they distinguished the "older model" (HBC, Crown-derived) from the North Borneo Company (powers derived from local Sultans).
- Lugard called the late-Victorian African chartered companies "the modern adaptation of the instrument by which the **Hudson's Bay Company had added the Dominion of Canada**, and the East India Company had secured India." North Borneo and BSAC promoters routinely paired the HBC with the EIC as the twin proofs that "where mercantile associations... have succeeded... Governments have signally failed." The HBC's 1870 settlement became the explicit template ("somewhat analogous") for valuing the **BSAC** buyout.
- **The famous survivor (Epilogue).** Stern names the HBC "no doubt the most famous survivor" among commercial colonial companies — "now largely an American-owned retail, technology, and real estate conglomerate," still advertising itself as "North America's longest continually operating company." It is the living emblem of the book's thesis: the colonial corporation as a fragile-yet-immortal body that keeps being "reformulated and transformed."

**In short:** the HBC functions in _Empire, Incorporated_ as (1) one of the two paradigmatic company-states; (2) the recurring proof of corporate _resilience_ against state and rival attack; (3) the Canadian mirror of the EIC's arc (trade → territorial government → regulation → state buyout, retaining a commercial afterlife); and (4) a self-consciously deployed _historical precedent_ that later imperialists used to legitimate (and price) a new generation of chartered companies — exactly the kind of usable-past mythmaking Stern sets out to expose.

---

## 6. Big takeaways

1. **Empire was incorporated from the start.** Even the "official" British Empire was assembled by absorbing private corporate enterprises; public and private were never cleanly separable.
2. **The corporation's power lay in its contradictions** — its jurisdictional evasiveness, its capacity to be person and polity, property and sovereign, at once and across generations.
3. **Continuity was real but not inevitable** — and the _narrative_ of a seamless "joint-stock principle" was itself an imperial invention that historians have too often inherited uncritically.
4. **Don't read the present back onto the past by analogy** — connect them by genealogy, recognizing both the deep resemblances and the radical differences between a 17th-century charter and a 21st-century multinational.
5. **The corporate colony never really ended** — it mutated, into limited companies, public corporations, development corporations, tax havens, and surviving conglomerates whose roots are easy to forget.
