## Pre Reading Capitalism Thoughts:
My Views on Capitalism and its Origins

Written 2026-04-15 by Thomas McIntosh

I am about to embark on a reading of Sven Beckert’s _Capitalism: A Global History_. However, before I do, I feel I should write something on my own interpretation of the origins and meaning of capitalism. After, I intend to review this, and write a response with Beckert in mind.

For me, capitalism is an elusive, almost useless word. There is little agreed upon definition, and a struggle to determine its origins. The debate itself is so contentious that it is often easier to just walk away then again. Marxists argue capitalism is a mode of production entered into following enclosures in 1400-1600’s England, as landowners emphasized profits derived from efficient farming over older feudal obligations. From there, early capitalism(s) develop, culminating in the free market liberal capitalism of the 19th century, and the progressive capitalism of the 20th century. The problem is, I am unaware of any other non-Marxist arguments for the origins of capitalism. However, building on some research such as John Munroe of the U of T, my own research into Roman economics, and other topics I will offer my own interpretation.

At the core I believe capitalism is fundamentally defined as using markets to exchange goods and services, dependent on various conceptions of property rights, underpinned by a set of institutions which make trade viable. At the root of this trade is money, and money  itself is an extension of the concept of credit. Thus for me, the core of capitalism is debt/credit. When debts are not easily trackable and transferable, moved across a large distance, or lack the necessary social and financial institutions to handle them at scale, money is an effective intermediary. It stores value, thereby making it a place to sink credit, and is generally accepted because people know it stores value. Though credit predates money, the two are essential and interlinked.

As a result, I do not agree with Marxists that capitalism is a mode of production which was entered into. I see capitalism as the basic mechanism which underpins any sufficiently large society, and alterne natively which underpins international trade as well. Though violence is a part of this, violence is really just the last resort. Any framework which over essentializes violence as necessary to uphold the system, thereby implicitly postulating that there is some alternative framework not ultimately upheld by violence is not only anarchic but a failure. It only takes one bad actor to use violence to overrule the system. As a result, violence whether as a monopoly held by the state to enforce fair transactions among its subjects, or held by individuals used to ensure equality in a transaction, is a necessary underpinning of all arrangements. Over-essentializing violence as Marxists, progressives, and other leftists do is a necessary move to make violence and harm abstract and central to their analysis, but meaningless when it comes to actually understanding capitalism.

So far, I have argued that capitalism is rooted in credit systems, which become encoded in money. I have argued that capitalism is a natural development of exchange between people, underpinned by a concept of property rights, though those rights need not appear in the same form in every society. I have also emphasized that the threat of violence underpins not just capitalism, but all social orders as it is a necessary feature to ensure cooperation rather than pure self-satisfaction.

However, such a view does underplay many of the unique features of modern society. Capitalism as an ism may be most simply defined as a system of political, economic, and social thought which emphases a growing return of capital. It emphasies growing wealth through efficiency, market actions, and illegal actions. Thus, capitalism cannot exist without a state, and is structued by the institutions it is operating within. The state is a powerful vehicle for the growth of capitalism as an ism. In effect, the demand of the state for more resources, funding, weapons, ammo, uniforms, paper, and other things necessary to run a modern military drove capitalisms development much more than enclosures and factories.

Starting with the Roman Empire, as Kieth Hopkin’s argues in his influential article, taxes, the state, coinage, and capitalism all come together. In short, Hopkin’s argues that as the Roman Empire conquered previously uncontrolled territory, it raised the standard of living. This is because it imposed taxes which needed to be paid in Roman coinages or in kind. This forced farmers to be more efficient and adopt roman farming techniques if necessary to increase the amount of food produced such that an excess could be sold at market for the coinage needed to pay taxes, or given in kind. As a result, capitalism, the emphasis on raising capital can be seen here. Similarly, the role of the state and taxes in driving its creation becomes apparent. This can exist because the Roman state has the force to make people pay. It also has a widely accepted coinage system allowing money to be raised and paid. It has a state which accepts those taxes to feed the soldiers, possibly even paying for the food with coin which will be used to pay taxes, which as tax money will be used to pay a solider who will use it to buy a good from someone else in the region, thereby circulating the money through the region, and by extension shwoign the integration of the region into the roman monetary, credit, financial, and broader economic systems and structures. 

The middle ages can be seen as a gap moment when the credit and fincial insittutinos fo the Roman empire collapsed and new one had to be generated. Hence the effective death of currency for several centuries as the economy entered a functional recessary due to instititonal collapse, out of which new instititiosn were growing. Early on, warfare was fairly easy. A smith was needed to produce weapons, which required some farms and resource extraction. This could be kept fairly local, thereby allowing local credit systems to reign supreme. Over areas of greater control and distrubtion, other credit systems such as the Medici’s banking infrastructure, the medieval pawn shops, or the growth of various rents developed to supply the credit resources necessary to fund larger wars. Larger wars demanded more troops who need more supplies which takes more money and credit, which requires more effiencietn institutions. As well, a professional army was superior to peasants or mercs, this required the state to pay for training, which required taxation capacity and some degree of standardization, requiring record keeping. This necessitates a larger state beacurqacy. All of which has to be funded  via the wonderful inveition of state debts from central banks!

As fincial and credit markets expand to meet the needs of the state to fight its wars, interest rates come to dominate. If your interest is too high for a state to repay, then the war may be won but defeat found in victory. The growing scale and demand for more complex parts pushes the economoy to innovate in search of profits, thus driving economic development. The state, by being a major vehicle of demand, drives the growth of the economy but is not alone. Around the early 1800s the size and scale of the economy had grown beyond the confines of the state and effectively became self sufficient.

As a result, I see the origins of capitalism not in lords enclosing their fields to make a profit, but in the state expanding its bureaucracy to win wars, by issuing debt and creating chartered companies. I look forward to seeing how Beckert challenges this.

## My reading review of Capitalism:
Sven Beckert’s _Capitalism: A Global History_

Review by Thomas McIntosh, Written 2026-05-10.

Beckert’s _Capitalism_ is a massive work, covering almost 1000 years of global history, in just over 1000 pages. These are perhaps the three most important facts of the book. It is not about the institutions which make capitalism work, nor about the states relationship to capitalism. Rather it is about the global growth of capitalism, from little islands of capital in which a tiny merchant classes trades for their own profit but does not engage in production, to a globe spanning system of profit seeking production, in the span of about a thousand years. Do not expect to get a deep dive into political, economic, military, social, or institutional history. The frame is global, and everything happens on a global scale, with vignettes of particular places or people for examination. Some characters are traced through several chapters, such as major steel works in Germany through the 1800s to the present, or the growth of the Swedish welfare state, others rise and fall, only to come roaring back, in some ways matching their historical development, such as China or India. Similarly, like his _Empire of Cotton_ there are large swaths of the book devoted to commodity gathering, production, and the political economy in which they exist, whether cotton, slaves, silver, or modern industrial goods, the global development of these are often explored.

For Beckert capitalism is defined by four core things: A cesaseless drive for more capital, a commodification of all inputs and outputs, a constant redrawing of the boundaries of what is and isn’t ‘within’ capitalism, and that the state played a central role in this process. Thus it’s a global process of ceaseless accumulation which constantly redraws boundaries of what is and isn’t within its purview to make available as inputs and outputs newly commodified things. As a result, capitalism is more than an economic system, it is an almost agentic force flowly over the world via capital, remarking the world in its revolutionary image to its liking and flavour, never static, constantly reforging and remaking itself and the world.

However, Beckert says that while markets and trading for personal profit has always existed, capitalism has not. Capitalism is for Beckert more than just property rights and free markets. Beckert says those things have always existed. It is the process whereby ceaseless accumulation comes to be the core driving purpose of society which defines capitalism, and with that, the rule of society by a class of people who hold the most capital, and who have remade the rest of society as those who need their capital, so they can exploit and use them.

That is where I start to find annoyance with Beckert. There is a near constant refrain about the way capitalism remakes global hierarchies, how it uses oppression and dislocation to advance its ends, how wealth is not generated it is extracted and stolen, that I find damaging to the book. The tacit implication is that the global hierarchy prior to capitalism’s takeoff was more just, more proper, more legitimate. That before capitalism there was a more positive or a better state. American and European slavery gets extensive condemnation, and yet little if any real space is given to extra-european slave trades. No condemntation is truly given, and only a passing level of outrage, at the vast majority of slavery. There is extensive discussion of the triangle trade driving the growth of European capital and economic advancement – that material wealth was extracted from America with human wealth stolen from Africa, thereby not generating new productive wealth for europeans simply moving it around the globe – but there is very little in this global history about the Arab slave trade, or how African kings were gathering the slaves for sale. These limitations hurt the work. They are not wholly absent, but their limited size and scope, which may well be a reflection of existing literature, still is an omission. Similarly, the treatment of North America, especially regarding Indigenous people, is poor. The trade routes of the Aztecs, Inca’s, and Mayan’s are explored for a handful of pages, however little if anything is said on the Hudson’s Bay Company connecting Indigenous people of North America into the global trade system, or how the American continent even prior to European arrival, had continent spanning trade networks, moving goods from central America to the plains and further north. These ommissions matter in a work which claims to be consciously avoiding Eurocentricity.

One could similarly point to the absence of pre-1400 trade. Where is the mention of the Roman Empire, which traded with China for generations, until its collapse? If silver from Ameirca was essential for unlocking Europe, should there not be mention of the Middle Ages when Europe saw a massive decline in coin usage, and mining in general? For a global history of capitalism, the work subtly continues to perpetuate a Marxist framework even while claiming ot be more rigorously engaged with alternatives beyond that. Capitalism starts in its special moment, and while he challenges marx that enclosures were not the driver of capitalism, the state and its push for warfare was, overall the framework is still Marxist as its core. This is not to say Beckert does not treat enclosures, indeed there is a whole chapter on the topic. Rather it is to say that Beckert’s treatment is limited and not given the centrality as the motivating force which other historians in the Marxist lineage claim. The Marxism presented is vulgar Marxism, but it is clearly the intellectual tradition the author is operating in. Schumpeter is given work but is not essential, and neither is Hayek nor Douglass North. The core authors regularly quoted approving are Marxists and the argument broadly is in that tradition. The new ruling class displaced the old through class warfare, challenging both elites and masses in reforging law and society to meet their desired ends. The current ruling class is set to fall one day as the contradictions of capitlaims work themselves out, but also, capitalism is ever changing and will reforge itself. Because for beckert capitalism is not just an economic system which logically connects with and thus effects  political, social, cultural, religious, legal, and other systems, it is actually something which goes deeper and is all of those things. The reason I phrase it this way is that Beckert gives little agency to things like political ideas, culture, religion, etc.. Sure, Marxism gets its treatment, but really only as an aspect of capitalism. The real horrors of the Marxist worldview are left aside. Unlike say, _A Brief Global History of the Left_, clearly written by someone on the left, this is a work from someone who I don’t think likes capitalism very much weakly pretending to give you both sides of the story.

Now turning to that story, Beckert argues that though these proto-capitalist seeds existed before America was intregrated into the global trade system, capitalism only really took off once America was introduced. This was because American silver gave Europe something which the rich east was willing to trade its luxuries for. As a result, trade began to develop between eastern producers of silk goods which were traded for slaves in Africa to mine silver in America which was then shipped to the east to be traded for silk. For Beckert this is war capitalism, a fomr of capitalism whereby the states need to make war demands more resources. This demand is put on merchants to supply it. Those merhcants do not scale production to be more efficient, they make profit off of scale, thus not producing new value but extracting surplus and capturing it for themselves. The states demands push capitalists to new extremes but they do this by building out large scale small artisian producers. SO lots, but each ony doing a small amount.

The transition to real, industrial, capitalism comes with the invention of the spinning jenny allowing cotton producers to spin loads more cotton. This increase in production allows a single worker to do more than many workers used to, increasing efficiency. Now capitalist move from just investing in new ways to move things to new ways to produce things, as they realize that if they produce more with less resources and labour, they earn more profit. This begins to drive industrlization, which in tunrs pushes transofmations in Capitalims hinterlands as resources need to be used. More processing demands coffee which means slaves need to gather it and it needs to be shipped here. To prepare it there needs to be sugar which takes coal and specialzeid machinery, that coal and machinery need resources mind, those resources need engines and railways to be moved on. That takes coal which pushes people to mine it and use it more efficncnely, thereby replacing human power with automated power. However, dislocated and disrupted laboru revolts against oprssive masters. The French revolution drives slaves in Haiti to revolt their own rights, hurting sugar production forcing it to move to new places, the global death fo slavery forces capitalism ot invent debt bondage and new fomrs of debt slavery, and use the state to force slaves to work while they resist. Elsewhere labour unions are locked into the factory, suffer a worse diet, horrid living conditions, and awful lives but band together in unions to improve things. They create clubs and vacations spots, education centers and political parties which gain access to power and start reforging the labour relations to be better.

At the same time growing effiency means more is being produced causing profits for each industry to shrink. Cartels form ot set prices and begin working with the state to promise resources in exchange for state power crushing competition. Free markets turn into corporatist societies with dense planning. Communism bursts in revolution like Haitian slaves scarying capital like they scared slave owners, driving repression and change. At the same to unite people the nation is invented and put forward and people find it easier to rally around than class. In the battle for labour, land, and resources companies begin offering better perks to workers while countries begin enclosing land and markets within their empires, forbidden them to others. This creates the fraturous enviroment for ww2, where these big blocs battle it out. In the end Britain is crippled and America begins battling the Soviet Union for hegemonic status. Beckert is not concerned with communism so does not give it much treatment, except ot note that it forced the United States to act more moral in its capitalist system.

For the third quarter of the 20th century capitalism developed a large welfare state, wealth inequality declined, masses were lifted from poverty into the middle class. Then in 1973 things changed, Bretton woods ended as wealth from oil and other areas disrupted the stability the gold standard had brought. Once off gold, free floating currencies wreaked havoc. Rich oil countries funded IMF loans for energy needing producers who had to pay higher oil prices following OPEC prices increases following the Israel war in those years. Higher energy costs combined with inflation created stagflation, a supposed impossibility under Keynesianism. This brought in the neoliberal reforms as those debts had to be paid, poor countries cut their governments and tried to stabilize their currencies, given Chile as an example but also Mexico. Even Sweden had to reign in its welfare state. The result saw inequality increase, but so too did economic prosperty. The welfare state was shrunk but not killed. Instead the crisis is now the slowly lurking and growing climate crisis of fossil fuel use.

The book ends with some concerns that capitalism probably won’t die. That it will hopefully reform to move away from its current harmful energy sources, and that possibly the future post-capitalist sociteis are forming, that a new middle class and new working class are emerging which will bring in a new society replacing the current capitalist one, or which may simply be a new evolution of capitalism.

Compared to my own preferences the book has a few weakness I would have liked refined. Some

On the whole, Beckert has written a wonderful history of capitalism usefully situating the importance of the global and commodity focuses within the broader history and alternative thesis for the origin, from Adam Smith, to the military revolution, to Marxist argues, and many others. An informed reader will see them and their responses given here. A new reader will be given a strong first interpretation without it being dogmatic.

### Chapter 1

#### Introduction: The Origins and Logic of Capitalism
*   **A Gradual Process:** Capitalism did not start at one specific time or place (e.g., Florence or Amsterdam). It is a process that evolved like rivulets merging into a powerful stream, drawing energy from various sources over time. 
*   **The Logic of Capital:** The core logic of capitalism centers not just on markets, but on the *growth of capital*—using money and goods specifically to generate more money/capital.
*   **The First Capitalists:** Long-distance merchants were the first to personify this economic logic. A prime starting point to observe this is the 12th-century port city of **Aden** (in modern-day Yemen). 

#### The Port of Aden: An Early "Island of Capital"
*   **Global Hub:** 900 years ago, Aden was a fortified commercial hub linking Africa, Asia, Arabia, and Europe. Merchants here bought low, sold high, provided shipping/credit, and sometimes financed production. 
*   **The Arab-India Trade:** Aden’s primary fame was linking the Arab world to India. Travelers like Ibn Battuta (1330) noted the massive wealth of individual merchants who owned entire ships and vast capital. 
*   **The Rhythm of the Wind:** Trade was dictated by monsoon winds. Ships arrived from the Red Sea/Egypt, and others left for India or the Swahili coast depending on the season. 
*   **Port Logistics:** Aden functioned like a well-oiled machine. Ships were met by messengers (*mubashshirun*), cargo manifests were checked, and goods were taken to customs for inspection and duty assessment. 
*   **Commercial Infrastructure:** The city was built for trade, featuring the *Dār al-Saʾāda* (wholesale market), customs houses, multistoried merchant homes, and markets for daily commodities.
*   **Diverse Merchant Class:** Historical records reveal a multi-faith, multi-ethnic community (Muslim, Jewish, Hindu). Notable figures include:
    *   *Rāmisht:* A wealthy Persian shipowner.
    *   *Madmun ben Hasan ben Bundar:* Port superintendent, merchant representative, and head of the Jewish community. 
*   **Trade Operations:** Merchants traded horses, madder, sugar, gold, pepper, and textiles. Some formed partnerships, wrote letters monitoring supply/demand, and even invested in the local production of ships and glass. Arabic served as the *lingua franca*.
*   **Institutions and the State:** Trade was supported by institutional networks. 
    *   *Informal:* Kinship and religious identities.
    *   *Formal:* Representatives (*wakīl al-tujjār*) resolved conflicts. 
    *   *The State:* Local rulers provided security, enforced contracts, and collected modest customs duties (around 20%, payable upon departure to encourage trade).
*   **Anachronistic Modernity:** Though trade was slow (a round trip to India took two years) and carried on small dhows, the logic of their economic lives—investing, monitoring markets, seeking profit—mirrors modern capitalism.

#### The Rise of the Sedentary Merchant and the "Capitalist Archipelago"
*   **A New Kind of Trader:** In the early second millennium, "sedentary merchants" emerged. Instead of traveling with goods, they stayed put and traded at a distance using capital, credit, and agents.
*   **Nimble Wealth:** Unlike landed elites who extracted wealth via violence or taxation, these merchants accumulated wealth through flexible, fungible capital.
*   **The Rogue AI of Capital:** These merchants introduced a strange new principle: deploying capital to make more capital. Eventually, this human-created relationship took on a life of its own, forcing more people to act on its logic. 
*   **The Capitalist Archipelago:** Capitalism was "born global." It originated in isolated nodes (islands of capital) that were intrinsically linked to other distant nodes, forming a global archipelago of trade. However, at this time, their impact on the everyday subsistence farmer remained minimal.

#### The Global Awakening (Circa 1000 CE)
*   **Why 1000 CE?:** The turn of the millennium saw intensified trade, increased agricultural output (due to new crops like sugar cane and better irrigation), climate warming, and the consolidation of Islam, all of which accelerated long-distance trade. 
*   **Precedents:** Trade existed before 1000 CE (e.g., 9th-century Arab-Russian trade, 8th-century Muslim presence in the Indian Ocean), but the 11th century marked a structural shift.

#### The Islamic World: The Heart of the Global Economy
*   **The Center of Trade:** For centuries, the Islamic world was the center of the global economy. 
*   **Reasons for Dominance:** 
    *   *Urban Bias:* Islamic elites lived in cities (unlike European feudal lords).
    *   *Unification:* A massive zone sharing a religion, language, legal framework, and reliable gold/silver currency (dinars/dirhams). 
*   **Three Major Trade Routes:**
    *   **The Middle Route:** Via the Persian Gulf, Baghdad, Basra, to Mediterranean cities like Aleppo.
    *   **The Northern Route (Silk Road):** Arose as Mongols secured the Central Asian steppes. Cities like **Bukhara** and **Samarkand** thrived as massive trade, credit, and manufacturing hubs connecting China to Europe.
    *   **The Southern Route:** Via the Red Sea, centered on Cairo and Aden, reaching out to India.
*   **The Rise of Cairo:** As Baghdad declined, Cairo (Fustat) boomed under the Fatimids. By 1325, it was the world's second-largest city. 
    *   *Ibn ʿAwkal:* A prominent 11th-century Cairo merchant dealing in 83 commodities, famously exporting 54 tons of flax in a single year.
    *   *Investment in Production:* Cairo merchants heavily financed sugar refineries, textile mills, and ceramics.
    *   *Economic Thought:* This vibrant economy inspired Ibn Khaldun (14th century) to develop early theories on economic growth and the labor theory of value.

#### South Asia (India): The Wealth of *al-Hind*
*   **Hub of the Indian Ocean:** Indian merchants traded spices, textiles, and Chinese goods westward to Arabia/Africa and eastward to Malacca. 
*   **Indian Textiles:** The most important export was high-quality cotton cloth.
*   **Major Ports:** Cities like Bharuch, Calicut, and especially **Cambay** became massively wealthy, multi-ethnic port cities.

### **Part 1: The Global Archipelago of Capital**
*   **The Emergence of Global Networks:** In the first half of the second millennium, diverse merchants (e.g., Shens, Bardis, Madmuns) forged long-distance connections linking distant producers and consumers across Asia, Africa, Europe, and the Americas. Merchants operated on a fundamentally different logic from the rest of the world: buying cheap and selling dear, investing capital simply to accumulate *more* capital.
*   **A Distinct Social Class:** Across vast geographical and cultural divides (from Guangzhou to Genoa), merchants became a recognizable, distinct social group. Unlike rural cultivators (who produced) or rulers (who plundered/taxed), merchants relied strictly on capital accumulation, leading to immense wealth that allowed them to patronize the arts and influence rulers.

### **Part 2: The Infrastructure of Trust and Self-Organization**
*   **The Necessity of Trust:** Long-distance trade was incredibly risky due to distance, pirates, and the inability to supervise agents. Because centralized legal enforcement was lacking, merchants relied on self-organized institutions and personal reputation (*xinyong* in China, *nanayam* in India) to survive. 
*   **Family as the Bedrock of Business:** Kinship networks were crucial for establishing trust. The Cairo Geniza documents show Jewish traders relying on family members to extend credit and share risk. Merchant women played vital roles in managing social ties, arranging marriages, and running the business when men were away.
*   **The Evolution of Family Firms:** In Europe (Amalfi, Venice, Genoa, Florence), trade was heavily delegated to sons, brothers, and in-laws. Over time, this reliance on kinship gave rise to the massive, elite Renaissance family firms. 
*   **Alternative Kinship:** When biological family wasn't enough, merchants used marriage, godparenthood (*compadrazgo* in Portugal), and adoption (in China) to expand their trusted networks.

### **Part 3: Guilds, Diasporas, and Social Identity**
*   **Beyond Family—Shared Origins and Religion:** Merchants formed alliances based on hometowns, shared religions, or castes. Muslim merchants built specific urban quarters across Asia with access to mosques and Arabic-speaking courts.
*   **Guilds as Proto-Corporations:** Guilds allowed merchants to regulate trade, lower transaction costs, and exert collective political power. Examples include the Hanseatic League in Northern Europe, the *Cinque Savi* in Venice, Italian *arti* (like the wool guilds), and Tamil merchant guilds.
*   **Extraterritorial Enclaves:** When trading abroad, merchants set up localized communities (e.g., Venetian *fondacos* or Hanseatic quarters in London) that functioned with their own judicial systems, chapels, and warehouses.
*   **Fashion and Social Tension:** Merchants signaled their cosmopolitan wealth through imported luxury clothing. This provoked resentment from traditional elites, resulting in *sumptuary laws* (e.g., in Italy and China) designed to restrict luxury dress to the nobility and keep merchants in their subordinate social place. 

### **Part 4: Institutional Innovations of Early Capitalists**
*   **Solving the Problems of Distance:** To avoid the danger of traveling with large amounts of cash, global merchants invented **bills of exchange/credit** (e.g., *hundis* in South Asia, *feitsyan* in China, *hawala* in the Islamic world, and *lettere di cambio* in Italy). These allowed for cashless transfers across vast distances.
*   **Mitigating Disaster through Insurance:** To protect against sunken ships or pirates, merchants collectivized risk. Islamic law created early liability transfers (*daman*). Dedicated maritime insurance emerged in Genoa by 1347 and was utilized widely by Indian (*jokhmi hundis*) and Ottoman merchants.
*   **The Invention of Accounting:** As trades grew longer and more complex, keeping track of capital was essential. 11th-century Jewish and Muslim merchants used sophisticated chronological ledgers. This evolved into Italian **double-entry bookkeeping** (formalized by Luca Pacioli in 1494), which allowed merchants to accurately calculate the profitability of individual ventures.
*   **Pooling Capital (Partnerships):** To mobilize larger sums of money, Muslims developed the *mudaraba* (an early separation of investors and traveling agents). Europeans adapted this into the *commenda*, allowing multiple people—even commoners of modest means—to buy fractional shares of a voyage, limiting personal liability.
*   **Early Holding Companies and Banks:** This pooling of capital evolved into the *compagnia* and medieval holding companies (pioneered by Francesco Datini). Early banking emerged from pawnshops and merchant credit. A prime example of future capitalism was the Genoese *Casa di San Giorgio* (1407), a creditor body that eventually took over state tax collection and governed overseas colonies.

### **Part 5: The Contrast—The Non-Capitalist World**
*   **An Agrarian World:** Merchants were a tiny minority. Around 1300–1500, over 90% of people in China, Europe, and the Middle East lived in the countryside.
*   **Production for Use (*Oikonomia*):** Most of humanity did not produce for the market. They lived in "use-oriented societies," farming and crafting strictly for their own household subsistence. Aristotle praised this as natural, while condemning market commerce as "unnatural."
*   **Tributary Systems (How Elites Got Rich):** Traditional elites (lords, emperors) did not accumulate wealth through market investment. They extracted wealth through *extra-economic* means: taxes, tribute, plunder, and forced peasant labor (feudalism). Wealth extraction was visible and legally mandated, not hidden in market transactions.
*   **Regulated Manufacturing:** Urban manufacturing was strictly controlled by artisan guilds that aimed to limit competition, set prices, and restrict investment, completely antithetical to free-market capitalism.

### **Part 6: State Relations and the Limits of Capital**
*   **Ambivalent Rulers:** Rulers depended primarily on taxing agriculture, leaving merchants politically subordinate. However, rulers sometimes supported merchants because they supplied luxury goods, loans, and taxable revenue (e.g., China's Song dynasty heavily relied on trade taxes). 
*   **Slow Economic Growth:** The world economy grew at a microscopic rate of roughly 0.05% annually from 1000 to 1500. Without mass growth, the expansion of capital was heavily constrained.
*   **Ecological and Social Walls:** The economy relied on human/animal muscle, severely limiting surplus production. Furthermore, peasants fiercely guarded their land and labor, making it impossible for merchants to take over the actual *production* of goods.
*   **Market Regulations and Elite Pushback:** The markets that did exist were heavily regulated by moral laws (e.g., Islamic *muhtasib* market inspectors). Furthermore, traditional elites pushed back against merchant power to protect their own tributary systems (e.g., China restricting overseas trade, Japan forgiving samurai debt to merchants).
*   **Class Rebellions:** When merchant capital tried to dominate the working class, it sparked massive rebellions, most notably the *Ciompi Revolt* (1378) in Florence, where lower-tier wool workers temporarily seized the city government.

### **Part 7: Ideological and Religious Hostility**
*   **Universal Condemnation:** All major world religions in the first half of the millennium viewed the logic of capital (making money from money) with deep suspicion.
*   **Islam:** The Quran strictly forbade *riba* (usury/interest). Philosophers like al-Ghazali accepted trade as a social duty but condemned the accumulation of wealth for its own sake.
*   **Confucianism:** Placed merchants at the absolute bottom of the social hierarchy (below peasants and artisans), viewing profit-seeking as parasitic to the agrarian ideal.
*   **Christianity:** Viewed usury as a sin (essentially "selling time," which belongs only to God). Merchants were heavily associated with greed. Wealthy merchants, like Cosimo de' Medici, lived in immense fear of damnation and spent fortunes on church building to atone for the "sin" of their banking practices.

### **Part 8: Conclusion—Capitalists, but not Capitalism**
*   **Capital Confined to the Margins:** Despite incredible innovations, capitalists existed, but a global system of *capitalism* did not. Merchants dominated long-distance trade but failed to alter local patterns of production and consumption. 
*   **The Need for an Ally:** The dominant system of the world remained the tributary logic (lords coercing peasants). Capitalists were hemmed in by ecological, social, and religious walls. To break out of their marginalized "islands" and forge modern capitalism, they would eventually require the backing of a vastly more powerful ally: **the State**.

*   **Indigenous Resistance:** The Pueblo Revolt (1680) in New Mexico temporarily expelled the Spanish. The Mapuche in Chile successfully fought the Spanish for centuries.
*   **Indentured Servants:** Bacon’s Rebellion (1676) in Virginia featured poor whites rising up. (This resulted in elites hardening racial lines to separate poor whites from enslaved Black workers).
*   **Enslaved People:** Constant threat of rebellion (Barbados had panics/revolts in 1633, 1640s, 1675, 1683, etc.). Revolts were met with unspeakable, sadistic torture (burning alive, gibbeting) to maintain control through terror. 

## The Unpaid Subsidies of Capitalism (Ecology & Reproduction)
*   **Free Ecological Inputs:** Capitalism relied on the earth's natural energy—cutting down ancient forests, exhausting soil, and utilizing Indigenous agricultural knowledge (e.g., Amerindian tobacco cultivation, Inca silver mining techniques).
*   **Social Reproduction:** Capitalism survived by relying on non-market labor. Enslaved workers were produced by African societies outside the market. Wage workers relied on non-market domestic labor (child-rearing, cooking). 
*   **Gendered Division of Labor:** This created a strict boundary where men’s market production was deemed "work," while women’s social reproduction and domestic labor was categorized as "non-work."

## Conclusion: The Capitalist Archipelago by 1750
*   By 1750, a vast array of commodities (sugar, silver, wheat, cotton) flowed from the global countryside into cities, produced not for subsistence, but for distant markets.
*   Europe sat atop this hierarchy, uniquely utilizing state power and massive violence (War Capitalism) to integrate the slave economies of the Americas, the wage economies of the West, and the serf economies of the East.
*   This massive transformation of the countryside generated the wealth and systems required for the next phase of capitalism: industrial manufacturing.

# Chapter 7: Intensifying Industry, 1600–1750

## Introduction: The Case of Jelenia Góra (Hirschberg)
*   **A Global Hub in the Countryside:** Jelenia Góra (a small city in modern Poland, known then as Hirschberg) serves as a prime example of early global manufacturing intensification. Three hundred years ago, impoverished weavers from surrounding mountains brought linen cloth to merchants in the town square. 
*   **The Atlantic Connection:** Merchants bought this linen, had it bleached, and shipped it to ports like Hamburg and Amsterdam. From there, it was sent to the Americas (Rio de Janeiro, Havana, Charleston) specifically to clothe enslaved workers on sugar, rice, and tobacco plantations.
*   **The Human Toll:** By 1750, local peasants operated over 6,000 looms. They were desperately poor; many worked the land by day and wove by night. Children as young as four spun flax. Most workers were unfree dependents of local nobles.
*   **Internal Colonialization:** Local merchants (e.g., Mentzel, Gottfried, von Buchs) commercialized the countryside. The author refers to this as "intensive internal colonialization." Coerced labor in the Americas directly deepened coerced labor (serfdom) in the European countryside.
*   **Evolution of the Trade:** Initially, Dutch and English merchants arrived in the 1500s seeking cheap linen to trade in West Africa for enslaved people. After the Thirty Years' War (1648), local Hirschberg merchants took over the trade directly. They formed the *Kaufmanns-Societät* in 1658 to regulate quality and create a trademark, making linen Prussia's most important industry by 1740.
*   **The "Second Serfdom":** To meet booming Atlantic demand, landowning nobles expanded *corvée* duties (unpaid, forced labor) up to three days a week, demanding rent in cloth or money. 
*   **Wealth Disparity:** This system created fabulous wealth for merchants and nobles (who built lavish homes with mahogany and damask) and utter misery for the producers. Writers like Gerhart Hauptmann and observers like John Quincy Adams explicitly noted this stark contrast between elite wealth and peasant starvation.

## The Shift to Urban Manufacturing
*   **Capital Meets Manufacturing:** By 1700, the capitalism of Caribbean slavery merged with central European serfdom. Merchants began moving their capital from trade into manufacturing, catering to long-distance markets. 
*   **Urban Origins:** This intensification began in cities and towns where manufacturing was traditionally rooted. 
    *   *Florence:* Woolen workshops expanded and began subcontracting spinning to rural women.
    *   *Danzig:* Booming wheat exports created local wealth, driving demand for manufactured goods. Guild masters hired more workers, turning apprentices into permanent wage workers (proletarians). By 1640, 6,000–7,000 people worked in Danzig textiles.
    *   *Ottoman Empire:* Cities like Cairo, Aleppo, and Bursa saw the rise of "artisan capitalism." Workshops grew larger (some with up to 60 looms) and drew skilled labor from surrounding regions.
    *   *China:* In Jingdezhen, 200–300 kilns employed up to 100,000 wage workers producing porcelain for imperial and global markets. "China" became a universal status symbol for global elites.
    *   *Italy:* Genoa expanded into silk, velvet, iron, and paper; Venice expanded its famous Murano glass industry.

## The Role and Limits of Guilds
*   **Guild Control:** Guilds mediated this early urban expansion. They existed globally (Paris, Cairo, Damascus) and strictly regulated production: setting prices, standardizing quality, and fixing the division of labor.
*   **Pushing Capital Outward:** However, guilds ultimately constricted capitalist expansion. They restricted labor supply, limited price competition, blocked technical innovation, and drove up labor costs (e.g., labor in Amiens was 50–73% more expensive than in the countryside). This constriction, along with urban labor strikes, pushed merchant capital out of the cities and into the countryside.

## Proto-Industrialization (Rural Manufacturing)
*   **Moving to the Countryside:** Seeking cheap, non-guild labor, merchants turned to the countryside. Rural peasants eagerly accepted this work to supplement their farming income.
*   **The "Uber" of the 17th Century:** This phase is called *proto-industrialization*. Merchants advanced raw materials or credit to rural workers, who worked in their own homes, at their own pace, using their own tools. They sold their "slack" labor time (winters, evenings) to merchants.
*   **Global Leaders in Proto-Industry:**
    *   *India:* The absolute global center. The largest proto-industrial sector in the world was Indian cotton textiles. Quality and quantity were unmatched globally. It absorbed one-fifth of the world's silver just to pay for cottons.
    *   *China:* Massive investments in the Yangtze and Pearl River Deltas. Millions of rural workers produced vast quantities of cotton and silk for domestic and export markets.
    *   *Japan:* Developed an import-substitution model to replace Chinese goods. Rural areas produced silk, porcelain, paper, and fish-meal fertilizer, financed by urban merchants.
    *   *Ottoman Empire:* Merchants funded rural women weaving mohair in Ankara, knotted carpets in Uşak, and cotton in the Balkans and Egypt.
    *   *Europe:* Catalan clothiers, English wool/cotton merchants, and Black Forest merchants all sent capital into the countryside to fund rural spinning and weaving.
    *   *Africa:* Eastern Kongo produced massive amounts of cloth (equaling European centers like Leiden). Kano (Nigeria) featured wealthy merchants investing heavily in rural cotton, leather, and iron networks.

## The Mechanics of Rural Manufacturing
*   **Business Models:** Systems varied. In India and China, weavers usually bought their own raw materials and sold the finished product to the highest bidder. In England, the "putting-out" system was common: merchants owned the raw materials and simply paid the peasant for the labor of processing it.
*   **The Merchant's Role:** Regardless of the system, merchants were vital. They provided circulating capital, raw materials, market information, transportation, and global marketing.
*   **Link to Agriculture:** Crucially, proto-industrial workers almost always continued to farm. Their low manufacturing pay was only sustainable because they grew their own food. Capital essentially piggybacked on the traditional family farm unit for its labor supply.
*   **The "Industrious Revolution":** Technological innovation was slow (though tools like the flying shuttle did emerge). Because labor was abundant, merchants didn't focus on labor-saving tech; they focused on quality and variety. Output grew simply because *more people were working harder and longer*.

## Concentrated Manufacturing (Proto-Factories)
*   **Gathering Workers Under One Roof:** Alongside rural proto-industry, merchants and states began investing in concentrated manufacturing sites: mines, breweries, sugar refineries, and arsenals. Here, workers were directly supervised by capital owners.
*   **The State as Industrial Driver:** States drove much of this to serve military and luxury needs. 
    *   *France:* The Lorient naval arsenal grew to support 14,000 residents; the Gobelins factory produced luxury tapestries for the elite.
    *   *Venice:* The Venetian arsenal employed over 2,300 proletarian workers.
    *   *China:* Massive state and private shipyards (like Longjiang in Nanjing) employed thousands of artisans to build the world's largest ships.
    *   *India:* Mughal *karkhanas* concentrated thousands of artisans to produce luxury goods for the court.
*   **Private Concentrated Industry:** Fueled by Atlantic trade, private capitalists built sugar refineries (Britain, Cairo), rum distilleries (Rhode Island), and massive ironworks (France, Russia). 

## Case Study: The Trip Family of Amsterdam
*   **War and Wealth:** The Trip family, wealthy Amsterdam merchants, lived in a massive mansion decorated with stone cannons. Originally metal traders, they moved into manufacturing to secure reliable supplies of weapons for the Dutch Republic and the VOC (Dutch East India Company).
*   **Swedish Investments:** Partnering with Louis De Geer, they invested heavily in Swedish ironworks (like Finspång). They imported 5,000 skilled Walloon laborers. 
*   **Scale and Unrest:** Their foundries produced millions of pounds of cannons, which were used to secure global Dutch colonial holdings. When the Thirty Years' War ended and layoffs loomed, their thousands of workers went on strike, showing the early rise of organized labor unrest.

## The Evolution of Labor Systems
*   **Diverse Labor Forms:** Early capitalism utilized every form of labor available: petty entrepreneurship, apprenticeships, family/patriarchal labor (women working unpaid for husbands), serfdom (Silesia), and outright slavery (Mexican textile *obrajes*, Egyptian oil presses).
*   **The Rise of Wage Labor:** Slowly, a distinct new form emerged: wage labor. Whether it was journeymen in Danzig, Walloon ironworkers in Sweden, or oil mill workers in Cairo, paying workers for their time (treating labor power as a commodity) became the laboratory for modern capitalist labor systems.

## The Crucial Role of the State
*   **State-Capitalist Alliance:** Rulers and capital owners collaborated closely. European states, trying to catch up with superior Asian manufacturing, utilized aggressive state economic policies.
*   **Protectionism:** Britain banned Indian cotton imports in 1701 to protect its own infant industries. The Navigation Acts forced trade onto British ships. Germany, China, and Japan also used tariffs and closed economies to protect domestic production.
*   **Regulation and Coercion:** The state enforced quality control. In Hirschberg, inspectors evaluated cloth, and weavers who broke rules were locked in iron collars at church. Furthermore, states used military force to suppress labor strikes (e.g., Abbeville, France, 1716).

## Conclusion: The Eve of the Industrial Revolution
*   **The Global Picture circa 1700:** The world was highly connected. India and China remained the manufacturing core, but Europe was catching up via state support and wealth extracted from the Atlantic trade.
*   **A Divergent Future:** Proto-industrialization did *not* always lead to modern factories. Hirschberg never industrialized and fell into poverty; India's industry hit an "evolutionary cul-de-sac"; East Asia followed its own unique path. No one at the time predicted the coming Industrial Revolution.
*   **The Fungibility of Capital:** The era proved that capital was fundamentally fluid. Unlike feudal land, capital could morph from trade wealth into agricultural investment, and then into manufacturing. This set the stage for the perfect storm that would soon create modern industrial society.

# Chapter Notes: The Perfect Storm

## Introduction: The Story of Johann Jakob Bethmann
*   **The Lure of the Atlantic:** In 1740, a young German merchant named Johann Jakob Bethmann moves to Bordeaux, France. Bordeaux is booming due to its pivot from the wine trade to the African slave trade and the import of Caribbean colonial goods (sugar, coffee, indigo). 
*   **A City Built on Colonialism:** By 1750, Bordeaux is France’s fourth-largest city. It claims a third of French colonial trade, almost entirely dependent on the slave economies of Saint-Domingue and Martinique. Colonial profits added ~7% to the French national income.
*   **The Tricontinental Network:** Bordeaux acts as a hub. Bethmann uses his family’s Frankfurt connections to import colonial goods into Central Europe. He vertically integrates his business, gaining monopolies (e.g., supplying a Habsburg sugar refinery).
*   **From Trade to Slavery:** Like many merchants, Bethmann inadvertently and intentionally becomes a plantation owner. To secure supply, merchants extend credit to planters; when planters default, merchants take over the land. 
*   **The Slave Trade:** By the 1760s, Bethmann begins investing heavily in the transatlantic slave trade. He becomes the largest investor in *Romberg, Bapst & Cie*, a massive firm that integrates slave trading, plantation management, and commodity shipping. Slaving becomes the firm’s most profitable venture.
*   **Capitalist Feedback Loop:** Bethmann makes staggering profits (363,000 livres in 1791 alone). Crucially, he sends these profits back to his family in Germany, where they are used to fund early European industrialization, including Germany’s first mechanized cotton-spinning factory.

## Core Concept: The "Perfect Storm" of Capitalism
*   **A Violent Coproduction:** Capitalism did not emerge from a static set of European institutions or cultures. It was a revolutionary, violent, global mixture of forces. The author identifies four interconnected processes that formed a "multidimensional feedback loop":
    1.  The Great Connecting (global trade networks).
    2.  The Transformation of the Countryside (commercial/plantation agriculture).
    3.  The Intensification of Industry (proto-industrialization).
    4.  The Expansion of State Power.
*   **Loop 1: Global Trade Boosts Industry:** Expanding global markets stimulate manufacturing worldwide. Indian weavers alter patterns to suit African tastes to trade for ivory and slaves; European linen makers drastically increase production for the Americas.
*   **Loop 2: Trade Drives Urbanization:** Global commerce causes port cities (Bordeaux, London, Amsterdam) to explode in population. This urbanization creates massive domestic markets for both rural food and manufactured goods.
*   **Loop 3: Global Competition Drives Innovation:** Exposure to superior foreign goods (like Chinese porcelain and Indian textiles) forces European manufacturers (like Wedgwood) to innovate, imitate, and improve efficiency to capture middle-class markets.
*   **European Exceptionalism:** While proto-industry happened globally (especially in Asia), the specific fusion of global trade, extreme rural transformation (slavery), and state military support happened uniquely in Western Europe. Extra-European exports directly supported Britain and France's core industrial growth. 

## The Role of the Countryside and Plantations
*   **Loop 4: Plantations as Captive Markets:** The radical transformation of the countryside—specifically the creation of slave plantations—created unprecedented demand for manufactured goods. Because plantations focused solely on cash crops, they had to import *everything*: tools, food, clothing for the enslaved, and luxury goods for the planters.
*   **Textiles and Slaves:** The production of textiles (in India and Europe) was directly fueled by the slave trade. For over 350 years, textiles made up more than half the value of goods exchanged for enslaved people in Africa.
*   **Heavy Industry and Slavery:** Heavy industries grew due to Atlantic demands. 
    *   *Copper:* English copper production boomed to create boiling kettles for Caribbean sugar and trade goods for Africa. Pumping water from these expanding copper mines led to the invention of the **Newcomen steam engine**.
    *   *Iron:* Foundries in Birmingham and Nantes specialized in forging the hardware of coercion: chains, shackles, and "negro irons" for slaving voyages.

## "Ghost Acres" and Raw Materials
*   **Escaping Malthusian Limits:** The Americas provided Europe with vast quantities of food, fiber, and raw materials. Sugar shifted from a luxury to a daily staple, providing massive, cheap caloric intake for British workers. 
*   **New Industries:** Slave-grown commodities spawned entirely new industries in Europe, such as chocolate manufacturing in Bristol (Fry's) and Switzerland. 
*   **Resource Reallocation:** Europe benefited from "ghost acres"—huge expanses of land outside Europe that fed its economy without using local European resources. This immense wealth reallocation benefited a tiny elite of European capital owners, while wages for the working class remained at medieval levels.

## Global Hierarchies: Enriching Europe, Draining the Rest
*   **Acknowledged Realities:** Early economic thinkers openly recognized that the slave trade was the "mainspring of the machine" of European wealth. It wasn't just port cities; entire national economies relied on it.
*   **Widespread European Complicity:** 
    *   **France:** Wealth from Saint-Domingue built the port of Nantes and fueled the Parisian real estate market.
    *   **The Dutch:** The slave economy added 5% to the total Dutch GDP (and 10% to the province of Holland), fueling their shipping, refining, and textile sectors.
    *   **Central/Eastern Europe:** German and Swiss merchants (despite being landlocked) heavily invested in plantations, slave voyages, and the production of trade goods (*indiennes*).
*   **British Dominance:** The Atlantic slavery complex accounted for ~11% of British GDP by the late 18th century. Plantations offered massive return rates. The wealth generated created multi-generational political and economic dynasties (e.g., the Drax family, whose descendants remain powerful today).
*   **The Ultimate Proof of Value:** When slavery was abolished in 1835, the UK government borrowed 40% of its national budget to *compensate the slaveholders* (a debt not fully paid off until 2015). 
*   **The Asian Drain:** Simultaneously, European colonial forces (like the East India Company in India) used their military power to extract massive agricultural taxes, draining wealth from Asia rather than investing in local development.

## Institutional Innovation and State Power
*   **Knowledge Production:** The "industrial enlightenment" and scientific progress in Europe were largely driven by the unique challenges of global extraction. Needs like ocean navigation, managing massive slave labor forces, and colonial resource censuses drove innovation. Europeans also appropriated knowledge (e.g., the Dutch transferring coffee plants from Yemen to Java to the Americas).
*   **The Crucial Role of the State:** Capitalists allied with states because rulers needed tax revenues from trade to fund wars. 
*   **War Capitalism:** This era was defined by "war capitalism"—a dual system featuring the rule of law, contracts, and private property *inside* Europe, funded by lawlessness, extreme violence, slavery, and military monopolies *outside* Europe. The peaceful development of Europe's interior relied on the violent expropriation of its exterior.
*   **Uprooting Competitors:** European state-capitalist alliances actively weakened competitors. Native American and African merchant classes were destroyed or marginalized. In Asia, colonial states began erasing traditional rights, forcing weavers and farmers into unequal, legally binding contracts.

## Case Study 1: Boston and the Caribbean
*   **A Struggling Colony Saved by Sugar:** Boston was originally a struggling, cold Puritan settlement. Its fortunes changed when Barbados fully pivoted to sugar production and stopped growing food or cutting timber.
*   **The Provisioning Trade:** Because of the British Navigation Acts, Boston stepped in to supply the Caribbean with everything it needed to survive: salted cod, beef, horses, timber, and grain. 
*   **Building Northern Wealth:** This trade rescued New England's economy, leading to a massive shipbuilding and rum-distilling boom. This inevitably led New Englanders directly into the African slave trade.
*   **Institutions Built on Slavery:** The vaunted "efficient institutions" of New England—including universities like Harvard—were deeply funded by merchants whose wealth derived from Caribbean slavery and the slave trade.

## Case Study 2: The Cotton Industry (The Eye of the Storm)
*   **Ancient Roots:** Cotton spinning was an ancient industry, with India acting as the undisputed global leader. Europe had only a small proto-industry of peasants working in their homes.
*   **The Catalyst of Global Trade:** Global trade exposed Europeans to Indian cottons, creating massive domestic demand and proving essential for trading for slaves in Africa. 
*   **Industrial Espionage:** To compete, Europeans (especially the French) engaged in industrial espionage, traveling to India to observe, document, and copy South Asian weaving and dyeing techniques.
*   **Securing Raw Materials:** Because Europe cannot grow cotton, it relied on the violently transformed countryside of the Americas. Slave plantations in places like Saint-Domingue began growing cotton, providing European manufacturers with an elastic, cheap supply of raw material.
*   **State Protectionism:** European states (France, Britain, Prussia) aggressively protected their fledgling cotton industries by banning or placing massive tariffs on superior Indian imports.
*   **The Breakthrough:** With a guaranteed market (via protectionism) and guaranteed raw materials (via slavery), European inventors faced immense incentives to automate. This led to breakthroughs like the spinning jenny (Hargreaves) and the water frame (Arkwright), launching the Industrial Revolution.

## Conclusion: Enter Adam Smith
*   **The Storm Gathers:** By 1750, the capitalist revolution was still a slow-moving, globally dispersed root system. However, in certain places (like Britain), it began to break through traditional economic systems.
*   **The Sanitization of Capitalism:** At this exact moment, Adam Smith observed the incredible productivity of this new system. However, Smith stripped his theories of the violence, state coercion, monopoly, and slavery that actually built the system. He attributed capitalism’s success purely to free markets and individual self-interest, forging the triumphant, sanitized myth of capitalism that persists today.
