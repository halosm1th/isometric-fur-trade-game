## Networking: Global Perspectives on Nineteenth-century British Missions - Tolly Bradford
- "A system of global networks were used and built by the nineteenth-century British mission movement. This article offers examples of these global networks and examines their significance and the reasons these global networks are overlooked in existing histories of missions. This brief examination concludes that while global networks of information gave missionaries a broadly contextualized view of their mission fields, it also clouded their judgment about local issues and affairs"

	- Vin 3: 1836 British Parliametnayr commission on Aboriginal people
- "The above vignettes suggest that throughout the nineteenth century, networks of information were built and used by missionaries, mission organizations and the empire. Missionaries, these vignettes suggest, -whether indigenous or British, whether at the periphery or in the metropole of empire - were thinking globally and locally as a result of their connections to these networks. What I argue below is that these complex networks animating the mission enter- prise are difficult to see in the existing historiography about nineteenth-century British missions. Existent historiography on missions explores how missionaries were linked to formal imperialism and how the encounter between missionaries and indigenous people occurred at a local level but says little about global missionary networks. What is missing in current scholarship, for example, are analyses of the how the CMS operations in West Africa influenced its operations in Canada or how Tiyo Soga’s understands of his local situation in South Africa was informed by his assumptions about missionary success in Fiji. Highlighting these mission networks in the history and their omission in the historiography is the focus of this article. In what follows I present a selected historiography before outlining how the idea of networks might be addressed in future research."

The three vignettes I opened with offer grounded ways to do this; I concluded with brief examples of these networks in action. - "Beginning in the 1990s, much of the historiography of missionary-Indigenous interaction in 19th-century Canada and the British Empire has explored how Indigenous leaders made very active and conscious use of the missionaries and Christianity in the framing and shaping of their politics, particularly in their political interactions with the colonial state.1 ... - "this article explores the uses of Christianity and missionaries by Plains Cree leaders in the area of the North Saskatchewan River valley between the late 1850s and the early 1870s. It argues that in the years immediately preceding Treaty Six (1876)—years marked by the collapse of the buffalo herds on the plains and conflict between the Cree and the bands of the Blackfoot Confederacy—there was significant division amongst Cree leaders about the value of missionaries. On the one hand, leaders around the areas of Edmonton and Fort Carlton sought to develop strong alliances with missionaries, seeing missionaries as useful allies in their bids to maintain leadership within their bands during an era of change. In outlining these patterns of responses, I emphasize that this divisiveness was not rooted in differing religious views per se, but rather in different beliefs about whether the missionary was an asset or a threat to a leader’s ability to provide for, and maintain their own authority within, their band. - "The traditional interpretation of the Evans scandal as a struggle be tween commerce and Christianity, or between an evil genius and a Christian Saint must be discarded. Social antagonism, not a fundamental split over policy between the missionary society and Company, was the principal factor causing the Evans difficulties. In large part the older interpretation is a product of the tendency to examine the Hudson's Bay Company in solely commercial terms. While commerce may have been its principal raison d'etre, it was also a civil government and to most in Rupert's Land a way of life. By the 1840's a unique society had evolved in the West. It was a society divided vertically by race, by religion, and by the military-like structure of the Company's service, but united horizontally by an intricate web of Scots, Orkney, Indian, and half-breed alliances. While the social gradations, upward mobility, and depth of class cleavage must still be determined, the Evans quarrel suggests that the stresses inherent in this society had a profound impact on the conduct and politics of the trade. Social and personal antagonism aggravated and were aggravated by the Red River free-trade turmoil of the mid 1840's. Perhaps the Sinclairs were as much social cast-offs from Company society as the Evanses. What is needed is a detailed study of the Company as a social rather than a commercial edifice."
