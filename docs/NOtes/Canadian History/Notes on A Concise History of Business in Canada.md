# Comprehensive Study Notes — Taylor & Baskerville, _A Concise History of Business in Canada_

_Graham D. Taylor & Peter A. Baskerville, Oxford University Press, 1994._ _Notes cover the Introduction, all 20 chapters, and the Epilogue at uniform depth, plus a consolidated Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) thread and a thematic synthesis. Baskerville wrote the pre‑1885 material (Intro and the early chapters); Taylor wrote the Introduction and the post‑1885 material._

> **Sourcing caveat:** the underlying text was an Internet Archive OCR scan, so specific figures, dates, and dollar amounts should be spot‑checked against the page images before being quoted in formal work. The arguments and named scholars are reliable; the third or fourth digit of a statistic is the kind of thing OCR can corrupt.

---

## 1. The book in one page (overall notes)

**Core thesis.** The book _qualifies_ rather than rejects Harold Innis's **staples thesis**. ==Canada did grow staple by staple (cod, fur, timber, wheat, minerals, newsprint, oil), but alongside the staples it built a diversified _internal_ economy — local manufacturing, retailing, finance, services, and a dense rural‑industrial fabric — that the staples model alone misses.== Staples conditioned development; they did not wholly dictate it.

**Framework.** ==The authors apply a Gras/**Chandlerian** lens — "capitalist evolution" through stages of organization (from the individual merchant, to the partnership, to the integrated managerial corporation) — but they insist on three distinctively Canadian dimensions: (1) organizational change over time; (2) a particular Canadian character (staples _plus_ persistent regional tensions _plus_ a heavy legal/constitutional/state hand); and (3) an international setting in which Canada is always pulled by a larger metropolis — first France, then Britain, then, decisively, the United States.==

**What's distinctive about the book's stance.** ==It restores _agency_ to actors usually treated as passive: Native traders, habitants, Maritime merchants, francophone entrepreneurs, women in business, small proprietors and co‑operators. It treats merchant capital as capable of _blocking_ industrialization (the Maritimes) as well as enabling it (Upper Canada). It treats the state as a constant, active partner of business — not a neutral referee. And it repeatedly argues that change was **gradual and continuous** rather than marked by sharp revolutionary breaks (no "1840 turning point," no "Conquest decapitation," no single "managerial revolution").==

**Recurring scholarly interlocutors.** Innis (staples); Chandler (managerial capitalism); Michael Bliss (_Northern Enterprise_; skeptical that any group durably controlled the economy); Tom Naylor (merchant‑capital/financial‑oligarchy thesis); Morris Altman, Douglas McCalla, Marvin McInnis (quantitative Upper/Lower Canada); Gerald Tulchinsky; Gilles Paquet & Jean‑Pierre Wallot (Lower Canada's "two developmental groups"); Serge Courville and Gérard Bouchard (rural Quebec); W.J. Eccles, Dale Miquelon (New France); Sylvia Van Kirk, Toby Morantz, Ann Carlos & Stephen Nicholas, Elizabeth Mancke (fur trade/HBC); Rosemary Ommer (Atlantic fishery); Ben Forster (tariff); Christopher Armstrong & H.V. Nelles (utilities); Kris Inwood & the Bloomfields (1871 industrial data); Hugh Aitken ("defensive expansionism"); Gabriel Kolko/Wallace Clement (corporate‑liberal & continental‑elite views).

## 2. Chapter‑by‑chapter notes

### Introduction — Approaching Canadian business history

- Sets up the **staples‑qualification** program and the Gras/Chandler "stages" framework. Business history is more than firm biographies: it is the study of how economic organization changes and how it interacts with geography, social structure, law, and the international order.
- Three analytic dimensions the book will track throughout: **organizational change** (merchant → partnership → corporation → managerial/conglomerate firm); the **particular Canadian character** (staples economy + regional/sectional tension + heavy legal and state constraint); and the **international setting** (a small open economy perpetually oriented to a larger metropolis).
- Frames the **HBC as an early multinational** — a chartered, London‑directed enterprise operating a continental resource business — foreshadowing the book's interest in foreign direct investment and branch‑plant structures centuries later.

### Part 1 — Commerce and Colonization

**Chapter 1 — Contact: trade and the commercial revolution.**

- **Trade was the smoothest zone of contact** between Natives and Europeans — less violent and more mutually legible than missionary or military contact, because both sides already understood exchange.
- **Pre‑contact Native trade networks were extensive and sophisticated:** Lake Superior copper, Lake Erie flint (the chapter's memorable phrase: flint as "the first casualty of free trade" as European goods displaced it), elite‑controlled ceremonial exchange at sites like Rice Lake. Native economies were _not_ subsistence isolates.
- **Europe's "commercial revolution"** was as much about the rural **putting‑out system** (merchants distributing materials to rural producers) as about glamorous long‑distance trade — a template for how a dispersed productive economy can underlie a trading one.
- The **Newfoundland cod fishery** is the first staple: a potential "staple trap," but the migratory fishery was run as sophisticated **"seasonal factories overseas,"** with complex labour, capital and provisioning organization. Hamell's work on **proto‑contact** (European goods reaching Natives ahead of Europeans themselves, reshaping demand).

**Chapter 2 — The fur companies, 1603–1663.**

- The **joint‑stock chartered company** appears as a novel organizational form — a way to pool capital and spread risk for a long‑distance venture, but also an arm of the state with **incompatible goals** (profit vs. settlement vs. evangelization vs. imperial strategy), which repeatedly undermined performance.
- The French **Compagnie des Cent‑Associés** and its successors **failed repeatedly**, but their bankruptcies effectively _subsidized_ settlement — a recurring Canadian pattern in which failed business ventures leave behind durable infrastructure and population.
- **Native middlemen** (Huron, Mohawk, Neutrals) controlled the interior trade and were indispensable partners, not bystanders; the **destruction of Huronia (1649–50)** was a commercial as well as a military catastrophe that reordered the whole trade.

### Part 2 — The Old Regime in New France

**Chapter 3 — The framework of New France.**

- After **1663** New France became a royal province. The chapter punctures the cliché of French "absolutism": in practice administration was **decentralized and venal** (the corruption of officials like Bigot is illustrative).
- The comparative argument: England tilted toward capitalism, France toward a more "feudal"/seigneurial order — producing **unbalanced rather than simply slower growth**. The point is structural: French institutions inhibited _linkages between sectors_, not raw output. Uses Altman's quantitative estimates of New France's economy.

**Chapter 4 — The fur trade frontier, 1653–1763 (founding of the HBC — key chapter).**

- **HBC chartered 1670.** The book adopts an **internalist, "proto‑multinational" reading** (Carlos & Nicholas; Mancke): the Company is best understood as an early integrated firm solving classic management‑at‑a‑distance problems.
- **Managerial control mechanisms:** loyalty oaths, performance bonds, reading servants' mail, recruiting disciplined **Orkney servants**, and an apprenticeship system — the same control toolkit the book will later see in railways and factories.
- The **"Standard of Trade" and the Made Beaver** unit: an administered‑price system that nonetheless allowed _competitive pricing within fixed published prices_. The **"stay by the Bay" strategy** kept overhead low (let Natives come to coastal posts), at the cost of ceding the interior to the French and later the Nor'Westers. The Company paid **no dividend 1690–1718**.
- **Native agency and credit:** the **homeguard Cree**; credit extended as a form of **reciprocity** (Morantz) rather than simple debt‑bondage; Wein's **"division of spoils."** Debt only makes sense inside its local social context — a point the book reuses for Upper Canada (Ch 9).

**Chapter 5 — Business in the Old Regime.**

- The **"Canada trade"** was structured by **kinship and religion** (La Rochelle and Bordeaux networks; Huguenot "New Converts"). **Louisbourg** as a cod and trade entrepôt; **Acadian illegal/contraband trade**; craft producers; the **Saint‑Maurice ironworks** as an early industrial enterprise.
- **Seigneurial dues siphoned off surplus**, dampening reinvestment. The book **rejects the "Conquest decapitation" thesis** (that the Conquest beheaded a French‑Canadian commercial class): business activity was funnelled into the countryside rather than destroyed. The **Cochrane / "Old Corruption" army‑bill affair** illustrates how military finance created fortunes.

### Part 3 — Colonial Capitalism in Transition, 1791–1871

**Chapter 6 — Introduction to Part 3.**

- Frames a **"long and languorous" industrialization**; the persistence of **gentlemanly capitalism** (landed wealth fused with commerce); the ebbing of mercantilism; the **US as a less‑fettered entrepreneurial model**; and a deliberately **region‑by‑region method** for the chapters that follow.

**Chapter 7 — The fur trade, 1763–1871 (the flagship HBC chapter).**

- **NWC vs. HBC rivalry.** The **North West Company's** wintering‑partner profit‑sharing made it agile and aggressive; the HBC's London control, Orkney labour and **York‑boat** logistics made it stable but slow.
- **HBC won by _copying_ the NWC:** it adopted **profit‑sharing (1809)**, St Lawrence labour, and backed **Selkirk's Red River colony** to control provisioning. The NWC's fatal flaw was an accounting structure (limited‑partnership, no amortization) that couldn't sustain a long price war.
- **Edward Ellice brokered the 1821 merger** — the HBC _name_ survived, but the NWC supplied 32 of 53 partners. **George Simpson** then ran a near‑monopoly **1821–71**: ruthless retrenchment; a hardening **racial hierarchy over a mixed workforce** to prevent collective resistance; country‑wife relationships ("bits of brown"); the **Snake‑country scorched‑earth** policy (deliberately trapping out the region to deter American competition → contributed to the **Oregon Treaty, 1846**); diversification from beaver to marten and into non‑fur goods; and the **Métis pemmican and buffalo‑robe trade**.
- **1869: Rupert's Land sold to Canada for £300,000.** **1871 crossroads:** diversify into retail/land vs. stay in fur — the diversify camp won _narrowly_; fur nonetheless remained the Company's most profitable line until about 1900.

**Chapter 8 — The Atlantic region: "arrested transition."**

- Maritime **"merchantocracies"** (Halifax, Saint John) **actively blocked industrialization** to protect their lucrative import trade — the book's clearest case of merchant capital as an _obstacle_ to development.
- The result was an **enclave / disarticulated economy** with weak inter‑sector linkages. **Shipbuilding** is described as "the oceanic extension of the merchant's warehouse"; **occupational pluralism** turned fishing/farming households into a cheap‑labour bank.
- Key sectors and firms: **lumber** (Cunard, Gilmour), **coal** (the **General Mining Association** monopoly), and the fishery's **truck system** (Ommer; Charles Robin). The chapter's verdict: the Maritimes substantially **"made their own bed"** — structural choices by the merchant elite, not just geography or later federal policy, set up the region's long decline.

**Chapter 9 — From hinterland to heartland: Upper Canada/Ontario.**

- **A commercial agrarian economy from the start.** Rural Upper Canada ran on **long credit and barter** through **country stores** and itinerant pedlars; it was _not_ a self‑sufficient subsistence world. Competition among local and urban wholesalers gave farmers real bargaining leverage (the farmer who hawked produce door‑to‑door rather than accept low merchant prices).
- **Mixed farming, not a wheat monoculture.** Even at the "wheat boom's" height, wheat averaged only ~22% of cultivated acreage; bigger farms _diversified_ more, not less. McCalla and McInnis show British prices were often _below_ Montreal prices, so the staple model mispredicts; **wood may have been the more important conditioning staple** for _both_ Canadas.
- **The land business.** Pervasive **absentee speculation** (Family Compact figures; the Baby/Askin families exploiting Native land); the **Canada Company's** purchase of **2.5 million acres in 1825**. The **Court of Chancery (est. 1837)** and judges like **John Beverley Robinson** and **William Hume Blake** adopted an **instrumentalist jurisprudence** — departing from British precedent to keep a smooth private land market running. **Bargain‑and‑sale contracts** (instalment purchase) were the workhorse financing tool because mortgages were scarce and legally cumbersome.
- **Forest enterprise.** Sawmills outnumbered grist mills; the **Ottawa Valley** trade (Hamilton Brothers at Hawkesbury; **Calvin & Company on Garden Island** as the great transhipper of rafts to Quebec City; oak cartels controlling two‑thirds of the trade). The **système agro‑forestier** linked farm and forest labour; big lumberers ran **company stores** that ate workers' wages. McCalla/McInnis: French‑Canadian woods wages may have helped Lower Canada _pay for_ Upper Canadian wheat — reinforcing wood's central role.
- **Commercial Upper Canada.** Early merchant‑bureaucrats (Richard Cartwright, **Robert Hamilton**, John Askin, **William Allan**) grew rich on **military provisioning** during the Revolution and the War of 1812. **Simcoe tried and failed** to subordinate merchants to the administrative capital; in practice **gentlemanly capitalism** fused merchants, bureaucrats and a land elite. Municipal incorporation was a **business‑dominated** project; notably, **Conservatives in the Assembly were _more_ business‑heavy than Reformers** (51% vs. 26% businessmen/lawyers in the 1830s).
- ==**The "1840 turning point" debate — rejected.** The book argues **capitalism was alive well before 1840**: chartered corporations from 1831, large state borrowing for canals in the 1830s, speculative banking — so the shift from gentry to bourgeois rule (Baldwin → Hincks) was a **deepening of a continuous trend, not a rupture.**==
- **Railways, industry and the state, 1850–71.** After two failed attempts to raise capital, state guarantees did the trick: the **Guarantee Act (1849)**, the **Consolidated Municipal Loan Fund (1852)**, and the **Main Trunk Line Act**. Great Western and Grand Trunk dominated; merchants invested **cautiously and indirectly** — "smart money went into the land the railway opened, not into railway shares" (George Brown's Bothwell townsite). American contractors (the corrupt **Samuel Zimmerman**) inflated costs; municipalities and railways drowned in debt; the **Bank of Upper Canada** nearly collapsed. The state then **cleaned house** (audit department, first consolidated balance sheet, a protective‑leaning tariff) and tightened labour control (**Master and Servant Act, 1847**; railway **rule books** with company‑levied fines) — building the **bourgeois state that made Confederation possible.**
- **Industrialization by 1870.** Still mostly **small, dispersed, craft‑based** (18,600 of 21,730 establishments employed ≤5 workers), but **diverse**: local production met ~80% of provincial needs (vs. ~50% in the Maritimes); Ontario already produced ~52% of Canada's industrial output. Railways (Grand Trunk, Great Western) were the first **large, vertically integrated, managerially advanced corporations** — but had **few imitators**. The **Jacques & Hay** sidelight shows the troubled transition to integrated management (kin loyalty trumping efficiency at the New Lowell branch). Steam spread slowly; persistent Great Lakes **schooners** were a _rational_ choice, not backwardness. The **Bank of Upper Canada** sidelight reframes that bank as a genuine commercial institution serving York merchants, not merely a political tool.

**Chapter 10 — Farm, factory, and finance: Lower Canada/Quebec.**

- **The "agricultural crisis" was relative, not absolute.** Wheat collapsed (from 60–70% of field crops to <15% by 1844) due to the **wheat midge, Hessian fly, short seasons** (no fall wheat) and being undersold by Upper Canada. But **diversification was a rational response** (Courville; Paquet & Wallot): many habitants were market‑oriented and the average peasant household grew _wealthier_ between 1800 and 1835, with rising income differentiation.
- **The seigneurial system was not feudal stasis.** By 1791 **two‑thirds of seigneuries were bourgeois‑owned** (split evenly between French and English bourgeois). Seigneurs invested directly in industry, using **credit instruments** suited to a cash‑poor colony — **annuities** and the **contrat à réméré** (sale with right of repurchase). Examples: Foretier, Proulx, Drapeau; **Matthew Bell's Saint‑Maurice ironworks**; Joliette's industrial village of **"Industrie"**; Taschereau's diversified complex; **women seigneurs** as active entrepreneurs.
- **Eastern Townships land business** (sidelight): held under British free‑and‑common‑socage; dominated by speculators and the **British American Land Company** (Galt). Imperial land policy favoured **short‑term revenue over local development** (Jack Little).
- **Rural industry.** Courville's data: **~12% of rural dwellers (40,000) did not farm in 1831**; ~1,300 production units in the seigneurial lowlands in 1831, doubling by 1851 — a **decentralized proto‑industrialization** resembling New England's.
- **Forest enterprise.** The post‑Napoleonic‑blockade boom plus imperial preference drove **BNA from ~1% to 66% of UK timber imports by 1816**. A tight **Quebec City Anglo‑Scots elite** controlled the trade and the **cullers' board**; firms like **Pollock, Gilmour & Co.** (Glasgow) and brokers like **T.H. Dunn** ran the squared‑timber trade. **William Price** was the dominant figure: diversification into sawn lumber (40+ mills acquired largely by foreclosure), the **Saguenay "kingdom,"** company‑store **vouchers**, and brutal labour control. Debate: were farm and forest **complementary** (Bouchard) or did agriculture suffer (Séguin/Gagnon)? Government granting of monopolies (and forests viewed as obstacles to farming) is criticized by Jack Little.
- **Seigneurial abolition (1854) favoured the elite**: the major savings only materialized on sale; **60,000 farmers were _still_ paying rent to ex‑seigneurs' heirs as late as 1935.**
- **State, law, and business.** The **Constitutional Act (1791)** split the old cross‑ethnic merchant alliance, producing the **Parti Canadien/Patriote** (in the Assembly) vs. **English merchant‑councillors**. **Paquet & Wallot's "two developmental groups"** thesis (British St‑Lawrence‑empire builders vs. a francophone program of local growth, many small banks, and import‑not‑land taxation) is endorsed **but qualified** — the spheres overlapped (the Bank of Montreal funded local production; ~80% of development measures passed the Assembly without opposition). The **1837–38 Rebellion** was led by **urban‑village merchants and professionals** (Cartier's clientele), not a peasant jacquerie. The **Special Council (1838–41)** pushed business‑state measures; the **Séminaire de Saint‑Sulpice** sidelight shows the Church transforming from feudal landholder into a **capitalist investor** in bonds and mortgages. A **lawyer‑legislator‑entrepreneur nexus** ("positive law": Cartier, La Fontaine, Morin) built the legal scaffolding (the 1866 Civil Code, streamlined contract and labour law) for industrial transition. Francophone banks emerged (Banque du Peuple 1835; Banque Nationale; Vézina and Chinic in Quebec City). Quebec City declined relative to Montreal. **By 1870 Montreal dominated** (leather its largest sector), but **Ontario's _rural_ industry outperformed Quebec's** — the chapter's striking quantitative finding (drawing on the Bloomfields/Inwood) that industrialization in Ontario was substantially a rural, small‑town phenomenon.

**Chapter 11 — Ties that bind: railways, tariffs, and business, 1867–1885.**

- ==Confederation's two economic props were **railways + the National Policy tariff** — but business was **not** a united "national dream" front; it pursued regional, sectoral, and personal ends.==
- **Intercolonial Railway** (completed 1876): the **longest** route to the sea, **military‑driven**, carrying mostly **local** traffic, losing money in **19 of its first 40 years**. Tilley: _"We won't have the Union unless you give us the Railway."_ Its problem was structural — by the 1870s, success required a **continent‑scale unified management**, which the Intercolonial lacked.
- **Canadian Pacific Railway.** The book **rejects the purely nationalist reading**: the CPR was rooted in **international capital syndicates and American expertise** (J.J. Hill, George Stephen, Van Horne; the **Pacific Scandal**/Hugh Allan; **$25M + 25M acres + a 20‑year monopoly clause**). It **deindustrialized British Columbia** (freight rates that made Winnipeg‑to‑interior shipping as cheap as coast‑to‑interior; the CPR buying eastern inputs after taking over local transport firms — John Lutz). It opened the West to competition but **tied it to Winnipeg and the East**. The HBC's response was to hire ex‑railwayman **C.J. Brydges** and decentralize into retail/wholesale shops along the line.
- **Tariffs and the National Policy (1879).** With **no income tax**, ~60–80% of federal revenue came from **customs**. Galt's 1859 tariff already blended revenue and protection. **Ben Forster's** key argument: the National Policy was a **brokered compromise** — the manufacturing lobby was _divided_ (regionally and sectorally), the policy was **revenue‑driven**, and the **branch‑plant risk was ignored** (politicians cheered US firms hopping the tariff wall). Its impact was **regionally uneven**, mattering most in the **Prairie West** (where the CPR forced US wholesalers to buy in Canada — Den Otter), while **Maritime weakness was rooted in pre‑Confederation structure**, not just the tariff. Sidelight: **enterprising women** in urban British Columbia, who used Married Women's Property Laws to become independent property‑holders and businesswomen.

### Part 4 — The Age of Business Consolidation, 1885–1929

**Chapter 12 — Commanding heights: finance capitalism.**

- The age of **finance‑capitalist consolidation**: chartered banks consolidated dramatically (the number shrank as branch networks exploded — toward a handful of national banks with thousands of branches), alongside mortgage, life‑insurance and trust companies.
- **Two "communities of interest."** _Montreal:_ the **Bank of Montreal/CPR group** (George Stephen, Donald Smith/Strathcona) reaching into Sun Life, Consolidated Mining & Smelting, Ogilvie/Lake of the Woods flour, cotton, pulp & paper. _Toronto:_ the **George Cox group** (Bank of Commerce, Canada Life, National Trust; ties to Mackenzie, Flavelle, hydro, steel, meat‑packing). Regional financiers were eclipsed — Halifax's **John F. Stairs**, then the departure of his protégé **Max Aitken** (Beaverbrook) to Montreal and London; the 1924 merger of the two largest francophone banks (Banque d'Hochelaga + Banque Nationale). The elite was tiny (~40–50 men), bound by **kinship, club, school and church** ties, overwhelmingly Scottish‑heritage.
- **Money markets and mergers.** Capital was mobilized through **private syndicates** and brokerage houses (A.E. Ames, Dominion Securities); the Montreal and Toronto exchanges handled only "the odds and ends" — the **big money came from London and New York**. Two merger booms: **1909–13** and a larger **1925–29** wave (315 consolidations, ~$1B). The **Max Aitken** sidelight: Royal Securities, and the assembly of **Canada Cement, Stelco, Canadian Car & Foundry, Canadian Consolidated Rubber** — pioneering capital‑mobilization techniques.
- **The power debate.** Did finance capitalists _control_ the economy? **Naylor** and **Clement** (entrenched financial oligarchy / continental corporate elite) vs. **Niosi** (the ruling class was never fully entrenched; new groups — managers, Jewish and French‑Canadian entrepreneurs, western oilmen — kept emerging) vs. **Bliss** (their power was **transitory**; all groups were assailed by competition and failure). Sectoral pattern: **banks/utilities stayed Canadian‑owned; industry and mining drew more on foreign direct investment** (US direct investment ~43% in manufacturing by 1910) — the **origins of the branch‑plant economy**. Verdict: the financiers "earned the opprobrium" heaped on them but largely **mirrored a global caution** of their era.

**Chapter 13 — Networks of progress: utilities and railways.**

- Electrification as a **"theatre of science"** (Armstrong & Nelles). Telephone (Bell Canada under **Charles Sise**), electrical equipment (Canadian General Electric; **Frederic Nicholls**), and especially **electric tramways/traction**, into which **CPR‑construction veterans** (Mackenzie, Mann, Holt, Ross) pivoted as railway contracting dried up — Toronto Railway Co., then Winnipeg/Montreal/Saint John; **B.C. Electric Railway**.
- Promoters **exploited the federal system** to shop for the most permissive charters (municipal → provincial → federal). Consolidation produced regional **oligopolies via cooperation, not price war**: **Montreal Light, Heat & Power** (Herbert Holt) and **Shawinigan Water & Power** carved up the Montreal market.
- **Public power.** In Ontario the **public‑power movement** — driven by Toronto consumers and aspiring industrial towns (Berlin/Kitchener, Waterloo, Guelph) fearing a Toronto monopoly — produced **Adam Beck and Ontario Hydro** (sidelight): a "crown‑company entrepreneur" who behaved like a robber baron and, by the late 1920s, controlled three‑quarters of the province's power. Public ownership also spread on the Prairies (municipal/provincial systems; nationalized telephones in Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Alberta).
- **Latin American "utility multinationals"** (Mackenzie, Pearson, Van Horne): **Brazilian Traction** ("The Light"/"the Canadian Octopus," later **Brascan**/Bronfman), Mexican Light & Power, Killam's International Power — a "curious capital flow" combining **American technology + British capital** to exploit a neglected niche; lucrative but ephemeral in the Canadian context.
- **Railways, round two.** Mileage doubled 1900–14; two new transcontinentals were chartered — **Canadian Northern** (Mackenzie & Mann) and the **Grand Trunk Pacific/National Transcontinental** (Charles Hays) — into a market that was **overbuilt and overcapitalized**. The **CPR under Shaughnessy** survived through cautious **equity financing** and **diversification** (hotels, steamships, Cominco, real estate). When war cut off capital in 1914, the two new lines collapsed; the government took them over → **Canadian National Railways**, the first major crown corporation. Historians debate Borden's motive: **reluctant nationalizer** vs. deliberate **rationalization** (Regehr, Eagle). The CNR under **Henry Thornton** added radio ("Hockey Night in Canada," 1924), the first diesel, and pension/labour‑relations innovations.

**Chapter 14 — Gifts of nature: wheat, mining, forestry.**

- The wheat boom mattered but explains only **part** of the era's growth; the economy was diversifying (agriculture fell below one‑third of the labour force by 1911).
- **Wheat/grain trade.** **Grain elevators** (line and terminal) and the **Winnipeg Grain Exchange (1887)** concentrated power; farmers protested the elevator‑CPR "combine," leading to the **co‑operative wheat pools** of the 1920s. Supply side: farm equipment — **Massey‑Harris** (the 1891 merger), challenged by the US branch plant **International Harvester** (1903); **Hart Massey** broke ranks on the tariff (export‑oriented firms feared high input costs). Wheat was a **"traditional" transatlantic staple** (British market), drawing **less** US direct investment than other resources.
- **Mining.** The "Second Industrial Revolution" demanded special metals (nickel for armour, zinc/copper for shells). **B.C./Kootenay:** Heinze's Trail smelter → bought by the CPR → **Consolidated Mining & Smelting (Cominco)**; the **Sullivan Mine** lead‑zinc. **Sudbury nickel:** Ritchie/Canadian Copper; Orford; **Ludwig Mond**; **J.P. Morgan's consolidation into International Nickel (Inco), 1902**; the **Inco‑Mond merger (1928)** cemented near‑monopoly. Agitation for the **"manufacturing condition"** (refine nickel _in Canada_) partly succeeded during WWI. **Cobalt** silver and **Porcupine/Hollinger** gold were accessible "poor man's camps" opened by the government **Timiskaming & Northern Ontario Railway**; **Noranda** (Rouyn) and **Flin Flon** followed. The **Klondike** sidelight: little long‑term economic impact; suppliers (incl. the **HBC**, which advertised Klondike outfits) profited more than prospectors. Toronto finance bankrolled the northern mining booms ("the opulent suburbs of Toronto spell out a progression of northern mining booms").
- **Forestry.** Two overlapping cycles: the **decline of squared timber** (steamships, overcutting) and the rise of **sawn lumber** (railways, Great Lakes/US‑Midwest markets; B.C. after the CPR), then the **new pulp‑and‑paper/newsprint** industry after 1900 (eventually Canada's top export, surpassing wheat by the mid‑1950s). Lumber still had room for smaller firms (John R. Booth; the Price descendants); pulp/paper's huge capital needs forced **joint‑stock corporations and mergers**. The **"manufacturing condition"** (Ontario 1900, Quebec 1910, etc.) plus the **US newspaper publishers' lobby** (Hearst, Pulitzer) drove US newsprint‑tariff cuts (1913). Debate: did **government policy** or **resource endowments** (Trevor Dick) drive the boom? Over‑leveraged merger‑built giants (Killam, Holt) collapsed in the Depression.

**Chapter 15 — Nation builders: the managerial revolution, manufacturing, retail.**

- **Did Canada have a "managerial revolution"?** Views vary: **Heron** (yes, a "second industrial revolution" by the 1930s, central‑Canada‑dominated); **Glen Williams** (more diffuse — small/medium firms sheltered by the National Policy); **Bliss** (skeptical of merger‑built "rationalization"). Big business _did_ emerge (~600 firms = ~60% of manufacturing output by 1930; 500+ mergers 1900–30), with a shift from utilities toward extractive industries and manufacturing, and rising foreign ownership.
- **The retail revolution.** Department stores — **Eaton's** (cash‑only, fixed prices, direct manufacturer links, in‑house manufacturing, departmentalization, the mail‑order catalogue, branch stores) and **Simpson's**; regional stores (Woodward's, Dupuis Frères) — plus **mail‑order** and **chain stores** (Woolworth's, Loblaw's/Dominion/Safeway, the "five and tens" and early supermarkets). Meat packers integrated forward (William Davies, **Flavelle** → **Canada Packers**). **HBC‑in‑transition** sidelight: a **late retail entrant**; London management split over the "dying" fur trade; the trade shifted to **fox/lynx/mink**; the **bush plane and wireless** opened the north to US competitors; Vancouver store (1887), heavy Winnipeg losses in the 1920s; **Hudson's Bay Oil & Gas (1929)**; destined to become the country's **top retailer in the 1980s**.
- **Manufacturing patterns** and the National Policy. Output quadrupled 1891–1915. Two case studies show the tariff's complicated effects: **cotton textiles** formed a de facto cartel of **"complementary monopolies"** (Dominion Textile, Canadian Colored Cotton, Montreal Cotton) — driven not by the National Policy itself but by its **attenuation** under Imperial Preference (1897); strong personalities (Gault, Gordon, Whitehead/Wabasso) ran tradition‑bound firms. **Iron and steel** faced poor ore/coal and a small market; Nova Scotia's early start (Nova Scotia Steel, **Disco**); Ontario (Hamilton Steel & Iron, Dofasco); **Clergue's Algoma** (vision exceeding management, saved by tariff/rail booms); consolidation into the "big three" — **Stelco** (Aitken, 1910; the strongest, thanks to central location, good management, diversification), **Besco** (Wolvin, 1921; watered stock, weak markets, brutal labour conflict → receivership → **Dosco** under Holt), and **Algoma** (later rescued by James Dunn) — none fully integrated; two survived on **government largesse**.
- **A foreign presence.** A strong link between the **tariff and US branch plants** (37 in 1879–87 → 1,000+ by 1929; US direct investment surpassing British by 1926). **Bliss's argument:** the branch‑plant influx was **deliberate, not accidental** — the tariff was built to entice US manufacturers to "keep jobs at home," and the **Patent Act (1872) "working clause"** was meant to import technology. The **auto industry** was "rapidly submerged" (70% American branch plants by 1933 — small market, poor roads, carriage‑not‑bicycle origins, scarce engineers, proximity to Detroit); petroleum likewise (Imperial Oil/Standard Oil).

**Chapter 16 — Integration and disintegration: the limits of big business.**

- The chapter is a deliberate **dialectic**: consolidation produced **counter‑tendencies** — labour resistance, regional inequality, small‑business persistence, and co‑operatives.
- **Workplace control.** **Scientific management (Taylor)** had limited/mixed impact in Canada and met effective **worker resistance** (the Knechtel Furniture strike, 1923; CGE Hamilton). The more powerful lever was **new machinery** (Ford's assembly line; continuous‑flow mining/newsprint), troublesome mainly in older fields (iron/open hearth). The **feminization of clerical work**: women rose from ~14% to ~half of clerical jobs (1890–1930), paid less than men.
- **Welfare capitalism.** Bonuses, pensions, profit‑sharing and stock plans; **planned company towns** (Copper Cliff/Inco; the **Arvida**/Alcoa sidelight); **"industrial councils"** modelled on **Mackenzie King's** _Industry and Humanity_ — criticized by unions as **"company unions."** A hard anti‑union line persisted (the **Winnipeg General Strike, 1919**; the Besco confrontations). Government involvement (the **IDIA, 1907**) leaned toward conciliation, but courts and police were generally hostile to labour.
- **Regional inequality.** Finance and industry concentrated in **central Canada** at the expense of the Maritimes and B.C. Two explanations: **"natural advantages"** (population, resource hinterland, proximity to the US Midwest, the rail network) vs. **government policy shaped by central‑Canadian business** (National Policy captive market; subsidized east‑west railways; discriminatory **freight rates**). The Maritime case is the most‑developed debate (the **Maritime Rights Movement** of the 1920s; the freight‑rate differential history; "misguided symmetry" after the Intercolonial‑CNR merger). But Maritime decline also reflected **entrepreneurial limits** and **structural shifts beyond anyone's control** (declining British lumber demand, sugar competition, the Panama Canal). The deep misfortune: the Maritimes were never a **hinterland** for central Canada — first a competitor, then a "neglected appendage"; the Prairies were "virtual colonies."
- **The persistence of small business.** Most firms were small (89% of manufacturers had <$200k output and <50 workers in 1930; small retailers were 90%+ of enterprises). In Canada, big integrated firms clustered in **producer goods and resource processing**, not consumer goods (unlike the US/Britain). The **fisheries** illustrate the limits of consolidation (B.C. Packers' repeatedly failed mergers; the Great Lakes and east‑coast fisheries; trawler licensing; the lobster fishery and the **Antigonish Movement** co‑operatives).
- **Agriculture and co‑operatives.** Unlike US consolidation, prairie farmers built **producer co‑operatives** and wielded **political clout**: the grain growers' co‑ops (Partridge; United Grain Growers, 1917, under **Crerar**); the **Wheat Board** saga (WWI board → 1919 board → the **wheat pools** of 1923–24, with the unlikely backers **Aaron Sapiro** and **R.B. Bennett** → collapse in the Depression, saved by Bennett's guarantees → compulsory board restored 1943). Co‑operatives **institutionalized in Canada the American Populist ideas** that withered in the US. The **caisses populaires** (Desjardins, Lévis, 1900) tied co‑operation to French‑Canadian nationalism and the Church, centralizing under the FQUR (Vaillancourt). The book stresses the **gap between co‑operative rhetoric and reality** — they were still run by professional‑manager hierarchies and self‑perpetuating director elites, "essentially a consortium of small profit‑oriented proprietors."

### Part 5 — The Age of the Activist State, 1930–1984

**Chapter 17 — The incomplete Leviathan: the state and the Depression.**

- **Theories of business–government relations:** Aitken's **"defensive expansionism"** (the state as growth engine resisting US absorption); the **political‑economy/staples** view (Naylor, Clement — the state serving a financial elite); the **"corporate‑liberal"** view (Kolko; Nelles, Traves — big business _promoting_ regulation to cartelize); and **Bliss** ("forcing the pace," but business cautious). The book leans toward a picture of a state that was **active but limited**.
- **Jurisdictional complexity** (federal vs. provincial; the Privy Council often siding with provinces over securities/insurance). State power over property was actually _more_ extensive in Canada than the US (fewer court appeals), but regulators were cautious and politically sensitive. Some regulation/crown corps had **nationalist aims** (Radio Canada, Trans‑Canada Air Lines to head off US entrants).
- Two key early‑1900s federal measures: the **Board of Railway Commissioners (1903)** (the freight‑rate/rebating grievances; the Standard Oil rebate rumour; **S.J. McLean** as the academic‑bureaucrat "policy entrepreneur"/proto‑mandarin; the **"capture theory"** debate, which **Cruikshank** treats cautiously); and the **Combines Investigation Act (1910)** (a weak anti‑trust tradition full of qualifiers like "unduly"; King choosing to police _abuses_ rather than break up efficient firms — partly because Canadian firms were already dwarfed by US competitors; the 1970s Royal Commission would conclude Canada needed _more_ concentration).
- **The limits of the state** were not just ideological: **no income‑tax revenue** before WWI, **deficits anathema**, **rudimentary statistics** (the Dominion Bureau of Statistics only in 1918), and a low‑paid, low‑prestige public service.
- **Confronting the Great Depression.** Global in scope, rooted in 1920s instability (overproduction; the 1929 crash as **trigger, not cause**; Smoot‑Hawley; collapse of world trade). Keynes was **not embraced until after WWII**. Canada was hit hard as a small export economy (1932–33: one‑fifth unemployed; national income ~half of 1929). **Mackenzie King** (lucky timing) vs. **R.B. Bennett** (corporate lawyer; protectionism then desperate expedients — several of which, including the **Bank of Canada** and **Radio Canada**, the Liberals adopted). Industries fared unevenly (staples hard‑hit; gold booming; the **chartered banks survived without a single failure**, thanks to the branch system). Depression‑era interventionism was "**as much symbol as substance**": **Bennett's "New Deal" (1935)** (unemployment insurance, pensions, marketing boards — mostly struck down as unconstitutional); the Wheat Board and Natural Products Marketing Board; **Harry Stevens** and the **Price Spreads** inquiry (chain stores vs. small producers) → his Reconstruction Party splitting the Tory vote. Business attitudes were **divided** (Finkel: the big‑business elite was more receptive to intervention _on its own terms_ than politicians — Gordon, Purvis vs. Flavelle; Holt promoting cartels to save his own ventures). The **Bank of Canada** sidelight: the most enduring Depression measure; opposed by chartered banks who became its main beneficiaries; **Graham Towers** as the apolitical mandarin. **Reciprocity (1935)** became King's recovery instrument (natural products only, to dodge the manufacturers' lobby). The Depression **reinforced caution, not experimentation** — no new tech‑innovative firms (unlike the US); successful entrepreneurs (K.C. Irving, Sam Bronfman) moved into **established** markets.

**Chapter 18 — The arsenal economy: war mobilization.**

- War (WWI, WWII, Cold War) **strengthened US–Canadian economic integration** and put **"dollar‑a‑year" businessmen** in charge of mobilization agencies.
- **WWI.** Canada was industrially unprepared; procurement was haphazard and scandal‑ridden (Sam Hughes; the incompetent **Shell Committee**; the faulty **Ross rifle**) → the **Imperial Munitions Board (1915)** under **Joseph Flavelle**, answerable to _Britain_, not Ottawa. Bliss notes Flavelle's method was **personal leadership and "reliable associates,"** not complex managerial systems — so Canadian war‑mobilizers emerged with a "traditional" business–government relationship intact (unlike the US War Industries Board). The IMB worked through private contracts (~$1.23B) but built **seven National Factories** and "nationalized" Ross's plant. Domestic controls (cost‑of‑living commissioner; food/fuel/paper controllers; the short‑lived **Board of Commerce, 1919**) fell short of central planning. The lasting legacy: **stronger US financial ties** (the first‑ever New York loan, 1915; the **IMB‑Ordnance Agreement, 1917**) — the war achieved in practice what the rejected **1911 Reciprocity** had sought; Britain never regained its market share; **income tax** (meant to be temporary) became permanent.
- **WWII.** Again unprepared (King's isolationism; the **Bren gun affair**) → the **Department of Munitions and Supply** under **C.D. Howe** (the "Minister of Everything"). Howe = master organizer, but (Bliss) best at **volume**, not cost/quality; idiosyncratic personnel choices; feuds (with H.R. MacMillan); Maritime complaints of regional discrimination. **46 crown corporations**, some built to seed **postwar** capability — notably **Polymer** (synthetic rubber, Sarnia → Polysar) and **Eldorado** (uranium → Atomic Energy of Canada). The **Wartime Prices and Trade Board** (Donald Gordon; a full wage/price freeze; subsidies; rationing; the 1944 **"Magna Carta of Labour"** granting union recognition) — more effective than WWI controls. The **Hyde Park Agreement (1941)** (King–Roosevelt) resolved the balance‑of‑payments crisis via US contracts + Lend‑Lease earmarking; **E.P. Taylor's** War Supplies Ltd (→ **Argus Corporation**, "a mid‑century Max Aitken"). The Canol Project and the Alaska Highway. Canada emerged with an enlarged, diversified industrial base **and** a strong domestic consumer market — avoiding the 1920s roller‑coaster.
- **Cold War mobilization (1949–59).** The **Marshall Plan** (Canada taking over ~$1B in orders); **GATT** (committing to dismantle the protective tariff over 30 years); Howe **rejecting a US‑Canada customs union** (the ghost of Laurier 1911). Strategic‑materials stockpiling drew US mining investment; the **Iron Ore Co. of Canada** (Labrador/Quebec) → the **St Lawrence Seaway** (pushed through as "national security," 1954; opened 1959). Korean‑War rearmament (the **Department of Defence Production** under Howe). An emergent **industrial‑development strategy** (a few advanced industries — atomic energy; Howe's airline and aircraft projects, with **Avro** "a tar baby"); the airline **duopoly** (TCAL/Air Canada favoured over CP Air). The **Canada‑and‑Atomic‑Energy** sidelight (Eldorado; Chalk River; **AECL/CANDU**; plutonium to the US; heavy‑water tech to India; later bribery scandals).

**Chapter 19 — "Province builders": the activist provincial state.**

- The postwar resource boom shifted initiative to the **provinces**, with five regional case studies.
- **Alberta oil/gas.** The dramatic centrepiece. From the swindle‑ridden **Turner Valley** boom and Imperial Oil/Royalite, through **Leduc/Redwater (1947–48)** and the "age of oil," to a structure of the **"Big Five" majors** (Imperial, Shell, Texaco, Gulf, BP) farming out leases to a culture of **Calgary independents**. Politics: **Social Credit** (Aberhart/Manning) — populist rhetoric, pro‑business reality; the **Oil and Gas Conservation Board** (prorationing = enforced cartelization benefiting the majors); the natural‑gas export fight (**Alberta Gas Trunk Line**). Then **Peter Lougheed's** Conservatives (1971) used the 1970s **energy crisis** as leverage — higher royalties, the **Alberta Heritage Fund** (~$8.5B), the Alberta Energy Company — with the **oil‑sands pitfalls** (Great Canadian Oil Sands; the **Syncrude** bailout). The boom ended in the 1980s.
- **Saskatchewan.** The **CCF** (1944–64) surprisingly _encouraged_ private resource development (potash, oil); then Thatcher's Liberals; then the **NDP under Blakeney** went interventionist (the **Potash Corporation of Saskatchewan, 1975**; crown corps in oil/uranium).
- **British Columbia.** The **Social Credit Bennett dynasty** — pro‑business/pro‑foreign‑investment with interventionist forays (the **B.C. Electric takeover → B.C. Hydro**, tied to the tangled **Columbia River Treaty/Peace River (Bennett Dam)** saga). Forestry (**MacMillan‑Bloedel**; US multinationals; sustained‑yield licensing, 1947) and mining (Alcan/Kitimat; Cominco; Japan as market) dominated; the Vancouver‑vs.‑interior divide.
- **Quebec — "Maîtres chez nous."** Convergence of French‑Canadian nationalism with Ontario‑style promotional policy. The myth that francophones avoided business is qualified (Forget, Béique, Sénécal; the caisses populaires) but **Linteau's** point stands — francophone firms were mostly small and were absorbed in the 1909–29 merger era. **Duplessis** (Union Nationale) talked autonomy but oversaw massive **foreign investment** (Iron Ore Co./Ungava; the 1949 asbestos strike) — though **Conrad Black** defends his record (real growth, rising living standards). Notable francophone firms: Simard/Sorel; Vachon (→ Culinar); **Bombardier** (Ski‑Doo → Beaudoin). The **Quiet Revolution** under **Lesage (1960)** was the most interventionist provincial government (modelled on postwar France): crown/mixed enterprises (**SGF; Sidbec** steel — chronic deficits), **Hydro‑Québec** (Lévesque's nationalization; "maîtres chez nous"; James Bay — the most enduringly successful), and crucially the **Caisse de dépôt et de placement (1965)**, the key vehicle for francophone business expansion (Provigo, Bombardier, National Bank; blocking foreign takeovers; the **Desmarais/Power Corp/CPR** episode → the 1982 federal 10% restriction). Most francophone business leaders were **anti‑separatist** (Desmarais). The limits: manufacturing still declined; francophone business concentrated in **finance/services**.
- **The Maritimes — "the perils of province‑building."** The Depression descended in **1921**; manufacturing kept declining (Dosco's steel‑plate mill closed under Howe; the merchant marine dismantled; rising freight rates). Provincial efforts to lure industry — Nova Scotia's **Industrial Estates Ltd (IEL)** under **Stanfield/Sobey** (the **Michelin** success vs. the **Deuterium/Glace Bay** heavy‑water fiasco and the **Clairtone** disaster). The **K.C. Irving** sidelight: the New Brunswick empire‑builder (Irving Oil, auto service, paper mills; vertical integration; resentment of Imperial Oil's pricing).

**Chapter 20 — Millionaires, mandarins, and multinationals.**

- The postwar **"age of giant corporations"** and professional management (Galbraith's **"new industrial state,"** 1967 — big corporations + Big Labour + Big Government). But behind the façade of stability lay change: the leading firms shifted from manufacturing/mining toward **oil & gas** and **"other"** (retailing, media, real estate) by 1980.
- **The conglomerators.** Canada offered a receptive environment (fewer antitrust constraints; a small cohesive financial community; resource firms dependent on external capital). **E.P. Taylor** as the proto‑conglomerator (Canadian Breweries → **Argus Corporation, 1945**; the Massey‑Harris/**Massey‑Ferguson** reorganization). Then the 1960s–70s spree: **Paul Desmarais** (Power Corporation; the failed Argus takeover → the **1975 Royal Commission on corporate concentration**, which concluded Canada needed _bigger_ firms); **Campeau** (retail); the **Reichmanns** (Olympia & York; Gulf Canada, Abitibi‑Price); the rival **Bronfman** branches (Seagram's → Conoco/Du Pont; Edper → **Brascan**, Noranda, Labatt's); **Ken Thomson** (newspapers, North Sea oil → **Hudson's Bay/Simpsons → "The Bay,"** the largest department‑store chain). The old giants became conglomerates too: the **CPR** (Ian Sinclair; Canadian Pacific Investments; dropping "Railway" from the name) and **Bell Canada** (Jean de Grandpré; Northern Telecom; **BCE**, the largest Canadian company by 1986). The 1978 Royal Commission noted conglomerate performance rarely matched expectations (debt; vulnerability).
- **The "caretaker state."** The "age of Keynes," though Keynesianism was never fully embraced (bankers wary of deficits). Canada's early commitment via the Finance **mandarins** (the 1945 **White Paper on Employment and Income**); the **Coyne affair** (1962). The 1960s expansion (health insurance, regional aid, the Industrial Development Bank; Trudeau's "just society"). Then the 1970s troubles and the era of **bail‑outs** — David Lewis's **"corporate welfare bums"** — Dosco/**Devco/Sysco** (Cape Breton); the aircraft industry (**De Havilland, Canadair** — the Dash‑7, the Challenger → later privatized to Bombardier and Boeing); the **1981–83 recession** (US Fed/Bank of Canada credit restraint; unemployment over 10% for the first time since 1939); the **Massey‑Ferguson → Varity** rescue; the **Atlantic fisheries restructuring** (the **Kirby Commission**; Fisheries Products International; National Sea Products).
- **The multinational storm.** The rise of the **"globalized economy."** US investment surpassed British by 1926 and surged postwar ($6.8B in 1950 → $35B in 1970, 80%+ of the total), controlling >half of mining/petroleum and 80–100% of autos/chemicals/rubber/tobacco — yet the foreign **share of top firms was actually declining** by 1980 (the debate peaked as control was diminishing). Key episodes: the **Trans‑Canada Pipe Line "pipeline debate" (1956)** contributing to the 1957 Liberal defeat; **Walter Gordon's** 1957 Royal Commission warning of foreign‑investment dangers; the **Auto Pact (1965)** (managed continental free trade in autos — later luring Japanese/Korean branch plants); the **Mercantile Bank affair** → the 1967 Bank Act limiting nonresident bank ownership. Rising 1960s–70s nationalism (Vietnam; Nixon's 1971 measures ending Bretton Woods) and the nationalist critique of branch plants (inefficient short production runs, no R&D, brain drain). Policy responses: the **Canada Development Corporation (1971)** (a government‑sponsored conglomerate) and the **Foreign Investment Review Agency (FIRA, 1973)** — but largely toothless (approving ~3/4 of applications; "defanged" by Mulroney in 1984). The one area approaching nationalist rhetoric was **energy**: the 1973 OPEC shock ($2 → $8/barrel; tankers diverted to the US; reserves overestimated) → controls, taxes, price freeze, and **Petro‑Canada (1975)** ("Red Square" in Calgary; under **Wilbert Hopper** it integrated downstream to become hard to dismantle) → the **National Energy Program (1980–81)**. The NEP — the apogee of federal activism outside wartime — antagonized Alberta and Washington and then **fell victim to collapsing oil prices** after 1982. The **Dome Petroleum** sidelight (Jack Gallagher's "northern vision"; over‑leveraged acquisition of Hudson's Bay Oil & Gas; the 1982 bank/government bailout; eventual sale to Amoco — Canada's most expensive takeover) epitomizes the era's debt‑fuelled hubris. The chapter closes on the **eclipse of the activist state** with the 1984 Conservative victory and the global free‑market turn.

### Epilogue — "Leaping in the dark"

- **The 1980s rebound.** GDP rose from ~$400B to ~$700B (1984–89); inflation tamed to ~4%. A landmark: **manufacturing exports exceeded the traditional staples (ex‑energy) for the first time in Canadian history.** Canadian conglomerators (Reichmanns, Campeau) invested heavily abroad — a "Canadian invasion" of US real estate — and Canada's net direct‑investment position even reversed.
- **Underlying weaknesses.** Chronic high unemployment; **lagging productivity**; Ontario manufacturing tied to a few product lines (autos) and vulnerable; export gains reflecting a **weak dollar** rather than competitive strength. The industrial‑policy debate (small/medium firms as job creators; niche markets; **McMillan** and **Michael Porter** urging Japanese‑style methods, technology investment, and education).
- **The Mulroney turn.** Dismantling the **NEP**; renaming **FIRA → Investment Canada**; cautious **privatization** (influenced by Thatcher and US deregulation; restrained by the Clark/Petro‑Canada lesson — De Havilland/Canadair sold absorbing debt; **Teleglobe**; **Air Canada**; **Petro‑Canada finally privatized 1991**). The **GST** replacing the manufacturers' sales tax.
- **The Free Trade Agreement.** Initiated by Trudeau's **Macdonald Commission** ("a leap of faith"); backed by the **Business Council on National Issues** and, strikingly, the once‑protectionist **Canadian Manufacturers' Association**. Drivers: rising **US protectionism** (the ITC; the 1986 softwood‑lumber ruling) and the risk of being shut out of emerging trade blocs. Opposed by labour, social reformers and nationalists. The negotiated **FTA** gave the US most of its energy/financial objectives and Canada a **bi‑national dispute‑resolution panel** (but no countervail exemption); Mulroney staked the **1988 election** on it (echoing Laurier 1911, but this time with business united) → in force **1989**, "the most dramatic shift in the country's economic direction since the National Policy." The early‑1990s recession complicated assessment; **NAFTA** negotiations (adding Mexico) were beginning.
- **Other challenges.** Environmental issues (oil spills; Great Lakes pollution; fish‑stock depletion; acid rain — Inco's tall Sudbury chimneys); shifting **gender** patterns (women running small/medium firms; banks abandoning male‑only credit norms); and brightening prospects for **Native business and land claims** (the 1975 **James Bay** Cree agreement and subsequent settlements) — the resource frontier finally generating Indigenous economic leverage.

## 3. The Hudson's Bay Company — consolidated thread

Pulling the HBC material together, since it runs across the whole book and was the priority focus.

- **Founding and form (Ch 2, 4).** Chartered **1670** as a London‑directed **joint‑stock chartered company** — the book's recurring example of an **early multinational** and of management‑at‑a‑distance. Carlos & Nicholas and Mancke supply the **internalist, "proto‑multinational"** reading: the HBC is best understood as an early integrated _firm_ solving information, agency and control problems, not merely an imperial instrument.
- **Control technologies (Ch 4).** Loyalty **oaths**, performance **bonds**, reading servants' mail, disciplined **Orkney** labour, apprenticeships — the same managerial toolkit the book later sees in railways and factories. The **Standard of Trade / Made Beaver** administered‑price system permitted competitive pricing _within_ fixed prices. The **"stay by the Bay"** low‑overhead strategy (no dividend 1690–1718) ceded the interior to rivals.
- **Native partnership (Ch 1, 4, 7).** Native traders and **middlemen** were indispensable; the **homeguard Cree**; **credit as reciprocity** (Morantz) rather than simple debt‑bondage. The book consistently restores Native **agency** to the fur economy.
- **The NWC contest and the 1821 merger (Ch 7).** The nimble, profit‑sharing **North West Company** forced the HBC to **copy it** (1809 profit‑sharing; St Lawrence labour; backing **Selkirk's Red River** colony). The NWC's accounting structure couldn't sustain the price war; **Edward Ellice brokered the 1821 merger** (HBC name kept; NWC supplied most partners).
- **The Simpson monopoly, 1821–71 (Ch 7).** **George Simpson** drove retrenchment; **hardened a racial hierarchy** over a mixed workforce to forestall resistance; ran the **Snake‑country scorched‑earth** policy (→ contributing to the **Oregon Treaty, 1846**); diversified the fur mix (beaver → marten) and into non‑fur goods; and drew on the **Métis pemmican/buffalo‑robe** trade. **Rupert's Land was sold to Canada for £300,000 in 1869.** At the **1871 crossroads** the Company narrowly chose to diversify (retail/land), though fur stayed its most profitable line to about 1900.
- **Adapting to the railway West (Ch 11).** Facing CPR‑driven competition, the HBC hired ex‑railwayman **C.J. Brydges** and decentralized into retail/wholesale shops along the line.
- **The retail transition (Ch 14, 15).** A **late** retail entrant; London management split over whether the fur trade was "dying"; the trade shifted to **fox/lynx/mink**; the **bush plane and wireless** opened the north to US competitors. A windfall outfitting **Klondike** prospectors; first urban store in **Vancouver (1887)**; heavy losses on the **Winnipeg** store in the 1920s; **Hudson's Bay Oil & Gas (1929)**. As a large, diversified firm it survived these setbacks.
- **The conglomerate endgame (Ch 19, 20).** Oil‑and‑gas earnings (the Continental Oil joint venture; HBOG) helped fund the retail transition; HBOG was later swept into the over‑leveraged **Dome Petroleum** empire. The Company gobbled up rivals (including **Simpsons**) and, under **Ken Thomson** (controlling interest from 1979), became **"The Bay" — the country's largest department‑store chain and top retailer of the 1980s**, exactly as the Ch 15 sidelight foretold.

**The throughline:** the HBC is the book's single best illustration of its central claims — early multinational organization, managerial control at a distance, Native agency, the danger of clinging to one staple, the necessity of _imitating_ a sharper rival, the constant continental pull, and the long arc from chartered fur monopoly to diversified modern corporation.

## 4. Cross‑cutting themes (synthesis for review)

1. **Staples qualified, not killed.** Staples conditioned growth, but a diversified internal economy (rural industry, local manufacturing, retail, finance, services) grew alongside them — and sometimes (wood in the Canadas) the "real" conditioning staple wasn't the obvious one (wheat).
2. **Agency everywhere.** Natives, habitants, Maritime and francophone merchants, women, small proprietors and co‑operators are treated as economic actors with strategies, not as passive recipients of metropolitan force.
3. **Social structure and law often outweigh geography.** Kinship, religion, ethnicity, gentlemanly‑capitalist norms, and an **instrumentalist legal order** (Chancery, the Civil Code, positive law) repeatedly shaped outcomes — sometimes more than resource endowment did.
4. **Credit and barter early; banks late.** A long‑credit economy preceded a developed banking system; debt must be read inside its local social context (the same lesson the book draws from the fur trade and from rural Upper/Lower Canada).
5. **Merchant capital cuts both ways.** It financed and diversified development in Upper Canada but **blocked** industrialization in the Maritimes to protect the import trade.
6. **Region over nation.** Business pursued regional, sectoral, and personal ends; the "national" institutions (Intercolonial, CPR, National Policy) were brokered compromises, not expressions of a shared national dream — and they produced **durable regional inequality**.
7. **The Chandlerian arc is real but uneven and late.** Large integrated managerial firms (railways, then resource processors, then conglomerates) did emerge, but with many laggards, much family/kin management, and frequent merger‑built fragility — Bliss's caution recurs.
8. **The state is a constant partner, not a referee.** From chartered companies and canal guarantees to crown corporations, bail‑outs, marketing boards, and the NEP, the state forced the pace — though its capacity was genuinely limited before the income‑tax/statistical/mandarin state of the mid‑20th century.
9. **A constant continental pull, re‑aimed over time.** The metropolis shifts — France → Britain → the United States — and the book's last act (branch plants, the Auto Pact, FIRA/Investment Canada, the NEP, and finally the **1989 FTA**) is the story of the US pull becoming decisive.
