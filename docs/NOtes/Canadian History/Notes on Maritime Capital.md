### Claude's Notes Study Notes — _Maritime Capital: The Shipping Industry in Atlantic Canada, 1820–1914_

**Authors:** Eric W. Sager, with Gerald E. Panting **Publisher:** McGill-Queen's University Press, 1990 (ISBN 0-7735-0764-7) **Dedication:** In memory of David George Alexander (1939–1980) **Evidentiary base:** The book rests heavily on the quantitative records of the **Atlantic Canada Shipping Project** (Maritime History Group / Maritime History Archive, Memorial University of Newfoundland) — chiefly Board of Trade vessel registries (series 107/108) and crew agreements for eight major ports of registry: **Charlottetown, Halifax, Miramichi, Pictou, Saint John, St John's, Windsor, and Yarmouth.**

---

## The Book in One Page (Central Argument)

Between roughly 1820 and 1880 the Maritime provinces (Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island) plus Newfoundland built one of the world's largest merchant marines under sail. By 1880 Canada may have ranked **fourth** among the world's shipowning nations; by 1910 it had fallen to **tenth or eleventh**, almost entirely because of collapse in the Maritimes. The fleets were roughly a third of their 1879 size by 1900, and shipbuilding output fell at ~13% a year in the two decades after 1879.

Sager's core claim is a **negative one against the conventional "technological obsolescence" story** (the "wooden ships and iron men" lament begun by Frederick William Wallace in the 1920s). Iron, steel, and steam are part of the story but cannot explain the collapse, because (a) world sailing tonnage was still growing into the 1900s, (b) demand for shipping in Canadian ports doubled in the very decades the fleets collapsed, and (c) Maritimers could simply have _bought_ British steamers rather than build them, but chose neither path.

His positive thesis: shipping was **"maritime capital"** — a branch of **merchant (commercial) capital** on a colonial frontier, oriented to overseas trade and the control of exchange rather than to revolutionizing production. The industry rose within British **mercantilism** (protection, the Navigation Acts, preferential timber duties) and fell during the **slow, "disarticulated" transition from merchant to industrial capitalism** and the region's integration into the Canadian Confederation. Within that transition a regional merchant marine became **redundant**. The decline was not imposed by impersonal forces but was "doomed... by Maritimers themselves" — a multitude of rational small decisions by merchant investors who pulled capital landward.

---

## Chapter 1 — Sailing Ships and Regional History (theoretical framework)

This is the conceptual manifesto of the book. It defines the problem and reviews/rejects competing explanatory frameworks.

**The problem stated.** The shipping _complex_ — three linked industries: shipbuilding, shipowning, and the carrying (shipping) of goods — did not merely decline but **collapsed** together. Because ownership and registry location were strongly correlated in the 19th century, the registry data reliably capture the collapse. Two questions any "technology" explanation cannot answer: if survival required iron steamers, why didn't Maritimers _buy_ more of them (as others did)? And if it required local production, why didn't they _build_ them?

**Frameworks reviewed and found insufficient:**

- **Neo-classical comparative advantage** (Ricardo; Peter McClelland's "location of industry" problem). Sager grants its merits and presents cost/profit evidence later, but argues it fails because: inputs were bought in international markets all nations could access; the model needs a 3-sided exchange (Canada / Maritimes / Britain) that can't be solved; it ignores **the state** (state policy repeatedly shaped comparative advantage — e.g. Japan); and it must hold **information and culture constant** (_ceteris paribus_), an assumption fatal to historical explanation. For _shipbuilding_ the model is worse: you cannot compute production costs for an ocean-going steel-steamship industry that **never existed** in the region, so "costs were too high" is unprovable. (Tellingly, by 1920 British Columbia — farthest from markets, highest pre-war costs — had 34% of Canadian steel-ship capacity vs. Nova Scotia's 14%.)
- **Staple theory / export-base theory** (Watkins, Innis, Douglass North). Appealing for a bulk-export colony, but: ships were _manufactured products_, not staples (in mid-1820s PEI, ~80% of exports were manufactures, chiefly ships); the colony most dependent on a low-value staple (Newfoundland) had the _largest_ per-capita capital stock; a transportation "linkage" need not appear at all in the staple frontier (the importing country can supply all transport). Sager invokes North's own qualification: the export base may reshape the economy's **factor endowments (especially "human capital"), the role of the state, and skills** — and _that_ points away from staples toward social structure.

**The hypothesis Sager adopts** (drawn from post-dependency / economic-anthropology literature, esp. a study of mining in Peru, and from Canadian scholars T.W. Acheson and Ian McKay): the shipping industry was part of a **mercantilist structure of exploitation of labour and resources on a colonial frontier.** Deep-sea sailing ships were "proto-industrial workplaces using wage labour" but served the interests of **merchant capital** and reinforced merchant dominance, petty commodity production, and even non-capitalist social relations. Merchant's capital captures surplus through **control of exchange and price mechanisms**, not by transforming production; per Marx, its "independent development... stands in inverse proportion to the general economic development of society." Hence the "Janus face" of merchant capital, and a regional pattern of **dualism, fragmented elites, and weak class hegemony** (the Peru parallel). Sager distances himself from R.T. Naylor's "international systems" over-emphasis on exchange relations imposed from Europe. Ships are framed as a vehicle that "produces a change of location" (productive in both neo-classical and Marxist terms) but, in Atlantic Canada, was **subordinated to merchant capital.**

## Chapter 2 — Rise of an Enclave Industry (origins to ~1850s; the role of protection)

Traces shipbuilding from a pre-industrial craft to a transplanted **British "enclave industry."**

- **Deep roots, late industry.** First recorded Maritime shipbuilding: two small vessels at Port Royal, 1606. But shipbuilding as a _manufacturing industry_ awaited the 19th century. It grew not just from forests but from **European wars + British economic growth + British policy.**
- **The protective apparatus.** The **Navigation Acts** (the 1660 "Charta Maritima"; act of 1696; the 1786 **Act of General Registry**) confined colonial trade to British ships and defined colonial-built ships as British. The rule restricting British registry to British-/colonial-built vessels stood until **1849.** Cornwallis's 1749 tonnage bounty at Halifax was an early local nudge.
- **First stimuli: West Indies + coasting.** Continuous activity by the 1780s, driven by the Loyalist influx (skills + ambition), the West Indies carrying trade (rebel colonies excluded), Nova Scotian fishing bounties, and the 1807 American embargo. Tonnage clearing NS for the British West Indies rose >4× between 1805 and 1814. Maritimers tended to **bilateral** (not triangular) trading and supplied their _own_ West Indian tonnage while relying on UK ships for the North Atlantic.
- **The timber-trade transformation.** After ~1807 the New Brunswick→Britain timber trade created a sudden, massive demand for eastbound tonnage. Correlation between timber loads exported and tonnage clearing is "virtually perfect" (annual-change correlation 1800–49 ≈ +0.94; ~88% of eastbound tonnage change attributable to timber). Tonnage clearing for the UK grew >16%/yr 1805–19.
- **An export industry to Britain.** 1815–60: **>2.2 million tons** built in the three Maritime provinces; **about half** of Maritime-built tonnage ended up on UK registry. By 1846 BNA accounted for ~64% of all colonial-registry tonnage; ~20% of all UK-registered tonnage had been colonial-built. Liverpool (timber/cotton trades), Scotland, and Ireland were the big buyers; London and Sunderland (which built its own cheap tonnage) cared less.
- **Why protection mattered.** Three distinct protections: preferential duties on colonial commodities; the Navigation Acts excluding foreign ships; and **duty-free entry of colonial ships** (a manufactured product) into Britain. Plus an effective "bounty": carrying a **homeward timber cargo** before sale (G.F. Young claimed £3–4/ton; Sager estimates a real ~15s–£1/ton, ~10% of selling price). Cheap local timber and a high inelastic supply of sailing tonnage made shipowning attractive to timber exporters (carry your own cargo, stabilize freight costs).
- **Repeal's lesson.** When the Navigation Acts were repealed (1849), foreign tonnage immediately surged into BNA–Britain trades (≈23% foreign by 1853), and UK owners bought foreign-built ships. This demonstrates the Acts' real (if unmeasurable) protective effect. Shipbuilding, like the timber trade Graeme Wynn called "a gigantic bandalore," moved with the British market. After mercantilism's dismantling, the inherited enclave industry would either **extend domestic roots or collapse.**

## Chapter 3 — Colonial Argosy (the ships themselves, their uses and technology)

The longest descriptive chapter: ships as **producer goods / inputs in staple trades**, and the surprising sophistication of "pre-industrial" wood-and-sail technology.

- **Falling freight rates ≠ falling demand.** Investment rose even as freight rates fell after 1815 because declining freights reflected **real productivity gains** in the industries transportation served, not merely a glut of ships.
- **Merchant = shipowner (and often shipbuilder).** Against the British trend toward specialization, the Maritime merchant firm _integrated_ staple trading, importing, wholesaling, retailing, and shipbuilding. Owning ships meant **control** — over staple output, distribution, and imports — on a frontier of scarce labour/capital and dispersed petty producers. (Following Davis/Williams: bulky low-value, high-freight cargoes encourage ownership by the cargo's owner.)
- **Fleet composition (registry analysis of ~5.3M tons in nine major ports).** Overwhelmingly **wooden and sail** (only ~4.8% steam, mostly auxiliary). Specialization by port:
    - **Saint John** — largest fleet (~34%), specialized in large ocean-going square-riggers (ships and barques) for timber and the UK passage.
    - **St John's / Newfoundland** — very different: ~60% schooner tonnage; the largest _steam_ share (~12.7%), mostly for the **seal hunt** (steam displaced sail and labour, intensifying capital–labour conflict).
    - **Halifax** — smaller, fore-and-aft-rigged vessels for fishing, coasting, West Indies; minimized capital outlay and left distribution labour to small owners.
    - **Miramichi** — heavily ocean-going (82% barques/ships); **PEI** — a "great shipyard in the Gulf" producing medium craft evenly across rigs for local, British, and Newfoundland owners.
- **Technological change within wood and sail.** The pre-industrial ship was an _evolving_ technology. Key trends: rising **average vessel size** (carrying capacity rose faster than cost/operating expense per ton); the shift **two→three masts** and **brig→barque/ship**; longer, deeper, finer hulls (Saint John length +99%, depth +74% from 1810s to 1850s); the **split (double) topsail** (Forbes/Howes inventions) and added staysails for weatherliness; wire standing rigging, patent windlasses, **donkey engines**, lighter spars, better cotton sail — all raising capital intensity and performance **without proportionate increases in labour.** Speed was sought not for perishables but to maximize revenue-earning passages per season and per vessel-lifetime.
- **The "coffin ship" reputation reconsidered.** Early colonial softwood vessels _were_ short-lived (NB-built ships of the 1820s lasted ~7 years on average; higher loss rates than UK ships in the timber passage). But much of the bad reputation was (1) interested testimony from British builders seeking a tariff and (2) the product of a _dangerous trade_ (deck-loads, winter passages) rather than the hulls themselves. **Capital consumption** was high early but fell dramatically: where transfers were rare (Yarmouth net investment ~43% of gross 1844–79; Windsor ~32%), it approached economy-wide norms. **Vessel longevity rose >50%** across the century (contract-building, iron knees & strapping, Lloyd's surveyors from the 1850s, mineral salt, up to 60 tons of iron in a 1,000-ton ship). Greater durability alone explains a large part of the fleet's mid-century size (a 1875 Saint John fleet ~30% smaller had longevity stayed at 1820s levels).
- **Price advantage persisted.** Colonial tonnage stayed remarkably cheap and price-stable through the 1860s–70s ($30–40/ton fitted) despite rising quality — sustained by cheap timber, **duty-free/low-duty imported inputs**, and an inherited shipbuilding infrastructure (yards as large craft "manufactories" employing 100+ men, with blacksmith shops, mould lofts, saw-pits, etc.).
- **Concentration + dispersion.** Shipowning clustered inside shipbuilding regions (proximity = supervision + supply). Output was spatially **more dispersed over time** (yards followed timber), yet **concentrated** in a few major locations and time periods — refuting the romantic image of square-riggers built "in nearly every hamlet." By the 1880s the great barque still "bore the imprint of its colonial heritage": a producer good, "the oceanic extension of the merchant's warehouse," and a form of merchant capital on a frontier.

## Chapter 4 — Maritime Merchants in the Old Colonial System (who owned the ships, pre-1850)

A social/ownership portrait of the first half of the century, built from registry shareholdings (~40,000 shareholdings across eight ports; shares measured in 64ths).

- **Widespread but concentrated ownership.** Only ~⅓ of shareholdings belonged to "merchants"; "mariners" held ~35%. But by _tonnage_, merchants dominated: ~two-thirds of new tonnage in the major Maritime ports (1820–49), similar in Newfoundland. An **era of merchants and independent owner-operators**: small proprietors (mariners, fishermen, traders, farmers, Newfoundland "planters") held nearly a quarter of new tonnage and most small (<250 ton) vessels.
- **The schooner as smallholding.** For outport families the schooner was _not capital_ but "a type of rural smallholding" — a property independent of mercantile control, vital to farming/fishing and to family self-sufficiency. Owner-operators were common (e.g., Sydney 1804: >⅔ of clearing vessels had an owner-master).
- **Ships as cautious, incomplete steps toward industrial capital.** For major investors, the barque/ship _was_ capital employing wage labour — but it remained **subordinate to commercial exchange**, and workers retained real control over ship and machinery. **The great shipowners were "merchants first and shipowners second."**
- **The "major owner" sample** (>1,000 new tons, 1820–1914): e.g., 358 individuals in Saint John holding ~73% of new tonnage there. Mostly merchants, with shipbuilders increasingly holding a few shares while merchants kept managing-owner roles. Only in **Yarmouth** did "shipowner" displace "merchant" as a primary self-description. Notable figures: **Samuel Cunard** (the archetype of diversified West Indies merchandising); **Thomas E. Millidge** (registered more tonnage than anyone); shipbuilder–owners John Duncan and John Owens; lumber merchant John Robertson.
- **Diversification within mercantile limits.** Merchant-shipowners built the **"social overhead capital"** of port towns — banks, insurance companies, gas/water/telegraph, wharves, utilities (Pictou's Carmichael/McKenzie/Graham; Yarmouth's Killams, Canns, Lovitts) — but concentrated in **commerce, finance, and infrastructure rather than manufacturing** (their main manufacturing was shipbuilding itself).
- **Why own ships rather than charter?** Profit from staple trading required _timing_ purchase/sale to maximize the buy/sell margin; owning your own vessel gave the supercargo **flexibility and "quick dispatch"** (William Roche's letter-books). Ownership also drew merchants into managing **wage labour** — disciplining masters and crews with pay bonuses for fast passages, withheld advances, and pressure for extra (dangerous) winter timber voyages (Allan Gilmour's "third trip" bonus). Yet effective control still lay with employees. The mercantile attitude (Newman, Hunt & Co.: "our business... is not to buy Fish, but to sell Goods") shows merchants dominated _exchange_, not _production_ — allowing petty commodity production and pre-capitalist relations to remain stable.

## Chapter 5 — Rise and Decline of the Ocean Fleets (the "golden age," ~1850–1900)

The analytical heart of the book: a detailed reconstruction of investment behaviour, and a **demolition of the falling-freight-rates explanation** for the collapse.

**The rise (~1850–1879).**

- The "golden age" was brief — barely a generation — and **concentrated in space**: the eight-port sample captured >80% of regional tonnage; four registries (Saint John, Yarmouth, Windsor, Halifax) accounted for ≥71% of growth to 1879.
- **The key discovery:** the boom was _not_ a surge in new building. Gross investment (new tonnage built) actually **grew more slowly** in the third quarter (~1%/yr). The age of sail was a **shift from short-term to long-term shipowning** — Maritimers _retained_ the products of an existing shipbuilding industry instead of selling them quickly to Britain. Net capital formation (transfers within 3 years removed) accelerated sharply in the 1850s (~9%/yr) and reached ~10%/yr 1849–79. **Increasing vessel longevity alone explains ~25% of the growth.** This refutes the "trapped by default" story — growth was strongest in Yarmouth/Windsor/Halifax, ports that had _never_ transferred much tonnage to Britain.
- **Why it happened — a conjuncture of state policy and markets:**
    - **Low colonial tariffs** kept inputs cheap (Acheson: nearly everything for shipbuilding/victualling entered NB duty-free).
    - **Non-tariff "protection":** rapid world trade growth (~60%/decade in the 1850s–60s) outran world carrying capacity, briefly arresting the freight-rate decline.
    - **The decline of the US merchant marine** (US carriage of its own trade fell from >70% in the 1840s to <20% by 1880) created an enormous demand for foreign tonnage in US export trades — to which colonists, via the **1854 Reciprocity Treaty** (natural products duty-free) and the telegraph, had privileged access. By the early 1880s perhaps ~40% of the Canadian fleet was involved in US trades. The **Civil War** added "war-risk" premiums on American hulls, favouring British/colonial ships.
- **Declining domestic returns pushed capital seaward.** Timber and fish trades hit limits to extensive growth; New Brunswick's **terms of trade turned sharply negative** (index 1.3 in 1824–40 → 0.86 in the 1860s); Nova Scotia ran growing trade deficits. Ships were a **highly mobile transport service** that could be diverted from faltering local staples into lucrative foreign carrying trades.
- **A fateful structural shift: shipper vs. shipowner.** As vessels worked on **charters as general cargo carriers**, the shipowner's interest (high freight rates) **diverged** from the shipper's (low freight costs) — a separation that "scarcely existed before 1850" and would prove decisive. Operating in distant markets also required the **telegraph and access to brokers/market information**, concentrating business in merchant hands. By 1863–1914 most voyages began in Britain/Europe/US, _not_ BNA — the industry had moved beyond its staple base into the **North Atlantic triangle**, loosening its links to the regional economy even at its peak.

**The decline (post-1879).**

- Ocean-going tonnage in the eight fleets fell **~6.1%/yr** 1879–99; coastal (<250 ton) fell only ~1.9%/yr. By the 1900s the regional fleet was smaller than in the 1840s.
- **The decisive negative finding:** the coincidence between declining freight rates and declining investment is **weak**. Freight rates fell ~2–3%/yr from the 1873 peak — serious, but nowhere near enough to explain a 6%/yr tonnage decline or a **15.5%/yr** collapse in new investment in large vessels. Costs were being cut (improving productivity), which should have cushioned returns. Crucially, **world tonnage grew ~56% (1870–1900)** and world sailing capacity was _greater_ in 1910 than 1850; **Norwegians expanded** their sailing fleets in the 1880s, partly buying used Canadian ships. The Maritime decline was thus an **anomaly** out of step with every major maritime nation (possibly excepting the US).
- **The real question reframed:** if at some point profits required new technology, _why did Maritimers fail to make the transition_ when Norwegians (similar industry) did? The golden age was "an outgrowth of the previous experience of merchant capitalists" within a domestic opportunity structure they controlled — and so was its decline. The fleet was doomed **"by Maritimers themselves,"** not by impersonal market forces.

## Chapter 6 — Capital, Labour, and Profits (were ships profitable? and how owners squeezed productivity)

A methodological and empirical chapter on **profit** — its definition and measurement — followed by evidence of intense cost-cutting.

- **What is "profit"?** Sager carefully distinguishes gross/net profit, **opportunity cost**, **time preference**, and non-pecuniary rewards (the _William D. Lawrence_ as object of prestige/beauty). He adopts the **rate of return** (net profit after depreciation ÷ capital employed) as the workable measure that also approximates how owners themselves saw their business, and he reads it as a _subset of Marx's surplus value_ rather than a return to capital alone. He flags the danger of **circular argument** (assuming high profits where investment went) and stresses that **imperfect information** may decouple investment from actual returns — a thread that pays off in Chapter 8.
- **Two methods of estimating returns:** (1) reconstructing **gross revenue** from UK **bills of entry** (vessel-by-vessel cargoes for Saint John/Halifax/Yarmouth/Windsor ships entering Liverpool/London in 1863, 1873, 1883) × current freight rates (deals, cotton, grain, petroleum ≈ 65% of entrances); minus operating costs (Killam's ~$14.50–15.50/ton; others $12–14/ton) and **depreciation** (~7% to 1880, ~9% after). (2) **Actual dividends** on six Nova Scotia vessels (Appendix C).
- **Findings:** shipping was **very profitable** in the boom. Most owners could amortize a vessel's purchase price in **5–6 years** and earn clear profit thereafter (vessels lasting ~12+ years). Estimated returns: comfortably above railway stock (~8%) in 1863; **~20%+** in 1873; falling to roughly 10–20% by 1883 and 9–18% by the 1886 low. The six-vessel sample averaged **25.9%** before 1880 and **14%** after (with huge variance, including near-zero years in the 1880s). Contemporaries confirmed it (Peter Mitchell; Edward Willis: shipping made "comfortable competencies, if not colossal fortunes"). The crucial perception in the 1880s was the **contrast** with the early 1870s — profit was still possible, but the _rate of profit had fallen steeply_ (Martin Dickie: "almost sick of it"; Robert Quirk the irascible Charlottetown owner).
- **The productivity squeeze.** Facing falling returns, owners "wrested productivity gains from ships and labour":
    - **Turn-around times** fell ~6 days between the 1860s and 1880s (then partly lost in the 1890s as steamers got port priority).
    - **Passage times** shortened on nearly every route (e.g., NY–Liverpool −6 days; UK–Rio −11 days); the average vessel could make 3 transatlantic voyages/year by the 1880s vs. fewer in the 1860s — adding ~10–15% to lifetime gross earnings and offsetting the freight-rate fall.
    - **Longer voyages** (South America, Asia, Australia) raised the sea-time : port-time ratio.
    - **Capital–labour ratio pushed to the limit:** man-ton ratios fell ~2%/yr 1863–1900; Maritime ships sailed with **fewer men** than British ships of the same class. **Wages fell ~20%** in the Saint John fleet (1874–86), more in Windsor; pressure was heaviest on able seamen.
    - This intensified **class conflict** — rising **desertion rates**, resistance at sea, prosecutions on land — with owners dependent on a wage-paid master beyond direct supervision.
- **The conclusion that sets up the rest of the book:** owners had reached the **limits of wood-and-sail technology**. The logical next step — invest in **iron/steel hulls and steam** — was well known to them. When Maritime capital **declined to take that step**, it did so "in full knowledge of those lessons and for reasons firmly rooted in the history and experience of merchant capital in their region." Declining freights and steam competition were **necessary but not sufficient** conditions; they only _narrowed the choices._

## Chapter 7 — Merchant Shipowners in the Industrial Era (the incomplete transition; capital pulled landward)

Examines _who_ made the fatal decisions and shows that capital was **pulled** out of shipping, not merely pushed.

- **An incomplete transition to industrial capitalism.** Had shipping industrialized like other sectors, most tonnage by WWI would have sat in a few incorporated companies. Instead the **merchant family firm remained dominant** to the end of the century; not until **1907** did the incorporated company account for most (new) capital formation in a now-declining industry.
- **Two ownership trends, 1850–1900:** (1) **small owner-operators displaced** (recovering their share only _after_ the collapse) — even in schooners and coasting; (2) rising investment by "shipbuilders" and "shipowners," but this **did not end merchant control** (merchants held ~30% of new tonnage in the 1880s–90s; merchants + "shipowners" together a majority). Joint-stock/incorporated ownership dominated only among **steam** investors (~54% of steamship tonnage; William Thomson of Saint John prominent). Only in **Yarmouth** did a true specialist "shipowner" class emerge — and even they came from mercantile families.
- **The central evidence: diversification preceded decline.** Using **company-incorporation statutes** as a proxy for investment timing: in Nova Scotia (467 incorporations, 1850–89), **41% involved at least one major shipowner**, and ~63% of major shipowners took part in some incorporation; **two-thirds of incorporations before 1870 involved shipowners.** So merchant-shipowners **"cut that path themselves... at the height of the age of sail"** — capital was _pulled_ landward by opportunity, not just _pushed_ by falling returns after 1879.
- **Where the capital went:** primarily **finance, merchandising, utilities, and social infrastructure**, _less_ into secondary manufacturing. Examples: Yarmouth (Bank of Yarmouth, railways, iron co.); Windsor (Bennett Smith, E.W. Dimock — banks, insurance, cotton mill, utilities); Halifax (banks, insurance, gas/electric, coal, railways). The Saint John incorporation table shows interest swinging after 1860 toward **public service/utilities (15.5%→31.5%)** and railways, away from manufacturing and finance. **Major shipowners were _less_ likely than minor ones to start new manufacturing** (only ~10% of major Saint John owners' incorporating activity was manufacturing).
- **The portfolio mentality.** Vivid cases (Frederick A. Wiggins — millionaire whose ships were <⅓ of assets; William A. Robertson; Archibald F. Randolph — "too widespread"; the Troops, atypically ship-heavy). Probate records: at death, major shipowners held **few or no ships**; ~⅔ of assets were cash/stocks/bonds, ~20% real estate. Ships had become **rentier capital** — "one item, along with municipal bonds and bank stock" — easily liquidated. **"The age of sail came to an end in a large number of relatively minor decisions by merchant investors."**

## Chapter 8 — A Culture of Entrepôt Growth (information, ideology, and the politics of decline)

The most original chapter: it argues that **information and culture**, not just costs, drove disinvestment — and that the **state/policy** failure was rooted in merchant ideology.

- **Information as an independent variable.** Neo-classical theory's "survival of the fittest" is a near-tautology (the conclusion follows from assumptions). Under **pervasive uncertainty** investors can rationally withdraw from a _high-return_ industry — exactly what the **US merchant marine** did in the 1860s–70s. So even if 1880s profits had been higher, the industry might still have declined. **Control of information was economic power** (e.g., the Truro Condensed Milk directors suppressing their dividend statement to deter competition; the Yarmouth campaign to copy Massachusetts cotton mills).
- **The puzzle of premature pessimism.** Merchant-shipowners "knew" the industry was doomed _before_ the profit collapse. Frank Killam (1876) called depression "universal" while returns topped 15%. **Thomas Kenny** repeatedly declared the industry destroyed by iron/steam and even admitted **iron sailing ships were more profitable** — yet **never bought them** and blamed the inability to _build_ them in Canada (ignoring that they were being built elsewhere in Canada). Their pessimism contradicted their own analysis.
- **The source: a "mind's eye" turned west.** A.H. Gillmor's insight: Confederation and railways fixed attention on the **western prairie**, not the Atlantic. The development model was **"entrepôt growth"** (Rosemary Langhout) — concentrating trade at favoured ports — in which expanding _shipowning_ was at best an ancillary benefit, not a goal. The word **"shipping" became ambiguous**: it came to mean the _shipper's_ business (export/import of goods) rather than the _shipowner's_ (carrying goods in ships).
- **The press confirms the culture.** Content analysis (Yarmouth Herald 1867; Saint John Globe) shows the world's 4th-largest merchant marine was nearly invisible in its own newspapers — more coverage of _US_ shipbuilding's fate than local shipowning; uniformly pessimistic; calls for **subsidies to attract (foreign-owned) steamships** to local ports, heedless that those steamers would undercut local owners. One Saint John writer even declared waterborne transport becoming obsolete (factually wrong even for coastal trade). External development models were **borrowed wholesale**: railways, cotton, iron, sugar were "the bedrock."
- **Shipper interest trumps shipowner interest (the steamship-subsidy fight).** Maritimers would _not_ unite to protect shipowning. The debate over steamship subsidies pitted Maritimer against Maritimer: A.G. Jones / William Welsh opposed subsidies that would aid competitors; **Thomas Kenny** (a sailing-ship owner!) argued _for_ steam subsidies to grow the **export trade** — because the underlying logic was always merchant capital's "buy cheap, sell dear." (Owners even opposed West Indies steamers partly to avoid **glutting fish markets** and lowering prices.) The shipowner was a **trader first.**
- **The state-policy failure.** Effective protection (cargo reservation, preferential port charges, flag subsidies) was _outside_ the merchant capitalist's perceived self-interest because it would **raise freight costs** and entail managing mobile wage labour. Hence the striking **silence before the 1904 Royal Commission on Transportation** — Maritimers lobbied for **harbours, "winter port" status, grain elevators, banana cargoes**, not for a merchant marine (it took the Toronto chairman, John Bertram, to raise shipbuilding bounties). Later, belated lobbying for shipbuilding bounties (J.F. Stairs, Thomas Cantley) was "too little and too late," sporadic, and undermined by structural obstacles:
    - Finance Minister **W.S. Fielding's paradox:** British ships entered Canada duty-free, but the _materials_ to build them in Canada were dutiable — and **Canada "had not power to tax British ships"** (acceptance of British maritime law and registry made a duty unenforceable). Real protection required **independence from imperial jurisdiction**, which Maritimers would not demand.
    - Duties protected the **domestic iron/steel** industry, which had no plate mill and preferred railway demand; a single shipyard offered marginal demand. The 1901–2 **Harry Crowe / Swan & Hunter** Halifax shipyard project collapsed for want of capital + bounty commitment.
- **The coasting-trade debate.** Maritimers were _surprised_ to learn (c. 1906) that **Canadian coasting was largely unprotected** — opened to foreigners not by imperial treaty (Fielding was wrong) but by revocable **orders in council** from the 1870s (US excluded for lack of reciprocity). Even so, when **Norwegian steamers** took the Cape Breton coal trade, shippers (Nova Scotia Steel/Coal, via E.M. Macdonald, Alexander Johnston, Thomas Cantley) **defended foreign ships** to keep freight cheap, over the protests of schooner owners. Again, **shipper interest prevailed.**
- **Synthesis.** A century earlier, an integrated mercantilist policy united builders, owners, shippers, and staple exporters. By 1900, capital was **fragmented** by "disarticulated development" and an incomplete merchant→industrial transition: iron makers, shipowners, and shippers were **rival urban elites**, "not part of a coherent regional or national bourgeoisie." Coal/iron/steel led _away from_, not toward, saving the marine industries.

## Chapter 9 — Maritime Capital and Economic Development (the big "did it matter?" question)

Weighs the industry's contribution to the regional economy and to (under)development — between **Innis's nostalgia** ("the linchpin was broken") and **McClelland's dismissal** ("a mere ripple upon the existing economy").

- **The two logical traps.** Innis: if shipbuilding was obsolete, how could its loss be a fatal half-century blow? McClelland: if the industries were unproductive/unattractive, why invest in them so long? Acheson's "inestimable" middle ground sidesteps the hard questions. Sager reframes: don't only ask _why growth was slower than central Canada's_ — ask **what form** industrialization took and **in what conditions** the merchant→industrial transition occurred.
- **The industries were large, not a "ripple."** Despite imported inputs and credit (the enclave character — PEI shipbuilding imports >¼ of all imports in 1826; Liverpool partners like Gibbs, Bright & Co. supplying the Wards; James Duncan owing a Liverpool partner >$119,000), the local **linkages and contribution were substantial**:
    - **Balance of payments / provincial income:** vessel exports likely turned PEI's visible trade balance _positive_; by the 1850s ships added ~⅓ to New Brunswick's real export value; shipowning earnings brought "gold... to pay for our heavy imports." Shipping was **second only to timber** in NB trade.
    - **Employment/wages:** census undercounts (only those at work on enumeration day). Shipbuilding likely ~**12% of NB industrial wages** (1870); in 1863–4 perhaps ~$1–1.6M in NB shipbuilding wages (vs. $3M forest-product exports). Shipbuilding likely ≥**5% of provincial product** (>10% in peak years like 1863–4) — bigger, proportionally, than wheat's ~3% of GNP in the 1900s. NB's ocean fleet earned gross revenues ~9–11% of GPP and perhaps **a quarter of all provincial profits** in the early 1870s.
- **But it did _not_ drive a transition to industrial capitalism — and may have impeded it.** Using neo-classical _and_ Marxist measures (Phillip Wood's framework):
    - **Wages** in shipbuilding were _higher_ than in other industries (so it wasn't the source of low per-capita incomes), and **value added per worker/per capital was relatively high** — shipbuilding was _not_ unproductive; capital that left it often moved into _less_ productive industries.
    - **But low capital intensity:** capital–labour ratio far below other industries (Ontario shipyards by 1890 were ~6× higher and building steamships). A **high share of value went to labour** (skilled craftsmen — caulkers struck for months in 1864), so the **rate of surplus value was low** (Table 9.4) even when the rate of profit was respectable. Skilled, mobile, organized labour was hard to displace; the **easy escape was to invest elsewhere.**
    - **Weak forward/backward linkages.** Most **metal inputs (~20% of a ship's cost) were imported** (low tariffs, merchant ties to British/US suppliers, cheap inward freight from spare tonnage). For every $1 of ship materials _made_ in the region, ~$1.40 was _imported_. This deepened a **"disarticulated" economy** with high import dependence (Maritime per-capita manufactured imports ~double Ontario's).
    - **Stabilizing pre-capitalism.** Timber (~25% of a ship's cost) linked shipbuilding to **occupational pluralism** — farmers cutting knees/timbers in winter as "money found." Crucially, by employing seasonal labour, supplementing farm/fishing incomes, and (via spare inward tonnage) keeping imported food cheap, shipping/shipbuilding **propped up small-scale, labour-intensive farming and fishing and independent commodity production** — working _against_ the formation of a **capitalist labour market.** The huge transport system also "explodes the myth of outport isolation," lowering the cost of dispersed settlement and thereby **retarding urbanization and concentrated labour markets** (in 1880 Arichat/Barrington/Shelburne were greater ocean ports than Montreal).
- **Timing.** In **1850** Maritime industry was as developed (per capita) as Upper Canada's; through the 1850s–60s Ontario surged into railways/foundries/machine shops while Maritimers kept building wooden fleets, so that by **1870 Ontario's per-capita output was ~60% higher.** Slow capitalist development "began in the third quarter... at the height of the age of sail."
- **The counterfactual (a steel-ship industry).** Sager rejects econometric "what-ifs" as romance: projecting a steel industry assumes a _different society_ (different political priorities to produce the bounty). His sober estimate: by 1890–1914 **freight factors had fallen too far** (grain's ocean freight cost was small relative to the rail leg) for new tonnage to benefit shippers; **forward linkages would be small** (only ~9% of the 1893 New Glasgow steel ship _Mulgrave's_ cost was imported steel; British practice put ~30% of steel output into ships but Canadian yards imported much); the main benefit would be **skilled employment** (the 1901 Halifax project hoped for 1,000 jobs). Capturing ⅓ of the 1918–20 Canadian government shipbuilding program would have made Nova Scotia's manufacturing base only **~4% larger** — an industry needing perpetual subsidy and lobbying, vulnerable to severe cyclical swings.
- **Bottom line.** The price gap between British and Canadian tonnage (a ~$30/ton bounty needed in 1913–14, >$90/ton by 1919–20) was the **symptom, not the cause**, observed after decades of decline. **A large national merchant marine was simply not in the interest of Canadian industrial capital.** The merchant marine's absence is an "anomaly" only if one rejects the logic of industrial capital. Sager closes by gesturing to alternative development models (transportation as part of the social product, controlled by producers) that Canada "forswore" — "we learn what we are and what we have lost."

## Chapter 10 — Postscript: A Comparative Perspective (why Atlantic Canada was the exception)

A deliberately **tentative comparison** (postscript, not conclusion) that throws the Maritime anomaly into relief.

- **The anomaly quantified (Table 10.1, 1880–1900 tonnage growth):** World **+1.36%/yr**; UK +1.74; Germany +2.77; Spain +3.62; **Japan +11.95**; Norway +0.30; Sweden +1.37. **United States −2.40; Canada −3.38; the Maritime provinces ~−5%.** British Columbia, by contrast, **+12.3%.** Only the US shared a significant decline — and after 1900 even the US decline was arrested while the Maritimes kept collapsing.
- **No single path to growth** — and **shipowning did not require domestic shipbuilding** (Sweden grew by buying _second-hand British steam tonnage_; Finland kept shipowning without rapid steam conversion). This further undercuts any "we couldn't build steamers, so we were doomed" claim.
- **Britain — a non-reproducible model.** A century earlier British shipowning had already seen what Atlantic Canada never did: **shipowning as a distinct, concentrated, professionally managed occupation** separated from the merchant; then **liner companies** dominating trades by the 1870s — powerful actors on shipbuilders and government. Plus unique structural advantages: an **industrializing island at the heart of world trade**, huge home/colonial/European trades, and massive outward **coal** freights (Robin Craig). Nothing comparable existed in Canada.
- **The minimal conditions for growth (Sager's synthesis):** **close integration of shipping capital with (a) commercial capital in major overseas trades and (b) state policy.** Present in essentially every growing maritime nation in Table 10.1; **conspicuously absent in British North America**, where a "nascent bourgeoisie was fragmented along regional lines" and Maritime maritime capital was poorly linked to central/western Canadian capital.
- **Japan — the polar contrast.** State + great commercial firms treated shipping as a **"strategic industry"** with "huge subsidies." The mercantile _seishō_ of the 1870s became the industrial _zaibatsu_ of the 1890s; the state gave **Mitsubishi/NYK** its first steamers, restricted competition, and pushed toward monopoly. Because the big firm was _both_ shipper and shipowner (and aligned with the state), the **shipper–shipowner conflict dissolved**: low freight rates made exports competitive and fed cargo back to the line. **Every condition present in Japan was missing in Canada.**
- **State support was the norm, not the exception:** Spain (naval/colonial policy → concentrated modern yards); **France** (1881 law: per-ton-per-1,000-mile bounties + shipbuilding subsidies to erase the cost gap with England); even **Britain** (postal subsidies, Admiralty contracts, troop transports, maritime law). Canada _did_ pay mail/steamship subventions, but they were **small, not reserved for Canadian vessels, and aimed at promoting trade and serving railway-less coastal communities — not at building a merchant marine.**
- **Beyond subsidy: culture and consensus.** Canada lacked a navy (and the shipbuilding stimulus one brings), and lacked any **nationalist maritime consensus** — no Japanese autarky, no French rhetoric of "tribute" paid to foreign carriers, no German "maritime enthusiasm" linking shipowners and exporters. **Scandinavia** offers the closest parallel _with the decisive differences_: shipping was **integrated into the local economy**, a much larger share of output, with no imperial motherland from which to borrow law and ships; the British steam lead inspired **emulation rather than fatalism**; and there was "limited scope for alternative investments" — the opposite of a Maritimes pulled toward a vast continental hinterland.
- **Final note (echoing David Alexander):** it was neither to the region's credit that a great merchant marine arose nor to its discredit that it vanished. The conditions were **transplanted from across the Atlantic and did not survive integration into North American industrial capitalism.** "There was little that Maritime Canadians could have done, by themselves, to preserve a merchant marine." In a framework of **regionally fragmented development, capital and the state together built a great coastal nation with a small merchant navy** — and the working people most affected "were not consulted."

## Recurring Concepts / Glossary (useful for revision)

- **Maritime capital** — not a separate mode of production but a _commercial capitalism_ oriented to overseas trade, marine transport, fishing, and other seaward activity; shipping as a branch of **merchant capital**.
- **Merchant vs. industrial capital** — merchant capital profits from **exchange** (controlling markets/prices) without controlling the labour process; its independent dominance is _inversely_ related to general economic development.
- **Enclave industry** — established largely by external (British) investors, with inputs and outputs exchanged in markets outside the region (early-century shipbuilding).
- **Entrepôt growth** — a development strategy concentrating trade at favoured ports; it valued the _shipper's_ business and port development over the _shipowner's_.
- **Shipper vs. shipowner** — after ~1850 these interests diverged (low freight costs vs. high freight rates); their split is central to the decline.
- **Disarticulated development** — juxtaposed but poorly integrated units of production, with a high ratio of external to internal exchanges; high import dependence.
- **Occupational pluralism / petty commodity production** — outport households mixing farming, fishing, lumbering, and seafaring; the schooner as "smallholding"; reinforced by cheap shipping, working _against_ a capitalist labour market.
- **Necessary vs. sufficient conditions** — falling freight rates and steam/iron competition were _necessary_ (they narrowed choices) but _not sufficient_; the sufficient conditions lay in the region's merchant-capital social structure and its integration into Confederation, which made a regional merchant marine **redundant**.
