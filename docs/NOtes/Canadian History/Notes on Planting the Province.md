# Gap-Filling Notes (supplement to the above)

> Covers the parts of McCalla's argument my notes above don't yet touch — Part One, forest products, agriculture, manufacturing, transport, the business/banking system, government, the chronology of fluctuations, railways, the mid-century — plus a short section flagging two of my own claims above that McCalla would push back on.

## Sharpening the thesis I already have

- The book's positive claim is **balance**, not "the internal economy was the prime mover." McCalla's point is that there is _no unique prime mover at all_: technology, forests, non-wheat farm exports, town growth, etc. were "simultaneous, mutually interconnected, reciprocal processes," and singling out any one (including local exchange) "is achieved only at an unreasonable level of abstraction." The enemy is monocausality, not exports specifically.
- He runs everything through **extensive growth** (output rises mainly by adding new people + new land; GNP rises fast, GNP/capita slowly) vs **intensive growth** (urbanization, industrialization, rising output per head). 1784–~1851 is overwhelmingly extensive; the mid-century is the hinge. Most "what was lacking" critiques judge an extensive-growth economy by intensive-growth standards.

## Part One: the founding decades

### Ch. 2 — The Loyalist Economy, 1784–1805

- ~6,000 Loyalists arrived 1784–5. Population then grew **>10%/yr** for twenty years — ~4× what natural increase allows, so **migration-driven, mostly from the United States**. People came on _information from those already there_ as much as on free-land proclamations.
- **Big nuance to "internal economy as driver":** **British government spending exceeded the value of provincial exports** until at least 1803, and total sterling from British spending exceeded export earnings until _after_ the War of 1812. Sources: Loyalist loss claims (£100k–160k, 1789–95), the annual civil grant, Indian Department salaries, and above all the **garrison** (~1,000 soldiers + provisions + officer pay).
- **The fur trade's real significance was the commercial/credit system it sustained** — the Montreal supply network, lake shipping, and the credit the farm economy plugged into all came from it. Merchants (Hamilton, Cartwright, Quetton St George) built their credit on fur/military supply, then pivoted to supplying settlers.
- **Wheat was a small earner per household**: exports <20% of the crop in 1803; ~£2.5–£4 export income per household. Pork was a genuine _second_ income source. A wide local market (fairs, taverns, hides, hay, teaming) existed from the start.
- **Native peoples were not dependent.** Six Nations got ~700,000 acres on the Grand River and behaved partly as land speculators; cross-border movement continued; a traditional woodland economy persisted — "considerable autonomy."
- By 1800 the enduring structure was set: ~7%/yr growth, ~3.9 improved acres/capita, regional specialization beginning, fluctuations tracking outside markets, and active **capital creation** (185+ mills in six districts by 1805; growing lake fleet). Not a capital-starved subsistence economy.

### Ch. 3 — The War Economy and After, 1806–1822

- **The War of 1812 "prosperity" was partly inflation mistaken for prosperity.** McCalla: "Canadians (and some subsequent historians) tended to mistake inflation for prosperity." Much British spending flowed straight back out (£630,000 just on Quebec→Upper Canada freight, 1812–15).
- **Army bills** = paper currency backed by the British government, authorized by Lower Canada's legislature (>£3.4M issued). Gave the economy _unprecedented liquidity_ and shifted payment from kind to cash — but inflationary, and their **withdrawal after the war caused the deflation**.
- **Severe deflation 1815–22**, bottoming 1822 (Lake Ontario wheat fell to a quarter of its 1814 value). This + inability to extract customs from Lower Canada bankrupted the provincial government and pushed it to back the **Bank of Upper Canada** (25% of shares) to protect public credit. The Welland Canal idea also came out of this distress.
- Migration source flips: from ~1817 the **UK becomes the main source** of immigrants. Corn Laws meant **colonial wheat often couldn't legally enter Britain** (barred when British prices were low, 1821–3), so wheat was a staple _crop_ but frequently not a staple _export_ before the late 1820s — Lower Canada was the reliable market.

## Part Two: the analytical core

### Ch. 4 — Forest Products, 1822–46

- **Forests probably earned at least as much as wheat as an export — likely ≥half of all export earnings 1815–40.**
- Not really one staple: square red & white pine, oak, elm, deals, boards, **and pot/pearl ashes** — the latter the _leading St Lawrence forest export by value_. Ash and stave exports predate the Napoleonic timber duties, undercutting "the duties created the trade."
- **Two different forest economies.** The _upper Ottawa_ was a genuinely **dependent single-staple pine economy** (the one place the staple model fits) and leaned on **migrant Lower-Canadian (French) labour** whose wages mostly went home, _out_ of Upper Canada. The _St Lawrence/Great Lakes_ zone was mixed forest-and-farm: staves, ashes, oak as **winter work integrated with farming**, avoiding dependency.
- **Freight factor:** up to ~half of recorded export value was really _transport_ income going to a small group of shippers/sailors, not producers.
- **Two famous claims debunked:**
    1. _Employment was wildly exaggerated_ — the "7,000–8,000 raftsmen on the Ottawa in 1835" figure is propaganda for the duties/river works; reconstruction gives ~1,500.
    2. _The timber→immigration link is weak_ — cheap-westbound-fares-on-timber-ships (A.R.M. Lower) doesn't hold: timber volumes and emigration correlate poorly; New Brunswick had the same cheap freight but grew half as fast; US-bound emigration rose without any timber preference. At most a _necessary, not sufficient_ — probably not even necessary — condition.

### Ch. 5 — The Structure of Agriculture, 1821–51

- **Upper Canada was _always_ a mixed-farming system**, not wheat-monoculture later "shifting to cheese." Farmers cleared far more land than wheat needed.
- Modelled out, **wheat was only ~12–21% of provincial income** even near the mid-century peak; a hypothetical 50% wheat-income collapse would cut real provincial income only ~6%. Not "highly vulnerable" the way a one-crop region is.
- **Country-store ledgers** (13 stores, 1791–1842): payment in cash, notes, wheat, pork, ashes, lumber, rye, tobacco, barley, butter, cheese, and **third-party credits**. Wheat dominated only in the Lake Ontario core.
- McInnis (1860–1): ~1/6 of farms had _no_ marketable surplus; the _median_ surplus fed only ~one other household (~$150). The self-sufficiency-vs-commercialization distinction "is not very important."
- Key move: farmers were **"economically alert"** and market-engaged _from the beginning_; later intensification isn't proof markets were absent earlier. The real constraint on local selling was that _everyone produced the same things into a non-farm sector growing only as fast as agriculture._
- **Women's dairy/poultry/garden work** was real and under-recorded. Indigenous reserve farming was sometimes comparable to neighbouring settlers — natives "at the margin of society" but not necessarily _materially_ marginalized.

### Ch. 6 — Artisans, Manufacturing, to 1851

- Counters the "import-dependent, capital-poor, no industry" image: a **real indigenous manufacturing base existed and was the foundation for later industrialization.**
- Mills were the largest private building projects locally (£500–£3,000), increasingly fitted with _domestically made_ castings. **Distilling** boomed as wheat prices fell. **Foundries** (Normandale; Fisher & McQuesten in Hamilton making ploughs/threshers) show industry could grow on **working capital + credit, not huge fixed capital**. **Printing/newspapers** concentrated early in Toronto; **brewing** was an early urban industry.
- Quantified: ~40,000 craft workers in 1851 earned ~£1.6M — _almost exactly_ McCallum's estimate of total wheat income that year. Industry was rural, diffuse, seasonal, integrated with farming.

### Ch. 7 — Transportation and Communications, 1800–50

- System "known more for what it lacked than for what it had," judged by travellers wanting year-round reliability. For an _extensive-growth_ economy it was largely adequate.
- **Private water transport responded well to demand**; **steam adopted early** (_Frontenac_, 1816, among the first on the Lakes). Slow 1840s steam-fleet growth reflected **demand, not capital scarcity** — a vessel could earn its build cost in one year of freights.
- **Roads/toll-roads couldn't pay their way**, yet the whole high-growth era happened while land travel barely beat walking pace. "Bad roads" did not constrain growth; demand never warranted big outlays.
- **Regional communications orientation** (via Goheen) = the source of my "Lower Canada↔Britain, Upper Canada↔US" note: Quebec closer to Britain, Toronto relied more on the US than on Quebec, the two poorly linked.
- By 1851 the province's enduring **urban map** was already set (Toronto, Hamilton, Bytown/Ottawa, London the long-run leaders; Kingston declining) — _before_ railway/telegraph reshaped it.

### Ch. 8 — The Provincial Business System (banking lives here)

- **Merchants were simultaneously the biggest creditors and biggest debtors**; their main asset was _debts owed to them_. So commercial investment was **long-term, not "short-term and anti-industrial"** (12-month+ credit couldn't be liquidated quickly).
- **Rejects the "exploitative merchant / unequal exchange" thesis:** competition among merchants, farmer mobility, and the merchant's interest in customer _success_ all cut against it.
- **Chronic liquidity problem:** cash (specie + notes) was scarce — only ~10% of store credits came in cash. Local exchange ran on **mutual credit and periodic "setting off"** (the McMartin/McNaught example: £6 settled £100 of dealings over 7 years). Deep version of my "credit was a key instrument" note.
- Credit was mainly a **risk-management tool**, not a trap.
- **Banking detail I compressed:** a charter was "literally a licence to print money," and **investors were often the principal borrowers** (the failed Kingston "Pretended" Bank; Gore Bank directors). York-controlled **Bank of Upper Canada** (1822) was the first chartered bank. **1837 suspension** of specie payments; Sydenham's 1841 government-bank-of-issue plan **defeated** by united bank resistance. Also emerging: insurance, savings banks, **building societies** (→ later mortgage giants).

### Ch. 9 — Government and Development, 1820–41

- **Major correction to "internal economy was the main driver":** government money was _mostly British, not local_. Across 1821–40, **British spending + British capital borrowed brought in ≥£4.5M sterling** — roughly equal to _timber_ exports and _far more_ than wheat. Includes the **Rideau Canal** (£830,000 stg, a _military_ work), garrison spending, Ordnance works, Canada Company payments, and a post-rebellion surge (~£3.5M in 1838–40) that restored liquidity.
- The province's _own_ revenues were modest (~7–8s/capita); routine spending wasn't that of an "active, intrusive state." Revenue: share of Lower Canada's customs (Canada Trade Act, 1822), crown-land revenue, Canada Company, timber duties, US-trade customs, liquor taxes, municipal property tax.
- **Public-works debt = my "great waterway projects on London credit" point.** Debt rose ~34× from 1825–37, almost all for public works; consolidated into **London-payable securities (1834)**. The **Welland Canal couldn't even pay its interest**; the **St Lawrence canals** (£352k) were "of questionable value as a transport system" (the Rideau already existed) — "only politics, easy credit, and the largely unquestioned strategic assumption" explain them.
- But: **Upper Canada borrowed/built much like adjacent US states** (Ohio, Indiana, etc. took on far more). Parallel population growth across the lakes implies **no uniquely necessary role for the tory state** in _timing/scale_ of growth.
- The state's _real_ contributions were **non-fiscal**: land disposal, law (esp. property rights), and **defence** — the last being why Upper Canada resembled but wasn't identical to the neighbouring states.

### Ch. 10 — The Chronology of Development, 1821–51

- **Booms/busts followed the general North Atlantic cycle, not the wheat market** (peaks 1825/1836/1845–7; crises 1837 & 1847; troughs 1819/1842/1848). Even locally-consumed-goods prices moved with the wider universe.
- **Emigration was an _investment_ process, not refugee flight** (1847 famine the exception). Three waves: post-war→mid-1820s; 1829–37; 1840–54 (peak 1847).
- **The 1837 rebellion was essentially a liquidity crisis, not structural collapse** (fast 1838 recovery on British military spending). Economically the rebels were _representative_ of rural Upper Canada; what set them apart was politics/religion/ethnicity/years-in-country — the **"autonomy of politics"** from reductionist economics. Debtor–creditor lines were often _intra_-class (e.g. Tory speculator Allan MacNab was exactly the kind of debtor a credit freeze hit).

## Part Three: the mid-century hinge

### Ch. 11 — Railways, 1850–70

- Canada's railway "lag" looks different treating **Upper Canada as a _western_ region (Ohio/Michigan/Indiana), not eastern.** The _will_ existed in the 1840s; the constraint was **financing** (London capital, US state defaults 1839–43, the English railway-stock crash).
- Government enabled it (1849 Guarantee Act, 1851 Main Trunk Act, 1852 Municipal Loan Fund) + heavy municipal boosterism/promoter manipulation. **Great Western** was the success; the **Grand Trunk** was over-scaled and over-capitalized.
- **Nuance to my "waterways not very useful vs the railroad":** McCalla sees rail and water as _complementary/competitive_, not simple railroad triumph. **Lake shipping grew ~5× between 1849 and 1874.** Railways' edge wasn't mainly speed (for freight) but **reliability** — fewer transshipments, less risk, year-round movement.
- The **construction boom was short-term and inflationary** (transient labour, imported inputs, funds leaking to metropolitan centres) — "almost entirely ephemeral" had railways not mattered long-term as transport. Lasting effects: lower transport costs, market integration, reinforcing the leading cities.

### Ch. 12 — The Economy at Mid-Century, 1851–70

- **Extensive growth ends ~1854–56, when net immigration stopped.** Growth: ~7%/yr earlier → **~4%/yr 1851–61 → ~1.5%/yr 1861–71**, with net emigration every decade 1861–1901. Sex ratio falls toward 103:100; the **fertility transition** begins. (Backbone of my migration note.)
- **Transiency/persistence:** huge churn — in Peel County only ~39% of 1851 householders remained in 1861; ~30–35% persistence in Hamilton. "The complex product of persistence and transience"; early-settled property owners persisted most. (The "lots in, lots out" point, with numbers.)
- **"Wheat→cheese" is too simple.** Mid-century was the _peak_ of wheat exports (8M+ bushels most years 1852–69), yet the farmer share of the workforce _rose_ in the 1860s, and animals/animal products became major exports. Cheese came after ~1870 and never rivalled butter/milk/beef.
- **Timber→lumber was an _addition_, not a replacement** — square-timber exports stayed high (peaking 1861–3) as the US plank-and-board trade exploded.
- **Service/tertiary sector grew faster than manufacturing** and was even more urban — my "service sector was a major part of cities" note. Leading "industries" by output in 1870 were still primary (flour, lumber); by value-added, secondary iron & steel led.
- **Banking structure** (my "oligarchical + local banks" note): a _few powerful multi-branch chartered banks_ clustered in big centres (Toronto 9 banks by 1871; Hamilton/London/Ottawa 5; Kingston 3), branches in ~45 towns; smaller "bankless" places relied on private/local banks. So "a few oligarchical chartered banks + many smaller/local lenders" is fair.
- Industrialization was real but **uneven/"modulated"** — factory-scale in Toronto/Hamilton and the big saw mills, but most production still owner-operated, small, seasonal, and **water-power was still as common as steam.** Province "in step with technology, not a lagging follower."

### Ch. 13 — Conclusion

- The three "political" turning points — **Reciprocity (1855–66), Crimean War (1854–56), American Civil War (1861–65)** — are **misleading as causes.** The trends began _before_ and continued _after_ them.
- Governing metaphor: development came not in **"cuts" but in "fades"** — or **layers, new added to (not replacing) old**; change was "modulated, balanced, and cumulative."
- Keep these lines:
    - **"The essence of the rural economy was its balance, not its specialization."**
    - Output relied on **human capital — skills/institutions/effort — more than on simple resource extraction.**
    - **"If the provincial economy must be summarized in terms of a single, pre-eminent product, the farms themselves were its chief accomplishment."** (The anti-staple punchline: the "staple" was the _farm itself_.)

## Two things to revisit in my notes above

1. **"Imperial economic policy… was more regional whereas the internal economy was the main driver."** Half right, but it understates Britain. British state money (military + Rideau Canal + Canada Company + borrowed London capital) ≈ _timber_ export value and _far exceeded wheat_ across 1821–40, and at the founding exceeded _all_ exports. McCalla's actual claim is stronger: **no single prime mover — staples, imperial policy, _or_ internal economy.** "Internal economy as _the_ driver" is itself a mild version of the monocausal move he's attacking.
    
2. **"Banks overlending paper money led to one pretty bad run… so never again."** McCalla argues nearly the _opposite_. The 1837 specie suspension was a **liquidity crisis driven by the international cycle**, not reckless overissue — he finds note circulation was **"not out of line with the needs of the community"** (banks never neared their legal ceilings; per-capita circulation barely changed across the crisis). The "overlending → run → never again" framing isn't his.
    

_(Smaller: on "great locks… not very useful vs the railroad" — he agrees the Welland and St Lawrence canals were dubious London-credit/political investments, but treats rail and water as complementary/competitive, with lake shipping growing ~5× alongside the railways.)_
