# Study Notes: _The Hudson's Bay Company as an Imperial Factor, 1821–1869_

### by John S. Galbraith (University of California Press, 1957)

---

## About This Book

Galbraith's study traces the **expansion, consolidation, and decline** of the Hudson's Bay Company's (HBC) fur-trade monopoly between two watershed dates:

- **1821** — the HBC's amalgamation with the North West Company (NWC) gave it a virtual monopoly over the fur trade of British North America.
- **1869** — the sale of the Company's proprietary rights in Rupert's Land to the new Dominion of Canada, marking its recognition that a fur-trade monopoly could not survive the pressure of agricultural settlement.

**Central thesis:** Galbraith argues that the HBC functioned as a genuine instrument of British imperial power in the North American West — "Imperial Britain" itself, in his words, in the territories beyond Canada — and that its commercial frontier policies (not formal colonial administration) were what determined the shape of Britain's diplomatic and territorial position vis-à-vis Russia, the United States, and a self-governing Canada. The Company is treated as an _active diplomatic and political force_, not merely a business enterprise incidental to imperial history.

**Structure:** The book is organized in four parts:

- **Part I – Frontier Policies** (Chs. 1–5): the internal organization of the Company and its trade-defense strategies on the Canadian, southern (US border), and western (Pacific) frontiers.
- **Part II – Relations with Russian America** (Chs. 6–8): the Anglo-Russian boundary settlement and coastal trade competition/cooperation with the Russian-American Company.
- **Part III – The Oregon Question and Its Aftermath** (Chs. 9–14): the Oregon boundary dispute, the Puget's Sound Agricultural Company, the Oregon crisis and its resolution, and the long tail of "possessory rights" and Vancouver Island questions that followed.
- **Part IV – The Last Years of the Monopoly** (Chs. 15–18): the unraveling of Company control at Red River, its entanglement in British and Canadian politics, and the negotiations that ended in the 1869 surrender of Rupert's Land.

# PART I — FRONTIER POLICIES

## Chapter 1: Prologue

_(pp. 3–13)_

**Overview:** Sets the stage by explaining how the HBC came to dominate British North America west of Canada, and lays out the analytical framework — "frontier policy" — that structures the whole book.

**Key Points:**

- Galbraith frames British imperial expansion generally as driven by the _mercantile class_ rather than by colonial officials — and in the North American West, the HBC effectively **was** "Imperial Britain."
- At its height the Company controlled **over 3,000,000 square miles** (~1/4 of the continent); the core was **Rupert's Land** (~1,400,000 sq. mi.), granted by **Charles II's charter of 1670**. The charter's validity was repeatedly challenged (notably by the NWC and later the Canadian government), but the Company's real power rested on its _efficiency_, not the charter's legal force.
- Corporate structure: governed from London by a **governor, deputy governor, and committee of seven** (all stockholders), answerable to the **general court** of shareholders.
- **Historical background to 1821:**
    - 17th–18th c.: HBC faced French military/economic rivalry until France's expulsion after the French and Indian War (Treaty of Paris, 1763).
    - Post-1763: harassed by small independent British traders.
    - **1784**: rival traders coalesce into the **North West Company** (Montreal-based). NWC's "wintering partners" profit-sharing system gave it dynamism; HBC's salaried-employee model was more conservative but ultimately more durable.
    - **1794 Jay Treaty** restrictions push NWC traders further north/west, intensifying rivalry.
    - **1811**: Earl of **Selkirk** founds the **Red River colony** (granted 116,000 sq. mi. by the HBC, in which Selkirk was a dominant shareholder) — becomes the flashpoint of a decade of violent competition between the companies.
    - **1816**: Governor-General **Sir John Sherbrooke** appoints **Colonel W. B. Coltman** to investigate the violence; Coltman concludes only a government-enforced monopoly can end the conflict.
    - **1818**: Colonial undersecretary **Henry Goulburn** presses HBC toward union with the NWC (Feb. 13, 1818); NWC (financially exhausted) also seeks peace.
    - **1820**: Death of **Lord Selkirk** removes the chief obstacle to union; Goulburn intensifies pressure, researching the charter's legal vulnerabilities (citing the 1749 House of Commons committee inquiry) to coerce HBC compliance.
    - **Edward Ellice** (NWC's London agent) later claimed personal credit for engineering the merger; Galbraith calls this an exaggeration — Ellice was influential but the union resulted from converging interests of all parties, not an "Ellice-Goulburn conspiracy."
- **March 26, 1821**: The two companies **coalesce** under the HBC name.
- **July 2, 1821**: Parliament passes an act licensing the coalition's fur-trade monopoly in British North America west of Rupert's Land (including the disputed Oregon Territory, where non-licensed British subjects were barred from trading).
- **December 5, 1821**: A 21-year trading license formally grants the HBC (with the McGillivrays and Ellice) exclusive rights west of Rupert's Land.
- **Administrative structure post-1821**: Trade supervised from London by the governor/committee plus an advisory board (2 members from each former company). North America divided into a **Northern Department** (HQ York Factory) and **Southern Department** (HQ Moose Factory); Canada trade initially run separately by McGillivrays, Thain & Co., folded into a **Montreal Department** in 1826 under a single governor, **George Simpson**. NWC's profit-sharing system for **chief factors/chief traders** was retained, with annual district councils.
- **1824 reorganization**: McGillivrays/Ellice converted from profit-shares to HBC stock; the 1821 joint advisory board was dropped — confirming HBC's dominance in the merged firm, while ex-Nor'Westers' energy revitalized it.
- **The "frontier" concept (the book's key analytical tool):** Unlike British colonial frontiers in India or South Africa (met by annexation), the HBC's frontier was **commercial, not territorial** — the line where its monopoly zone met open competition. It could not garrison this frontier with troops (too costly/impractical), so it relied on a **commercial strategy**: flooding frontier districts with its most aggressive traders, paying top prices for furs, and absorbing short-term losses to bankrupt or exhaust rival traders — "outbidding" opposition until competitors gave up. In secure interior districts, by contrast, it could restrict liquor, limit over-trapping, and operate profitably and (relatively) humanely.
- Quoted at length: **George Simpson's** statement (shortly before his death) that the "fundamental principle" of HBC business was to secure _all_ furs in its territory to deny rivals any foothold, even if it meant matching any competitor's prices.
- The Company's frontier strategy worked well against **rival fur-trading companies** but was far less effective against **agricultural settlers** — lumbermen and farmers in Canada, Red River "half-breeds" who combined farming and trading, American settlers in Oregon, and miners drawn by the Fraser River gold rush. Quotes **James Douglas** ("the interests of the Colony and Fur Trade will never harmonize...") and **Peter Skene Ogden** ("the Fur trade and Civilisation can never blend together") to illustrate this recognition within the Company itself.
- The Company's frontier officers who became historically memorable (McLoughlin, Ogden, Douglas) were disproportionately those posted to contested frontiers, since "conflict is the stuff from which history is written."
- Expansion was driven by two intertwined motives: **pursuit of profit** (e.g., trading ventures to California, an agency in Hawaii) and **denial of territory to competitors** (e.g., maintaining unprofitable New Caledonia posts simply to block rivals from crossing the Rockies into the Peace River country).

**Key Figures:**

- **Earl of Selkirk** — founder of Red River colony; dominant HBC shareholder; his death (1820) cleared the way for union.
- **Henry Goulburn** — colonial undersecretary who pressured the HBC toward amalgamation.
- **Edward Ellice** — NWC's London agent; overstated his personal role in the merger.
- **George Simpson** — becomes overseas governor of the united Company (1826); author of the frontier-policy "maxim" quoted at length.
- **John Henry Pelly** — HBC governor during whose tenure frontier policy was systematized.
- **John McLoughlin, Peter Skene Ogden, James Douglas** — exemplary frontier officers, central to later chapters.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Rupert's Land** — HBC's chartered territory (1670).
- **Frontier policy** — the book's organizing concept: commercial (not military) defense of monopoly territory via aggressive underpricing of rivals.
- **Wintering partners** — NWC's profit-sharing field officers.
- **Chief factors/chief traders** — post-1821 HBC equivalent profit-sharing ranks.
- **Coalition/amalgamation of 1821** — merger of HBC and NWC.

**Important Dates:**

- **1670** — HBC charter granted by Charles II.
- **1763** — Treaty of Paris; French removed from Canada.
- **1784** — North West Company formed.
- **1794** — Jay Treaty intensifies NWC westward push.
- **1811** — Red River colony founded.
- **1818** — Goulburn presses for union.
- **1820** — Selkirk dies.
- **March 26, 1821** — HBC–NWC coalition.
- **July 2, 1821** — Parliament licenses the fur-trade monopoly.
- **December 5, 1821** — 21-year exclusive trading license issued.

- **1826** — Canada trade folded into Montreal Department under Simpson.

**Significance:** This chapter establishes the book's core interpretive lens — that the HBC's expansion after 1821 was driven by a distinctive, non-military "frontier policy" of commercial warfare against rivals — and previews the three frontiers (Canadian, southern/US, western/Pacific) that structure the rest of Part I, as well as the tension between fur trade and settlement that will recur throughout the book (Red River, Oregon, Vancouver Island, Confederation).

## Chapter 2: Personnel of the Monopoly

_(pp. 14–23)_

**Overview:** Profiles the small, tightly-knit group of men who actually ran the Company — its capital structure, ownership, London leadership, and the overseas "viceroy" George Simpson — and the workforce that carried out its trade. Argues that the Company's power rested on the talent and cohesion of a handful of individuals, not sheer size.

**Key Points:**

- **Capital & ownership:** Pre-1821 HBC capital stock was only **£103,950**; rose to **£400,000** in 1824 (after Ellice/McGillivrays converted profit-shares to stock); reached a ceiling of **£500,000** in 1854, held until the 1863 sale of the Company itself. Proprietors numbered only **18** at the 1670 charter, **64** by 1822, rising to **216** (1838) and **252** (1853) — but effective control stayed with a small circle of intermarried families: **Pelly, Berens, Harrison, Colvile, Halkett**. Shareholder "general court" meetings rarely challenged the governing committee — a structure that preserved useful secrecy but also fed public suspicion that the Company was a sinister, exploitative monopoly (a charge Galbraith says was largely undeserved: its success came from experience and competent management, not fraud or abuse).
- **John Henry Pelly** — dominant figure on the board for three decades. Family HBC connection since 1788; joined committee 1806; deputy governor 1812; **governor 1822**; simultaneously a **Bank of England director** (1822–death), deputy governor of the Bank (1839), governor of the Bank (1841–42); made a **baronet by Queen Victoria in 1840**. Pelly's real influence predated his formal title — he was effectively running the committee even as deputy governor before 1821. Illness in 1841 and a fatal head abscess (burst Dec. 1850) gradually curtailed his role; **died August 13, 1852**. His policies continued to guide the Company through 1863 even after his death.
- **The "quadrumvirate"** that drove Company policy: **Pelly, Andrew Colvile, Edward "Bear" Ellice, and George Simpson.**
    - **Andrew Colvile** — brother-in-law of the 5th Earl of Selkirk; joined the committee 1810; deputy governor 1839–52; **governor 1852–56** (succeeding Pelly); patron who made Simpson overseas governor.
    - **Edward Ellice the Elder** — the Company's political liaison, well-connected with British politicians of all parties; influence peaked in the 1830s under his brother-in-law, PM Earl Grey; active until his death in 1863. A hostile Canadian editor later overstated his "backstairs influence," per Galbraith, though he genuinely was the Company's chief government contact.
    - **George Simpson** — overseas governor **1826–1860** (shared power awkwardly with **William Williams** 1821–26 before becoming sole authority). Effectively the "Little Emperor" of the fur trade, though Galbraith argues "viceroy" is the more accurate label: Simpson remained fully subordinate to the London committee (e.g., rebuked by Pelly in 1846 for overstepping on Montreal Dept. clerk appointments), but wielded enormous _de facto_ discretion due to communication lag with London (he predated the trans-Atlantic cable) and his unmatched knowledge of North American conditions — by the 1840s he could often draft the annual instructions the committee would send him.
        - Simpson dominated the field officers' advisory councils through respect, personal charm, and the implicit threat of banishing dissenters to unattractive postings.
        - Character: Uriah-Heep-like unctuousness masking "cold, ruthless efficiency"; illegitimate birth (birth date unknown — he concealed his origins even in a published aristocratic "biography"); cynical about politicians' venality, employing agents like **Stuart Derbishire** to win favor through gifts and even outright bribery of Canadian and American officials (quoted letter re: cigars for "Bouchette," 1852). Simpson's cynicism about buying politicians was, Galbraith notes, not very successful in practice. Personally scrupulous about Company funds — his considerable fortune came from investing his own salary, not corruption.
    - Pelly and Simpson, despite contrasting personal styles (Pelly = honorable London businessman; Simpson = amoral Montreal operator), were united in commitment to preserving the monopoly and worked as an effective team.
- **Workforce:** The 1821 coalition provided for **25 chief factors and 28 chief traders** (profit-sharing "commissioned officers," the old NWC "wintering partners" model retained), overseeing roughly **1,500 contractual employees** across a territory of 3,000,000+ sq. mi. — a very thin staffing level, deliberately kept lean by Simpson's cost-cutting, which sometimes left frontier posts under-resourced for competition.
    - **Mid-1830s employment table** by department (Europeans / Half-breeds / Canadians / Sandwich Islanders): Northern Dept. 141/130/157/—; Columbia 138/47/218/55; Southern Dept. 81/89/51/—; Montreal Dept. 38/17/132/—. **Grand total: 1,294.**
    - Primary European labor source: **Scotland**, especially the impoverished **Orkney Islands** — wages plus food/lodging made HBC service attractive to men with few alternatives, though appeal declined as British wages rose while Company pay stayed flat; competition from Canadian employers and a failed experiment recruiting in **Norway** followed, along with rising desertion/discipline problems.
    - Talented officers — **McLoughlin, Douglas, Ogden, John Rowand**, and others — made Simpson's and the directors' policies effective in practice; some nepotism existed (Pelly/Simpson/Colvile relatives in the service) but was not the norm.
- **After the 1863 sale**, the new owners (facing pressure from the British government) chose politically prominent men as governors — **Sir Edmund Head, Lord Kimberley, Sir Stafford Northcote, George Joachim Goschen** — a sharp contrast with the pre-1863 governors, who (apart from Ellice) were essentially unknown in politics.
- **Closing argument:** The Company's political privileges survived mainly because the British government did not want the cost/responsibility of direct rule over Rupert's Land — not primarily because of Ellice's lobbying. Until Canada developed its own interest in the region, there was no practical alternative to Company rule, so HBC policy effectively _was_ British policy for the region, shaping the diplomacy discussed in later chapters.

**Key Figures:**

- **John Henry Pelly** — HBC governor 1822–1852; also Bank of England governor; baronet (1840); dominant policy-maker.
- **Andrew Colvile** — governor 1852–1856; Selkirk's brother-in-law; Simpson's patron.
- **Edward "Bear" Ellice** — political liaison/lobbyist for the Company, d. 1863.
- **George Simpson** — overseas governor 1826–1860; the "Little Emperor"/"viceroy" of the fur trade; knighted 1841; d. 1860.
- **William Williams** — Simpson's early co-governor (to 1826).
- **Stuart Derbishire** — Simpson's political fixer/agent in Canada.
- **John McLoughlin, James Douglas, Peter Skene Ogden, John Rowand** — leading field officers (chief factors), central to later chapters.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **General court** — the full body of Company shareholders.
- **Governor and committee** — London's seven-member governing board.
- **Chief factors / chief traders** — profit-sharing commissioned field officers.
- **"Viceroy" vs. "Little Emperor"** — Galbraith's preferred characterization of Simpson's real, but bounded, authority.

**Important Dates:**

- **1788** — Pelly family's first HBC connection.
- **1806/1812/1822** — Pelly joins committee / becomes deputy governor / becomes governor.
- **1821–26** — Simpson shares power with Williams.
- **1824** — Capital raised to £400,000.
- **1826** — Simpson becomes sole overseas governor; Montreal Dept. created.
- **1840** — Pelly made baronet.
- **1841** — Simpson knighted.
- **1846** — Pelly rebukes Simpson for overreach.
- **Dec. 1850 / Aug. 13, 1852** — Pelly's fatal illness / death.

**Significance:** Establishes the human infrastructure behind the "frontier policy" described in Chapter 1 — a small, secretive, family-dominated London leadership (Pelly, Colvile, Ellice) paired with an extraordinarily capable and largely autonomous overseas governor (Simpson) commanding a lean but skilled officer corps. This personnel structure explains both the Company's remarkable effectiveness on its frontiers and its vulnerability to political attack once its monopoly came under scrutiny in the 1850s–60s (Chapters 16–18).

## Chapter 3: The Canadian Frontier

_(pp. 24–45)_

**Overview:** The first detailed case study of "frontier policy" in action. The Canadian frontier (Ottawa/St. Maurice valleys and the north shores of Lakes Huron/Superior) was not profitable in itself but was defended vigorously — at a loss — because it was the route by which a new "North West Company" might again emerge to threaten Rupert's Land.

**Key Points:**

- The Company inherited depleted, low-value Canadian posts in 1821 but held them as a **buffer/protective zone**, not for direct profit — memory of how the original NWC had emerged from Canada made the governor and committee (and Simpson) determined to prevent a repeat.
- Three sub-regions, each with distinct opposition:
    - **Ottawa River valley** — most dangerous route into Rupert's Land; opposition from "auxiliary fur traders" (lumbermen, potash-makers, storekeepers) who traded furs as a side business and thus couldn't be undercut on overhead costs the way dedicated traders could. Iroquois hunters, cut off from NWC-era interior work, were hostile to HBC.
    - **St. Maurice River** — hunted by half-breed **Abenaki** (from St. Francis, subsistence farmers who hunted seasonally) and **Wamontachingues ("Montachines")**.
    - **North shores of Lakes Huron/Superior** — rocky Laurentian Shield, thinly populated; opposition purely from petty/independent traders (the type of rival the Company was _most_ effective against).
- **The King's Posts affair** — a costly cautionary tale: London directors under-bid (offered only £500–600/yr) at the 1822 lease auction for the King's Posts (a historic Quebec crown lease dating to 1653/1733, previously held by the NWC), losing it to **John Goudie**. Realizing their strategic error, the Company spent years and much money undoing it: leasing the flanking seigneuries of **Mille Vaches** and **Mingan** to squeeze the lessees, engaging in price wars and litigation (including a 1830 armed collision), and finally in 1830 **buying out the lease from the Lampsons** (successors to Goudie) for **£25,000** — after estimating annual losses of ~£2,000/year from the feud. Lesson: never let a strategic lease go cheaply; London reprimanded but later vindicated agent **James Keith**'s costly buyout decision.
- **Bargaining principle illustrated:** the Company never "bought out" small, capital-less petty traders (that would only invite more to demand buyouts) — it simply outcompeted them into ruin. It _would_ negotiate paid withdrawal with well-capitalized rivals like the Lampsons, when the cost of continued war exceeded the cost of a settlement.
- After winning the St. Lawrence north shore, the Company still had to defend the **upper St. Maurice** from Abenaki incursions from outside Rupert's Land (some seizures of furs ordered by Simpson, 1830s) and maintained money-losing posts there through the 1840s–60s purely to block competition further inland (e.g., a **£100 net loss in 1847**, yet judged worthwhile).
- **Ottawa River posts** (Timiskaming, Fort Coulonge, Lake of Two Mountains) retreated steadily upstream as settlement advanced: Lake of Two Mountains' county reached 27,000 people by the early 1840s; Bytown (Ottawa)'s Carleton County grew from 2,116 (1824) to 10,128 (1840). Simpson closed the lower posts and opened a minimal **agency at Buckingham (1847)** — just a rented house, an interpreter, and a traveling clerk paying cash, no more credit or provisioning of Indians.
- Even reduced-cost agencies struggled: in 1858 the Company paid 5s.–7s.6d./lb for beaver at Buckingham against a London sale price of only 5s.8¾d./lb — a loss-leader necessary to keep furs from New York/Montreal buyers.
- **Timiskaming** was a genuinely valuable district (≈60 packs/5,400 lbs of "very superior" furs annually in the 1820s) that stayed largely undisturbed until the **McConnell brothers** (lumbermen from Hull) began cutting timber and trading there in winter 1838–39. Simpson's countermove: have Company crews **log the area themselves (1840–43)** to deny timber to rivals — a money-losing experiment that failed to keep lumbermen away once timber prices recovered (200–300 lumbermen present by summer 1845). Timiskaming was formally merged into the settlement-facing **Montreal Department in 1851**; cash tariffs replaced barter by the mid-1850s; Canadian customs officers fined the Company for unpaid duties in 1858 — clear signs the district had become part of the ordinary Canadian economy.
- **Fort Coulonge/Lac des Allumettes**: settlement reached Fort Coulonge by 1847; Simpson sold the post's land/buildings in 1855 (£500) and consolidated trade upstream at Lac des Allumettes; by 1860 settlement had advanced to the Joachim rapids, 42 miles above Pembroke. Simpson's successor **Alexander G. Dallas** later replaced fixed forts (e.g., Fort William, near Pembroke) with minimal agencies as settlement advanced further — a more decisive break from "Indian country" trading habits than Simpson himself had managed.
- **Lake Huron/Superior district:** opposition from petty/discharged American Fur Company traders based at Sault Ste. Marie, Drummond Island, and Newmarket. The Company entered the **fishing business in 1840** on Lake Superior — partly for profit, mostly to deny Indian labor/fish resources to rival (mostly American) fishermen-traders — but its officers proved as inept at curing fish as at logging, and the business was scaled back after 1859.
- **1845 copper rush** on both lakeshores briefly boosted regional population (mostly gone by 1848); construction of the **Sault Ste. Marie ship canal** (opened to shipping 1855) and growth of Minnesota increased American competitive pressure. From 1857 the Lake Huron district was supplied from Lachine rather than Moose Factory, reflecting its reorientation toward the settled Canadian economy.
- **Political maneuvers to blunt Indian-agency competition:** Simpson lobbied (successfully) for a **customs collector at Sault Ste. Marie** to burden American rivals with duties; and used arguments about liquor abuse and epidemic disease (citing officer **John Ballenden**'s 1845 letter about deaths from measles brought back from Manitoulin Island gatherings) to press the Canadian government to let the HBC distribute Indian treaty presents locally instead of at Manitoulin Island — this campaign helped shape the **Robinson Treaties (September 1850)**, negotiated by **W. B. Robinson** with heavy reliance on HBC officers, ceding Indian land rights from Lake Huron to Rupert's Land's border for £4,000 plus a perpetual annuity (rising to £1/Indian/year from land-sale proceeds); the Company was made responsible for distributing payments/presents at Fort William and Michipicoten.
- **King's Posts controversy (political fallout):** Simpson's 1836 bid to cut timber on the King's domain (partly to deny lumbermen the area, partly to employ Company men off-season) provoked backlash from **Louis Joseph Papineau**'s French-Canadian reformers in the Lower Canadian Assembly, who saw it as another "special privilege for the English." The Company abandoned the timber license, but the episode fed years of hostility. Colonial Secretary **Lord Glenelg** promised in 1837 the lease wouldn't be renewed, but after the 1837 Lower Canada Rebellion suspended the Assembly, a more sympathetic Executive Council renewed the (hunting/trading-only) lease for 21 years in **1841** (£600/year). By the 1840s, 400+ settler families on the Saguenay ignored the Company's "exclusive" rights (unenforceable — no local jury would convict a violator), and by 1858 the Canadian government (with HBC consent) **terminated the King's Posts lease** to open the fisheries — amid a wave of anti-Company sentiment fueled by wildly exaggerated profit estimates (e.g., a claimed £5,000/year from salmon fisheries alone, reported by the _Canadian News_, which the Company's own silence did nothing to rebut).
- **Outfit 1862 financial table** (illustrative of the frontier-loss strategy in practice): total returns $93,555.15 against total losses of $22,396.50 (net loss $20,527.23) across Lake Huron, Ottawa, Montreal Agency, St. Maurice, King's Posts, Mingan, etc. — only Mingan showed a profit. Only **Esquimaux Bay (Hamilton Inlet)** was reliably profitable and was administratively detached in 1859 to be supplied directly from England.
- **Chronic problem:** rival traders sometimes sold their best furs in New York and dumped inferior furs on the HBC in Canada at high prices — a recognized vulnerability of the "buy everything to deny it to rivals" strategy.
- **Conclusion:** Despite steady financial losses, the frontier-defense strategy succeeded in its true purpose — by 1869, when Rupert's Land passed to Canada, the interior beyond the Ottawa gateway remained undisturbed by competition and the monopoly price structure held. Quotes **Edward Hopkins** (a Simpson protégé and later critic) conceding the underlying strategic logic was sound. The chapter closes noting the political cost: the Company's aggressive frontier tactics fed a growing Canadian narrative of the HBC as a "greedy, grasping monopoly" blocking settlement — a resentment that would resurface powerfully in Part IV.

**Key Figures:**

- **John Goudie / the Lampsons (Curtis M. Lampson)** — successive lessees of the King's Posts who out-competed and then sold out to the Company.
- **James Keith** — Montreal department officer who negotiated the costly 1830 King's Posts buyout.
- **William Williams** — early Company officer ("like the Hydra heads another succeeds" quote on petty traders).
- **John Ballenden** — Sault Ste. Marie officer whose letter on Indian mortality/liquor abuse helped shift government present-distribution policy.
- **W. B. Robinson** — Canadian treaty commissioner (Robinson Treaties, 1850).
- **Louis Joseph Papineau** — French-Canadian reform leader who attacked the Company's King's Posts timber license.
- **Lord Glenelg** — colonial secretary who promised (then saw reversed) non-renewal of the King's Posts lease.
- **Alexander G. Dallas** — Simpson's successor; replaced fixed forts with agencies as settlement advanced.
- **Edward Hopkins** — Simpson protégé/critic who nonetheless validated the frontier strategy's logic.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **King's Posts** — historic Quebec crown fur-trade lease (est. 1653) on the lower St. Lawrence/Saguenay.
- **Auxiliary fur traders** — lumbermen, farmers, shopkeepers who traded furs as a side income and were hard to price out.
- **Robinson Treaties (1850)** — Indian land-cession treaties from Lake Huron to Rupert's Land, brokered with HBC assistance.
- **Frontier loss-leader strategy** — deliberately unprofitable posts maintained to protect profitable interior districts.

**Important Dates:**

- **1822** — King's Posts lease lost to Goudie.
- **1830** — Company buys back King's Posts lease (£25,000).
- **1836–37** — Simpson's timber-cutting bid on the King's domain sparks French-Canadian backlash.
- **1838–39** — McConnell brothers threaten Timiskaming.
- **1840** — Company enters Lake Superior fishing business.
- **1841** — New 21-year King's Posts lease issued.
- **1845** — Copper rush on Lakes Huron/Superior; Ballenden's letter on Manitoulin Island mortality.
- **1847** — Buckingham agency established; posts reach Fort Coulonge's settled frontier.
- **September 1850** — Robinson Treaties concluded.
- **1851** — Timiskaming merged into Montreal Department.
- **1855** — Sault Ste. Marie canal opens; Fort Coulonge sold.
- **1857** — Canadian government eyes southern Rupert's Land for settlement; Lake Huron district resupplied via Lachine.
- **1858** — King's Posts lease terminated; Canadian customs fines the Company at Timiskaming.
- **1859** — Esquimaux Bay detached from Montreal Department.
- **1862 (Outfit 1862 accounts)** — net loss of $20,527 documented across Canadian frontier posts.

**Significance:** Demonstrates concretely how the "frontier policy" of Chapter 1 operated on the ground — sustained, often money-losing competition maintained purely to protect the profitable interior — and shows its political price: mounting Canadian public hostility (French-Canadian and Anglo alike) that foreshadows the Company's growing vulnerability in Canadian and British politics explored in Part IV.

## Chapter 4: The Southern Frontier

_(pp. 46–77)_

**Overview:** Covers the most dangerous frontier of all — the international boundary from Lake Superior to the Souris River — and the rise of **Red River** as the "Achilles' heel" of the Company. Traces the Company's relatively cordial trade rivalry with the **American Fur Company**, and then the far more destabilizing internal challenge posed by Red River **free traders and métis (Boisbrûlés)**, culminating in the collapse of the Company's legal monopoly at the **Sayer trial (1849)**.

**Key Points:**

- The southern boundary offered easy water-route access deep into Rupert's Land (Athabaska, Saskatchewan country), making it strategically critical.
- **Red River colony composition (1821):** métis/Boisbrûlés (half-breed descendants of Indian women and French/British traders), Selkirk's Scots settlers (from Orkney/Highlands), Swiss colonists, **De Meuron** mercenary veterans (disbanded 1812), and retired Company servants (Simpson estimated ~4/5 of ~2,000 inhabitants in 1824 were retired servants, Boisbrûlés, and Indians). The Swiss and De Meurons were initially the most "restive" but many emigrated to the US; half-breeds seemed docile at first but Simpson recognized their latent volatility.
- **1824 licensing system:** Simpson ended direct Company trading with district Indians (except barter at Fort Garry) and instead let settlers get **licenses** to collect Indian furs for cash, aiming to reduce local resentment and intercept furs that might otherwise go to Americans. Risk: licensees might get a taste for profits and become independent competitors — which is exactly what eventually happened. Early friction cases: **Ferdinand Cels** (arrested, later pardoned) and **Andrew McDermot** (furs seized, then compensated) in 1826.
- Simpson also **tacitly let licensees trade across the US border**, technically against the governor and committee's instructions, rationalizing that they were "not connected with the Company" — a policy Galbraith criticizes as morally corrosive to the Company's own case against illicit trade in its own territory.
- **The American Fur Company (AFC):** founded by **John Jacob Astor** (1808 charter); absorbed the Mackinaw/Southwest Company (1817) after forcing NWC partners out via an 1817 law barring foreigners from the US fur trade; managed by **Ramsay Crooks**. Contact with HBC began **1822** near Rainy Lake/Vermilion Lake.
- Relations between HBC and AFC were unusually **"gentlemanly"** — both sides avoided violence and largely respected the boundary (Simpson: "There are 1,000 ways of annoying and harassing our opponents without having recourse to violent measures"). Notable incident: **John McLoughlin** (in charge at Lac la Pluie from 1822) briefly detained two American clerks who crossed the border (1822–23) to deter further trespass — no violations reported thereafter.
- Both companies made competing (unreliable) claims about the other's profitability; Astor's own claims to Congress (1829) about AFC losses are judged self-serving and unreliable.
- **1829 peace overtures:** AFC's **William A. Aitkin** proposed ending frontier competition (AFC would abandon posts west of Lake Superior except Vermilion Lake); **William B. Astor** separately proposed a mutual end to the liquor trade. HBC's governor/committee were skeptical, doubting AFC's ability to enforce restraint given its own struggles with liquor-dependent Indian bands — Simpson quotes **Astor's "cow" metaphor** (HBC holds the head, AFC the tail, petty traders "milk the cow" if liquor curbs let in new competitors).
- Simpson meanwhile used **Red River half-breeds** (e.g. trader **Nolin**) as unofficial proxies to harass AFC across the border at Pembina — foreshadowing the métis "right to trade" claims of the 1840s (Nolin's 1830 argument that as a "native of the soil" he wasn't bound by trade restrictions is an early statement of the doctrine that later destroyed Company control).
- **1833 Rainy Lake Agreement:** After **Ramsay Crooks** reorganized AFC's frontier operations around independent postmasters (La Rose and Couttier) rather than direct company control — a change London feared would produce less scrupulous rivals — the governor and committee authorized Simpson to negotiate. Simpson struck a deal with Aitkin directly: **AFC would abandon its posts from Pembina to Lake Superior for £300 sterling/year**, for 5 years. Highly successful — by 1839 the district was secure enough that HBC banned liquor distribution outright there.
- The agreement was periodically renewed (with AFC winning concessions like fishing-post rights and provisioning of Indians) until **1847**, when AFC's changed ownership made further protection impossible. In the interim (1841), 500 Rainy Lake Indians forced Simpson to partially restore liquor distribution (reduced from 42 kegs/year pre-1839 to 8 kegs after 1841) by threatening to withhold their wild-rice trade.
- **AFC's decline:** the Panic of 1837 crippled AFC; Crooks sold its upper Mississippi posts to **Pierre Chouteau, Jr. & Co.** (1841); AFC suspended payments in **September 1842** with ~$300,000 in debt and went into receivership — ending its usefulness as a buffer for the HBC.
- **Norman W. Kittson** emerges as the real threat: established as a fur trader/merchant near Fort Snelling (1839), then made "special partner" to **Henry H. Sibley** (Chouteau's regional agent) in **1842**, given the upper Minnesota/Red River valley trading territory. Kittson set up at **Pembina** (just south of the border) in 1844 specifically to draw Red River half-breed furs south.
- **Rise of Red River free trade:** licensing (1820s) had initially seemed to work (Simpson satisfied 1825, 1828) but by 1829 unlicensed settlers were pushing into Swan Lake, Manitoba Lake, and the Qu'Appelle River — the first sign of the independent spirit that would break the monopoly. Two restraining factors before the 1840s: (1) the HBC–AFC agreement denied settlers a nearby competitive market; (2) sparse US population nearby. Both disappeared as **Wisconsin's population grew 25-fold (1840–60)** and **Minnesota Territory** grew from <5,000 (1849) to 172,000+ (1860); trade with **St. Peter, Minnesota** grew through the 1830s–40s (initially cattle-for-horses, later furs).
- Key Red River malcontents: **Andrew McDermot** and **James Sinclair**, merchants with Company freighting contracts, dissatisfied with rates — became Kittson's allies once he opened at Pembina (1844).
- **1844: Simpson cancels independent trading licenses** (fearing they'd given settlers "a taste for the fur trade") and Governor **Alexander Christie** issues a loyalty-oath proclamation (settlers must swear off fur trafficking since Dec. 8, 1844, or lose Company transport privileges) — a move Galbraith calls delusional given the Company's lack of enforcement power. McDermot and Sinclair refused to comply; Sinclair actively defied and encouraged others.
- **August 29, 1845** — Sinclair-led half-breeds present Christie with a formal claim of an inherent right, as "natives of the country," to hunt and sell furs to any buyer — a claim Christie rejected legally but could not suppress practically (the Company's small constabulary had itself been implicated in illicit trade and was disbanded before 1845).
- **Simpson's covert countermove (1845–46):** sent **Henry Fisher** (nominally an American citizen) to set up a rival post at Pembina under cover of an American Fur Company-issued license, to undercut/ruin Kittson from within — Governor Christie objected on ethical grounds (it made the Company as much an "illicit trader" as its opponents). Simpson also tried to enlist Crooks to get Kittson's US trading license revoked over liquor sales.
- Simpson separately **bought off McDermot (£100, 1846)** and eventually reconciled with **Sinclair** (also compensated £100 via Pelly in London, 1846; later given a Company post at Fort Nez Percés/Walla Walla in 1853 to remove him from Red River).
- **Pelly's ethical intervention:** Simpson's attempt to discriminate against free traders via currency manipulation (a proposed 5% premium on bills of exchange, 1844) was **overruled by Pelly**, who argued it was dishonest and beneath the Company's dignity, and who articulated a fundamentally different strategy: abandon the fiction of exclusive chartered rights near the settlement and instead compete honestly — "selling as low as you can afford and buying as high" — i.e., extend the frontier-competition model to Red River itself.
- **Governor Christie's alternative** was a defensive cordon of well-manned posts from Lac la Pluie to Fort Ellice (400 miles) to intercept smugglers — recognized by Christie himself as impractical without a reliable large force, which the Company could not muster or afford.
- **The Sayer Trial (May 17, 1849):** the decisive turning point. **Pierre Guillaume Sayer**, tried for illegal fur trading, was surrounded in court by an armed métis mob; found technically guilty but given no punishment (not even confiscation of furs) amid cries of **"Le commerce est libre!"** ("Trade is free!"). Galbraith: the Company never again attempted to prevent free trade by half-breeds — what Pelly had proposed to concede voluntarily in 1846 was now extracted by force.
- **Aftermath:** Kittson's operations expanded (posts at Turtle Mountain, Mouse River, Lake Roseau, Lake of the Woods by 1848, Rainy Lake by 1850; ~$12,000 capital, 4 clerks including **Joseph Rolette, Jr.**, 10 voyageurs by 1850). The Company responded in kind — matching prices, opening competing posts (Pembina 1845, Turtle Mountain and Lake of the Woods 1846) — effectively **abandoning chartered-monopoly pretensions** and simply out-competing rivals as it did elsewhere. Kittson eventually wound down (withdrew from Rainy Lake 1851, quit entirely by 1853–54, transferring to Rolette), reappeared briefly in 1856 but was outbid by **Chief Factor John Swanston** and did not return.
- Free trade nonetheless kept spreading westward and northward (free traders camped near Fort Carlton by winter 1855–56; rumors of expeditions toward the ultra-lucrative **Athabaska/Mackenzie River** districts alarmed the Northern Department Council at its **1855 Norway House meeting**, which briefly authorized seizure of any such expedition — later softened by Simpson to a strategy of deterrence via well-supplied "watching parties" and liberal Indian prices along the route, since the sheer remoteness of the Mackenzie (5–7 years' round trip via England) was itself a natural barrier).
- **1856 — Simpson's remarkable admission** to Governor **John Shepherd** (Colvile's successor): the Company's charter, "as far as exclusive right of trade goes, is almost a nullity," and he suggests **voluntarily surrendering the charter for compensation** (on the East India Company model) — a striking anticipation of the eventual 1869 sale, though nothing came of it immediately.
- **The Bannatyne affair (1857):** Chief Factor **George Barnston** at Norway House, frustrated by years of Company passivity, arrested free trader **Andrew G. B. Bannatyne** (McDermot's son-in-law) for openly loading a trading outfit. Legal adviser **Francis G. Johnson** (governor of Assiniboia) advised immediate release as the arrest was illegal; the incident came at a highly damaging moment — right after a **British House of Commons select committee** investigation (1857) had criticized the Company's administration and recommended opening southern Rupert's Land to settlement (previewing Ch. 15–16). Barnston was reassigned to Michipicoten; this episode, per Galbraith, "destroyed all hope" of ever reviving the Company's monopoly claims by coercive means.
- **New logistics, same policy:** From 1859 the Company began shipping goods via rail from Montreal to **St. Paul**, then steamboat to Red River — a pragmatic improvement, though it also cemented St. Paul as the region's dominant supply/fur-trade hub, benefiting free traders too.
- **Standard 1850s–60s buying rule at Fort Garry:** pay ½–⅔ of the latest London sale prices, adjusted upward via special bargains or **derouine parties** (winter fur-collection agents) whenever needed to match American offers.

**Key Figures:**

- **John Jacob Astor / Ramsay Crooks** — American Fur Company founder / manager.
- **William A. Aitkin** — AFC officer who negotiated the 1833 Rainy Lake non-competition agreement.
- **Norman W. Kittson** — Pembina-based free trader who became the HBC's chief antagonist in southern Rupert's Land.
- **Henry H. Sibley / Pierre Chouteau, Jr.** — Kittson's business patrons.
- **Andrew McDermot, James Sinclair** — leading Red River free-trade merchants, later co-opted by the Company.
- **Alexander Christie** — governor of Assiniboia; issued the failed 1844–45 loyalty-oath policy.
- **Henry Fisher** — Simpson's covert agent sent to undercut Kittson at Pembina.
- **Pierre Guillaume Sayer** — defendant in the pivotal 1849 free-trade trial.
- **George Barnston** — Chief Factor who provoked the 1857 Bannatyne incident.
- **John Henry Pelly** — overruled Simpson's currency-manipulation scheme on ethical grounds; proposed honest price competition instead.
- **John Shepherd** — governor who received Simpson's 1856 suggestion to surrender the charter.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Métis / Boisbrûlés** — half-breed descendants central to Red River free trade.
- **Free trade movement** — the Red River campaign to break the Company's legal fur-trade monopoly.
- **Sayer Trial (1849)** — the case that effectively ended enforcement of the Company's monopoly.
- **Derouine parties** — Company agents sent out in winter to buy furs directly from Indian hunters.
- **"Le commerce est libre!"** — the rallying cry of the Sayer trial mob.

**Important Dates:**

- **1822** — HBC–AFC contact begins near Rainy Lake.
- **1824** — Licensing system introduced at Red River.
- **1829** — Aitkin/Astor peace overtures.
- **1833** — Rainy Lake Agreement (£300/yr, AFC withdraws to Pembina–Lake Superior line).
- **1839** — Kittson begins trading near Fort Snelling.
- **1842** — Kittson becomes Sibley's "special partner"; AFC enters receivership (Sept.).
- **1844** — Kittson opens at Pembina; Simpson cancels licenses; Christie's loyalty-oath proclamation.
- **August 1845** — Sinclair-led half-breeds assert trading rights.
- **1845–46** — Henry Fisher's covert Pembina operation.
- **May 17, 1849** — Sayer Trial.
- **1850** — Company matches Kittson's prices openly at Red River/Lac la Pluie.
- **1853–54** — Kittson withdraws from the trade.
- **1855** — Northern Dept. council authorizes (then Simpson softens) interception of Mackenzie River expeditions.
- **1856** — Simpson's letter to Shepherd suggesting charter surrender.
- **1857** — Bannatyne affair; House of Commons select committee report.
- **1859** — Montreal–St. Paul supply route begins.

**Significance:** This is the pivotal chapter for understanding the internal collapse of Company authority — the same "frontier competition" strategy that worked against rival companies proved unable to suppress a _domestic_ population (the métis) claiming an inherent right to trade. The Sayer trial (1849) marks the practical end of the chartered monopoly's _legal_ enforceability, even though the Company continued to dominate _commercially_ through aggressive pricing — setting up the political crisis of Part IV (Chapters 15–18).

## Chapter 5: The Western Frontier

_(pp. 78–110)_

**Overview:** Covers the Company's "fur desert" strategy in the **Snake country** (roughly eastern Oregon/Washington and Idaho) — the fourth Pacific-slope frontier and the one facing direct American fur-trade competition — plus the Company's transition, as American settlers poured into Oregon, from fur trading to retail sales, and finally withdrawal. Closes Part I with the chapter's key summary of the "frontier policy" concept across all theaters.

**Key Points:**

- Four Pacific-slope frontiers: facing **Russian America**; the **Pacific coast**; south toward **California/Mexico**; and the **Snake country** — the chapter's focus, since it's where the Company met the American fur trade most directly.
- The Columbia district's seven inherited NWC posts (**Fort George/Astoria, Fort Nez Percés/Walla Walla, Spokane House, Flathead House, Kootenay Fort, Fort Okanagan, Fort Kamloops**) were retained after 1821 less for their own profit than as a **bargaining chip** for the still-undefined US-British boundary south of Russian America (per the governor and committee's 1822 letter to Simpson) — occupancy strengthens diplomatic claims.
- The district was transformed from a money-loser under NWC mismanagement into a profitable one through: **George Simpson's 1824–25 visit** and cost-cutting (workforce cut from ~220 toward efficiency, centralized under McLoughlin); competent new officers (**John Haldane, John Dugald Cameron, James McMillan, John Lee Lewes**); and a **new supply/marketing system** — shipping via Company vessels from England instead of the unprofitable Boston-agency/Canton-sale arrangement with **J. & T. H. Perkins** (which had taken ~25% commission and sold Columbia beaver in Canton for only $3.50/skin, far below London prices).
- **John McLoughlin** appointed Chief Factor in charge of the Columbia — becomes the dominant administrator of the district for two decades (his career continues through Chapters 9–12, Oregon crisis).
- **The Snake country expedition:** originated under ex-NWC man **Donald McKenzie** (1818–21 expeditions) using a self-sustaining, horse-mounted trapping-party model (trapping directly rather than trading with Indians). Initially judged unprofitable and nearly abandoned due to Blackfoot/Piegan hostility, but **John Lee Lewes** and Chief Factors **Alexander Kennedy** and **Cameron** argued the district was undervalued; a 1823 "caretaker" expedition under **Finan McDonald** (which lost 6 men to the Piegans but still returned 4,000+ skins) proved the potential.
- **Alexander Ross** led the first regular Snake expedition (1824) — a difficult assignment (unruly freemen, hostile Blackfeet/Piegans/Snakes) further complicated by the appearance of **American trappers** for the first time since the failure of Astor's Astoria venture — Ross's party met **Jedediah Smith**'s group (from General **William H. Ashley**'s company) in 1824. Ross indiscreetly bragged to Smith about the Company's rich returns, fueling American interest.
- Simpson replaced Ross with **Peter Skene Ogden** after his 1824 return (uncharitably judging Ross "self-sufficient" and "empty headed" — Galbraith regards this as unfair to a genuinely capable man). Simpson's real insight: the Snake country could be turned into a deliberate **"fur desert"** — trapped so thoroughly that American traders would have no incentive to push toward the Columbia, simultaneously supporting Britain's diplomatic claim to a favorable Oregon boundary.
- **Peter Skene Ogden profile:** entered the fur trade from a spirit of adventure (well-connected loyalist family — his brother Charles was Lower Canada's attorney general); joined NWC ~1809–10; transferred to the Columbia in 1818 after alleged violence against HBC men; re-employed after 1821 union due to his evident value. Renowned as tough, fair to his men (key to freeman loyalty), ambitious, and undisciplined about respecting territorial boundaries.
- **The Gardner incident (1825):** Ogden's 1824–25 expedition crossed the 42nd parallel into **Mexican territory**, met American trapper **Johnson Gardner**, who told Ogden's freemen they were on American soil and thus free of HBC obligations — **23 of Ogden's freemen deserted** with horses and furs. London directors castigated Ogden and Simpson for the border crossing (though Simpson/Ogden/McLoughlin had never actually been instructed to respect the US or Mexican boundary before this). Ogden still returned with meaningful furs (2,485 large + 1,210 small beaver, ~£2,000 profit).
- Response: freeman contracts were liberalized (better beaver prices, cheaper supplies/horses) after 1826 to prevent further defections — successful; some 1825 deserters returned to Company service by 1828.
- Ogden led **six Snake expeditions (through 1829–30)**, ranging as far south as the Gulf of California and as far east as the Bear River (Utah), continuing to trap deep in Mexican territory with no diplomatic repercussions since no Mexican authorities were encountered. Fur catches declined steadily under sustained over-trapping (large beaver taken: 1826—2,099; 1830—1,330; 1831—737; 1832—788; 1834—350; 1835—220; 1836—800) — evidence the fur-desert strategy was working as intended.
- **Simpson's July 9, 1827 letter to McLoughlin** is quoted as the classic statement of the fur-desert doctrine: keep the country "closely hunted" to remove Americans' economic incentive to colonize, while treating Company trappers with fairness/good pay to prevent desertion, and underselling any American traders encountered.
- Simpson's 1826–27 scheme to recruit Red River **half-breeds** (led by **Cuthbert Grant**, "Warden of the Plains," hero of the Seven Oaks affair) to reinforce Ogden's parties was vetoed in spirit by the governor and committee, who (unlike Simpson) worried about provoking violent incidents that might draw US government intervention — "opposed, not by violence... but by underselling."
- American competition in the Snake country was inherently weak: poorly capitalized St. Louis-outfitted trappers, little internal discipline, prone to desertion and Indian attacks (32 Americans killed 1824–26). The most serious American operation, **Ashley's** firm (sold in 1826 to **Jedediah Smith, David Jackson, William Sublette**), retreated from the Snake country by 1830 (selling to the Rocky Mountain Fur Company) as returns declined and Santa Fe trade grew more attractive; Smith's party was ambushed on the Umpqua River in 1828 (15 killed).
- **Joshua Pilcher** (failed Missouri Fur Co. leader) proposed an alliance with the HBC in 1828 that Simpson publicly refused (citing respect for US law, for the record) while privately instructing Ogden to poach Pilcher's freemen and furs — by the late 1820s Simpson could say "the Company had nothing to fear" from American land-based rivals in the Snake country.
- **Maritime competition (1829–30):** American ships **Owhyhee** (Capt. **John Dominis**) and **Convoy** entered the Columbia River and undercut HBC's coastal tariff drastically (e.g., guns for 6 skins vs. the Company's prior rate of 18). McLoughlin and Simpson (visiting at the time) matched prices and sent buying parties ahead of the Americans; a related trader, **Bache**, was eventually re-hired into Company service to end his competition at The Dalles. McLoughlin negotiated (controversially, against strict Company policy of never "buying out" opponents) to purchase Dominis's remaining trade goods for lumber rather than furs — justified by his precarious supply situation after the wreck of the _William and Ann_.
- **Nathaniel Wyeth:** arrived 1832 hoping to found a salmon-curing/fur-trading post; his supply ship wrecked, leaving him dependent on McLoughlin's hospitality over winter 1832–33. Returned in 1834 (after establishing **Fort Hall** near the Snake-Portneuf junction when a contract with the Rocky Mountain Fur Company fell through) and negotiated an unusually generous **territorial-division agreement** with McLoughlin — HBC would not trade beyond the Grand Ronde River (except via **Francis Ermatinger** and **Thomas McKay**'s parties, who retained rights to the Green River rendezvous) in exchange for Wyeth not competing at Fort Vancouver. London censured McLoughlin for this deviation from standard aggressive-competition doctrine, quoting the governor and committee's forceful restatement of frontier policy (oppose all competitors "at all points," accept "temporary loss," never negotiate reciprocal arrangements). Wyeth's business failed by 1836; the Company bought Fort Hall in 1837.
- Contrast: **Captain Benjamin Bonneville** (arrived 1832, 120 men) was met with orthodox all-out opposition, not negotiation — McLoughlin reprimanded clerk **Pierre Pambrun** for even a minor accommodating trade with Bonneville's party.
- **Thomas McKay** (McLoughlin's stepson) ran a semi-independent trading operation at **Fort Boise** (built winter 1834–35) under Company supply, generating solid profits (£411 and £997 in 1835–36 and 1836–37) — though Simpson disliked the "agent" arrangement and had it terminated in 1836.
- **Shift to settlement (late 1830s–40s):** missionary **Jason Lee** (Methodist, arrived 1834 with Wyeth) settled in the Willamette Valley and became, in effect, an advance agent for American colonization rather than a missionary to Indians. "Oregon fever" brought organized wagon migrations starting 1839, growing yearly through the early 1840s (chief trader **Pambrun**'s 1841 letter to Simpson notes immigration "has become so common & regular that it forms no further a novelty").
- **Fort Hall under Richard Grant (1842–51)** pivoted to selling provisions to Oregon Trail emigrants — profitable for a time, but competing settler/emigrant demand for meat/leather also reduced beaver-hunting incentives. **Mormon settlers** (from 1847) were initially good customers but became active retail competitors by 1850, undercutting Company prices at Fort Vancouver. Fort Hall's fortunes collapsed after Grant left (1851); his successor McArthur deserted in 1854.
- **Final abandonment:** Snake country posts abandoned **summer 1856** — no longer a fur frontier, no longer profitable, and American settlers (especially after Indian wars 1850–55) were increasingly hostile to Company employees (unverified rumors that postmaster **Charles Ogden** at Fort Boise had armed Indians who killed immigrants fed anti-Company sentiment).
- **Post-1846 Oregon department:** the Company stayed on mainly to preserve leverage for an eventual **sale of its posts/possessory rights to the US government** (developed fully in Ch. 13), shifting increasingly from fur profits to retail sales to settlers (e.g., in 1849–50, of £35,390 total department profit, £14,902 came from the Fort Vancouver sales shop alone). Profitability became erratic: net loss of £6,754 in Outfit 1851, profit of £5,400 in 1852, loss of over £1,200 in 1853 — the Company nonetheless held on until **1860**, hoping continued occupancy would improve its negotiating position for compensation.
- **California/Hawaii contrast:** unlike Canada, Red River, or the Snake country, the Company's California trapping expeditions (and its Hawaii agency, 1839–1859) were run on **pure profit-motive** terms with no protective/strategic rationale — trade was simply discontinued once profits declined, unlike the loss-absorbing frontier-defense logic elsewhere.
- **Chapter/Part I closing synthesis:** the frontier-defense strategy succeeded wherever the opposition depended solely on the fur trade (rival companies, individual traders) but was powerless against agricultural/settler expansion. This distinction — effective against traders, helpless against farmers — is the thread that connects Canada, Red River, and Oregon, and explains why the Company's political troubles (Parts III–IV) came from settlement, not rival commerce. The chapter's final paragraph explicitly frames the Company as the true **author of British North American diplomacy** in the West: its trading activity produced the Oregon question and the Russo-British boundary dispute, and its vested rights later became the chief obstacle to Canadian expansion — setting up Parts II–IV.

# PART II — RELATIONS WITH RUSSIAN AMERICA

## Chapter 6: The Anglo-Russian Treaty

_(pp. 113–132)_

**Overview:** Explains how the 1825 Anglo-Russian boundary convention — usually understood as pure state diplomacy — was in substance a negotiation between two fur-trading monopolies (the HBC and the Russian-American Company), with Governor Pelly effectively dictating British terms to Foreign Secretary George Canning.

**Key Points:**

- Northwest American history before ~1840 was essentially fur-trade history: Russian, British, and American claims were built almost entirely on the activities of their respective trading interests.
- **Russian American Company (RAC):** grew from **Gregori Shelekhov**'s 1784 Kodiak Island post; chartered by Tsar **Paul I** (July 8, 1799) with a 20-year monopoly north of 55°N and governmental authority within it — structurally similar to the HBC's Rupert's Land charter but functionally more like the pre-1813 East India Company. From 1819 supervised intimately by Russia's **Ministry of Finance**; naval officers required for senior colonial posts — a rigid, paternalistic system that (per **James Douglas**'s 1840 assessment, quoted at length) left the company overstaffed, poorly run, and increasingly stagnant.
- **Alexander Baranoff** (chief manager 1799–1818) was the RAC's great expansionist: founded Sitka/New Archangel (1799, rebuilt 1804), and pushed as far as **Fort Ross, California** (1812) and even attempted a foothold in **Hawaii**.
- **American maritime fur trade:** originated from the accidental discovery (via Cook's crew, 1779) that Chinese buyers paid huge sums for sea-otter pelts; British captain **James Hanna**'s 1785 voyage (560 skins for ~$21,000) triggered a rush; the **Nootka Sound Convention** (1789, following the Meares-Spanish clash) grew from this competition. Trade statistics 1800–1803: **34,357 sea otters ($695,313)** and over 1 million sealskins (~$907,278) sold at Canton. American traders dominated maritime competition after ~1793 (war kept British ships away) and were purely maritime/petty-capitalist, unlike the RAC/HBC monopolies — giving them weaker territorial claims.
- Russia repeatedly tried and failed to restrict American trading with Pacific coast natives (1808 Romanzoff proposal; 1810 renewed effort, rejected by **John Quincy Adams**; post-1815 trade restrictions that only caused supply crises in Russian America, forcing a reversal via the **1824 Russo-American treaty** reopening New Archangel to American trade).
- **HBC's claim to the region** rested almost entirely on inherited **North West Company** activity — only one early HBC party (**Joseph Howse**, 1810–11) had crossed the Rockies. NWC explorers **Alexander Mackenzie** (reached the Pacific 1793 via Peace River/Dean Channel), **Simon Fraser** (established Fort St. James, Fraser's Lake Post, Fort George in New Caledonia, 1805–07), **Daniel Harmon**, **David Thompson**, and **John Stuart** built the network of interior posts between 51° and 55°N that gave Britain its foothold — trade conducted **en derouine** (peddling from posts rather than waiting at fixed stations), oriented north-south along river valleys rather than toward the coast (hostile coastal Indians discouraged westward extension). By 1821 no NWC/HBC posts existed north of 54°30′ or west of 125°W — meaning British and Russian traders had **never yet come into direct contact**.
- **The Ukase of September 4, 1821:** Tsar's decree claiming Russian sovereignty over all territory/waters north of 51°N and barring foreign vessels within 100 Italian miles of the coast — aimed chiefly at American interlopers but also directly threatened HBC ambitions; provoked British protest on both maritime-rights and territorial-sovereignty grounds. The **RAC's charter renewal (September 13, 1821)** extended Russian claims explicitly to "the northern point of the Island of Vancouver."
- News reached the HBC late (Feb. 1822, via unofficial channels) — Pelly (then deputy governor) pressed **Lord Londonderry (Castlereagh)** for protection; after **George Canning** became foreign secretary (Sept. 16, 1822) the Company found an energetic advocate.
- **Diplomatic context:** Britain's Oregon/Russian-boundary interest was a low priority compared to the Greek insurrection, the Spanish revolution, and the question of European intervention in Spanish America — Canning avoided letting national prestige get entangled in "a few thousand square miles of barren wilderness," which explains British willingness to make concessions.
- The HBC pushed hard for territorial expansion on the ground to strengthen its diplomatic hand: directed by the governor and committee (Feb. 27, 1822) to extend trading as far north/west of the Fraser as practicable "to keep the Russians at a distance," and to report on Russian vessel movements. A parallel commercial move — a **China-trade partnership with the East India Company** (1824, selling 20,000 beaver/7,000 sea otters annually) — proved unprofitable and was abandoned.
- On-the-ground expansion was modest given the harsh, unmapped terrain and small workforce: **Chief Trader William Brown** reached Babine Lake and built **Fort Kilmaurs (Babine Fort)** in 1822 — the Company's northernmost/westernmost post before 1825. A planned Chilcotin post was delayed until 1829 by unrest and warfare among local tribes. New Caledonia's total staff in 1822 was only ~44 (2 officers, 6 clerks, 2 interpreters, 34 men) — far too few for the aggressive expansion London wanted; local Indian hostility (e.g., the 1823 killing of two Company servants at Fort George) compounded the difficulty.
- **Pelly's negotiating leverage:** as the sole source of British information on the disputed territory, Pelly effectively dictated the facts (and sometimes inaccuracies, e.g. mis-locating Fraser's Lake by nearly a degree of latitude, based on outdated **Daniel Harmon** journal data) that shaped Canning's instructions to envoys **Sir Charles Bagot** and **Stratford Canning** in St. Petersburg. Nesselrode, the Russian foreign minister, explicitly told RAC director **Admiral Mordvinof**, "Great Britain, on her part, represents the rights of the Hudson's Bay Company."
- **HBC's core objectives**, all substantially achieved: (1) push Russian claims back at least to the 1799 ukase line, maximizing British expansion room; (2) secure transit rights between the interior and the Pacific; (3) preserve the Mackenzie River fur system entirely under Company control.
- **Negotiating chronology:** exploratory Wellington-Lieven talks at the Congress of Verona (1822); Canning initially hoped for joint Anglo-American pressure on Russia but abandoned this after discovering the US (via **Richard Rush**) sought to exploit the Russo-British dispute for its own Oregon claims; Canning then negotiated bilaterally. Pelly proposed a hierarchy of acceptable boundary lines (Chatham Strait → Stephen's Passage → a 50-100-mile coastal strip for Russia) — the last became the basis of the final settlement.
- The **Russo-American Convention (April 17, 1824)** set 54°40′N as the US claim's northern limit — Galbraith stresses this did _not_ force British "surrender," since Canning and Pelly had already decided to accept terms close to the Russian position before the news arrived; unlike the Russians, the British had no vital security interest riding on the exact line.
- **The critical concession:** the Russians agreed to **guarantee British subjects free navigation of rivers flowing through the Russian coastal strip to the interior** — a concession that, combined with the interior fur wealth the HBC already controlled, effectively let the Company outflank and undercut RAC's coastal middleman trade with interior Indians (fully exploited in Ch. 7). At the time (1824) the HBC had shown no interest in coastal trade, but Pelly's April 19, 1824 letter shows early awareness of this opportunity.
- **Treaty of February 28, 1825** — key terms: boundary begins at the southern tip of Prince of Wales Island (54°40′N), runs up Portland Canal to 56°N, then follows the crest of coastal mountains (never more than 10 marine leagues from the coast) north to the 141st meridian, then straight north to the Arctic Ocean. British subjects granted 10-year trading rights at Sitka and in Russian coastal waters south of Mt. St. Elias, plus a **perpetual right of interior river navigation**. Canning secured the 141° (rather than Russia's proposed 139°) meridian specifically to keep the boundary further from the Mackenzie River's mouth.
- **Galbraith's verdict:** despite superficial Russian "wins" on paper, the treaty was substantively a defeat for the RAC — its government had (in seeking to shield it from American smugglers) instead legalized American coastal trade for a decade and opened its interior fur supply to legalized HBC interception via the river-navigation clause. Russia's priority was really the Balkans/Near East, not North America, explaining Nesselrode's relatively easy concessions (quoted letter cautioning Mordvinof against letting "desirable acquisitions" override larger state interests).

- **Count Nesselrode** — Russian foreign minister.
- **Alexander Baranoff** — RAC's expansionist chief manager (to 1818).
- **Sir Charles Bagot / Stratford Canning** — British envoys in St. Petersburg.

- **William Brown** — established Fort Kilmaurs/Babine Fort (1822).
- **James Douglas** — later HBC officer; quoted assessing RAC mismanagement (1840).

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Ukase of 1821** — Russian decree claiming exclusive sovereignty/navigation rights north of 51°N.
- **En derouine trade** — peddling goods to Indian encampments rather than waiting at posts.
- **New Caledonia** — the HBC's interior British Columbia district.

**Important Dates:**

**Significance:** Demonstrates concretely the book's thesis that the HBC functioned as "Imperial Britain" in diplomatic practice — Pelly, not the Foreign Office, supplied the facts, priorities, and even proposed boundary lines that became British policy. Sets up Chapter 7's account of how the treaty's navigation clause was actually exploited against the Russians.

## Chapter 7: Competition on the Northwest Coast

_(pp. 135–155)_

**Overview:** Traces the HBC's campaign to dominate the maritime fur trade of the northwest coast between 1824 and 1839 — first driving out American "petty traders," then maneuvering (via the **Stikine/Dryad incident**) to pressure the Russian American Company into a **1839 lease agreement** that gave the HBC commercial control of the Russian mainland coastal strip.

**Key Points:**

- Even before the 1825 treaty was signed, the governor and committee ordered reconnaissance of the coast north of the Columbia (harbors, river navigability, Indian populations) — clear evidence the Company intended to enter the maritime trade regardless of North American officers' skepticism.
- Field officers (and initially Simpson himself) doubted maritime trade could be profitable, citing Russian difficulty holding even a foothold against coastal tribes; but Simpson's actual visit to the Columbia (1824–25) converted him into an enthusiast — his March 10, 1825 letter from Fort George predicts the coastal trade could **"yield double the profits"** of any other North American operation if properly managed and combined with New Caledonia's inland trade.
- **Strategic goal:** total monopoly of the Pacific fur trade from Mount St. Elias to California — targeting American "petty traders" first (weak capital, dependent on reselling to the Russians at Sitka), then turning on the Russian American Company (RAC) itself.
- **Coastal post chain (1827–1836):** **Fort Langley** (1827, Fraser River mouth) → post at the Nass River mouth (1831), relocated and renamed **Fort Simpson** (1834, after **Lieutenant Æmilius Simpson**, RN, the Company's marine superintendent and George Simpson's relative, who died in 1831) → **Fort McLoughlin** (1833, Lama Passage) — roughly 200 miles apart, reinforced by trading vessels **Cadboro** (1827), **Lama** (bought in Hawaii 1832), and the pioneering steamer **Beaver** (arrived 1836).
- **Price-differentiation strategy:** McLoughlin ran **three price tiers** — low in the protected interior, moderate on the coast absent competition, high on the coast when American ships were actually present — which created friction with interior Indians who learned of the disparity (e.g., Nez Percé anger at **Chief Trader Samuel Black**, Fort Walla Walla, 1829, when Indians discovered a blanket worth 5 skins inland was worth just 1 at Fort Vancouver).
- Standard 1830 coastal tariff (150% markup on invoice cost) is tabulated in the text — e.g., large beaver 20 shillings, sea otter large £6–£8 — with the Company absorbing real losses to win the price war: an estimated **£2,800 loss** on £13,000 of coastal fur purchases in 1833. Considered a worthwhile cost for eventual unchallenged monopoly.
- The Company also matched American traders in supplying **liquor, arms, and ammunition** despite the 1825 treaty's prohibition — Simpson's justification: "we must therefore either abandon the contest altogether, or follow the example of our opponents."
- By **1833 no American vessels remained on the coast**; sporadic reappearances occurred but sustained American opposition was effectively over. The RAC itself helped finish off the Americans by closing Russian waters to them in 1834 once the treaty's 10-year trading privilege expired.
- **Russian alarm:** RAC governor **Baron Ferdinand Wrangell** recognized by 1832 that the HBC, not the Americans, was the real long-term threat — quoted warning his board that superior HBC goods would let it capture "the whole fur trade in north-western America" unless matched.
- **Early HBC overtures to the Russians** (from 1824–25, formalized 1828–29 via Simpson) proposed supplying the RAC with manufactured goods and Fort Vancouver farm produce at cost — rebuffed by RAC governor **Captain Peter Chistakoff** (lacked authority) and coolly received in St. Petersburg, which also reminded London that HBC arms/liquor trading violated the 1825 treaty.
- **The Stikine/Dryad crisis (1834):** Based on a geographic misidentification (Lt. Æmilius Simpson wrongly believed the Stikine River was the Babine, and that a fort there could tap the same rich interior fur trade), the HBC planned a post at the Stikine River mouth — technically defensible under Article VI (free river navigation) and Article VII (10-year coastal trading rights) of the 1825 treaty. Wrangell, seeing the danger, pre-emptively built a Russian post (garrison of 20 under **Lieutenant Dionysius Zarembo**) at the Stikine mouth and, when **Peter Skene Ogden** arrived on the brig _Dryad_ (June 18, 1834) to establish the British post, Zarembo formally barred him from entering the river, citing (per Wrangell's instructions) a strained reading of **Article II** (governing landings at existing settlements) to justify blocking free navigation. Ogden withdrew, relocated the "new" **Fort Simpson** to a site south of the Portland Canal, and was further rebuffed when a wood-cutting party was driven off near **Tongass**.
- **Diplomatic aftermath:** McLoughlin tallied claimed damages (£22,150), and Pelly filed a formal protest (Oct. 1835) citing violations of Articles VI, VII, and XI (the latter banning use of armed force) — pursued vigorously in St. Petersburg by ambassador **Lord Durham** under Foreign Secretary **Palmerston**. **Count Nesselrode** essentially conceded the substance of the British case in his first reply to Durham (regretting the "authorities of the Russian settlements" had acted contrary to the government's wishes) even while, for two years, Russian negotiators used delaying arguments (Ogden's caution rather than genuine threat; the _Dryad_'s superior size; HBC's own treaty violations via liquor/arms trade) to avoid paying damages.
- **Parallel commercial track:** while pressing the damages claim, the HBC simultaneously kept renewing trade-supply offers to the RAC (via **Chief Factor Duncan Finlayson**'s 1836 visit to new RAC governor **Ivan Kupreanoff**, successor to Wrangell) — a "carrot and stick" approach. A fresh wave of aggressive American competition (ships **Peabody** and **LaGrange**, 1836) gave Russia additional incentive to consider a deal, since Kupreanoff was impressed by both HBC goods and the prospect of a reliable Columbia River wheat supply (versus unreliable California shipments).
- **Interior "back door" plan:** while negotiating, the Company simultaneously prepared to outflank the Russians from inland — Chief Trader **John McLeod** had found a river (named the **Pelly**) believed to be the upper Stikine; the Northern Council (June 1836) authorized building **Fort Drew** to intercept furs headed to the coast before they reached Russian middlemen — illustrating that Pelly/Simpson pursued both diplomatic _and_ commercial-military pressure simultaneously.
- **1838 St. Petersburg negotiations:** **Pelly and Simpson** traveled to St. Petersburg (Aug. 1838) — noting wryly the RAC's opacity and bureaucratic slowness ("three of them saints' days or holy days" delayed even translating a letter) — and negotiated directly with the RAC board (Wrangell now the board's dominant figure). Pelly's proposal: (1) RAC buys all its former-American-supplied goods from the HBC; (2) the companies stay out of each other's territory; (3) both end the arms/liquor trade. The clinching offer: the HBC would **sell the RAC the interior land furs it needed for its protected domestic Russian market**, letting the RAC re-sell them profitably duty-free — removing the RAC's main reason to resist ceding coastal trade.
- Meanwhile, in case talks failed, Pelly and Simpson had already planned a **large, secretly-organized expedition** (3 ships, 104 men, under **William Rae**) to seize the Stikine post site by force if necessary, per instructions to **James Douglas** to keep the plan secret (even suggesting the cover story that the real destination was Vancouver Island) and to take hostage Indian chiefs if needed. This expedition became unnecessary once the lease was agreed.
- **The 1839 Hamburg Agreement (signed February 6, 1839):** RAC leased the mainland coastal strip south of Cape Spencer to the HBC for **10 years from June 1, 1840**, for an annual rent of **2,000 land otter skins**; the HBC would sell the RAC further otter skins at fixed rates (23s./skin west of the Rockies, 32s./skin east); the HBC would supply the RAC with foodstuffs (wheat, peas, barley, butter, beef, ham) and British manufactured goods (freight at £13/ton); RAC promised 3 months' evacuation notice in case of war; and **the HBC dropped its Dryad damages claim** as part of the settlement.
- **Galbraith's assessment:** this was a purely **commercial** settlement negotiated directly between the two companies with minimal government involvement — neither the Russian nor British Foreign Office was informed until the deal was essentially done (Nesselrode learned only Dec. 30, 1838; Pelly told Palmerston only in vague terms on Jan. 4, 1839, and never disclosed the lease's actual terms — Palmerston had explicitly warned the Company had no authority to "barter away territorial or other rights of the British Crown," a warning effectively ignored). The agreement held until the 1867 sale of Alaska to the United States.

**Key Figures:**

- **Peter Skene Ogden** — led the failed Stikine expedition (Dryad incident, 1834).
- **Baron Ferdinand Wrangell** — RAC governor (to 1835), architect of Russian resistance at Stikine, later dominant RAC board member during the 1838–39 negotiations.
- **Lieutenant Dionysius Zarembo** — Russian officer who physically blocked Ogden at Stikine.
- **John Henry Pelly / George Simpson** — negotiated directly with the RAC board in St. Petersburg (1838) and concluded the Hamburg lease (Feb. 1839).
- **Lord Durham / Palmerston** — pursued the British damages claim diplomatically.
- **Count Nesselrode** — Russian foreign minister, conceded the substance of the British case but stalled on damages.
- **Ivan Kupreanoff** — Wrangell's successor as RAC colonial governor; receptive to trade terms.
- **Duncan Finlayson** — negotiated informally with Kupreanoff at Sitka (1836).
- **Æmilius Simpson** — HBC marine superintendent; founded Fort Langley/early coastal posts; his geographic error about the Stikine's identity precipitated the crisis.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Dryad incident (1834)** — the Stikine River confrontation between Ogden and Zarembo.

- **1824–25** — Simpson's first Columbia visit; early Russian trade overtures begin.
- **1827** — Fort Langley established.

**Significance:** Completes Part II by showing the endgame of Anglo-Russian fur-trade rivalry: a purely commercial settlement, negotiated company-to-company with minimal state oversight, that gave Britain (via the HBC) practical control of the Alaska panhandle's fur trade for three decades — reinforcing the book's thesis that the Company, not the Foreign Office, was the real architect of British policy on this frontier.

## Chapter 8: The Pacific Coast Monopoly, 1839–1867

_(pp. 156–174)_

**Overview:** Follows the HBC's Pacific coast monopoly from the 1839 lease through the 1867 sale of Alaska to the United States — covering the internal Simpson-McLoughlin dispute over post consolidation, the Company's controversial (and knowing) encroachment into Russian territory at **Fort Yukon**, the remarkable **Crimean War neutrality agreement**, the disruptive impact of the **Fraser River and Cariboo gold rushes**, and the lease's final unraveling after the Alaska Purchase.

**Key Points:**

- After 1839, freed from American and mutual competition, the HBC and RAC jointly lowered fur prices and phased out liquor sales (fully ended by an 1842 Simpson-Etholine agreement); a standard coast-wide tariff was introduced by **James Douglas** and RAC governor **Ivan Kupreanoff** in 1840.
- A detailed officer's calculation shows the **effective rent was much lower than the nominal 2,000 otter skins** — after accounting for HBC's profitable resale of otters back to the RAC at advantageous rates, the true annual cost came to only about **£150**.
- **Simpson vs. McLoughlin feud:** With competition gone, Simpson wanted to consolidate coastal posts (retain only Fort Simpson as a supply base, use the steamer _Beaver_ to collect furs coastwide) — McLoughlin bitterly opposed reducing establishments, a dispute that fed into his broader break with Simpson (developed further in Ch. 12).
- **California:** interest dated to 1829 (A. R. McLeod's Sacramento expedition) but never yielded much; the governor and committee explicitly declined to compete with the RAC's sea-otter trade there once informed (via Wrangell) that it was already unprofitably over-hunted — a rare instance of the Company voluntarily deferring to a rival's turf as a matter of "good faith."
- **Post consolidation on the leased strip:** Douglas took possession of **Fort Dionysius (renamed Stikine)** and began a post near the Taku River (1840). An ambitious original plan for a full network of interior _and_ coastal posts along the entire strip (to intercept furs directly from interior Indians, bypassing coastal middlemen) was abandoned by 1841 once exploration showed the country was less populated/valuable than assumed and the coastal-interior Indian trade network was too well established to break economically.
- **Northward expansion beyond the leased strip:** **Robert Campbell**, from Fort Halkett, explored into the Yukon (from 1839), reaching the Pelly River; built **Frances Lake post (1842)**, **Pelly Banks (1846)**, and **Fort Selkirk (1848)** at the Pelly-Lewes junction — pushing within 200 miles of the Russian 141° meridian boundary.
- **Fort Yukon (1847) — a deliberate, acknowledged treaty violation:** Based on **Chief Trader John Bell**'s 1844 scouting, clerk **Alexander H. Murray** built a post at the Porcupine-Yukon junction — more than 5° of longitude _inside_ Russian territory, a fact Murray himself explicitly acknowledged in his journal (quoted: he kept his knowledge "to myself" and maintained a night watch in case of Russian "surprise"). Pelly and Simpson knowingly rationalized this land-grab, instructing Murray to stay put unless Russia formally protested — no Russian appeared until **1863**. The Company held Fort Yukon until after the 1867 Alaska cession.
- Aside from Fort Yukon, an 1863 Russian government committee found the HBC had fulfilled its lease obligations "promptly and to the letter" — genuine mutual goodwill prevailed for most of the lease's life, though occasional food-supply shortfalls from Fort Vancouver caused minor friction.
- **Profitability:** the lease was privately judged very profitable by HBC directors (e.g., Outfit 1843: ~£8,000 in furs collected from the strip, plus ~£4,000/year profit on freight carried for the Russians, plus ~£750 from Columbia grain shipments) even though Pelly, in correspondence with Wrangell, consistently _downplayed_ profitability to avoid rent increases — a negotiating tactic on both sides (Wrangell reciprocally hinted the RAC board might not renew, likely also a bluff).
- **1849 renewal:** signed April 3, 1849, extending the lease **9 years from June 1, 1850** — dropped the foodstuffs/otter-sale provisions (unreliable Columbia supply, soon made moot by the California Gold Rush's disruption of Oregon's economy) but retained **Articles 4 and 5**, guaranteeing mutual honoring of contracts and a peaceful 3-month evacuation window in case of Anglo-Russian war — provisions that would prove crucial during the Crimean War.
- **The Crimean War neutrality agreement (1854):** As Britain and Russia drifted toward war, both companies feared for their Pacific possessions — Simpson recalled an informal 1841 conversation with RAC governor **Adolf Etholine** proposing that both companies lobby their governments to keep the northwest coast neutral, framed loftily as protecting their shared "civilizing" mission. The RAC (whose governor **Alexander Rudakov** admitted its forts could resist "savages" but were "almost useless" against real warships) formally proposed this to the HBC on **Feb. 14, 1854** (six weeks before Britain's formal declaration of war), already authorized by the Russian Foreign Office to pursue such a deal. The HBC — fearing loss of its steamers _Beaver_ and _Otter_ and its Victoria depot, and with no naval or military capacity of its own — enthusiastically agreed. **Foreign Office approval came March 22, 1854**, limited to land territory only (high seas and shipping remained open to capture, and the Kurile Islands/Petropavlovsk, outside the agreement, were in fact bombarded by Allied forces on September 2, 1855, demonstrating what Sitka would have faced without the deal).
- Galbraith explicitly rejects the "legend" that the two governments cynically subordinated national interest to two companies' commercial interests — rather, neither government had any strategic reason to fight over this remote, low-value theater, so accommodating the companies cost nothing. Colonial Governor **James Douglas**'s request for £-funded troops to defend Vancouver Island was refused by the Colonial Office, which offered only periodic naval patrols — confirming Britain's minimal genuine stake in the region.
- The neutrality period actually **strengthened** HBC's coastal position, since Russian trading capacity was hampered by the war while the Company traded uninterrupted — it even rented ice-cutting rights near Taku to a San Francisco firm throughout the war.
- **1858 lease renewal** (through Jan. 1, 1862) reserved ice/timber/coal/fish rights to Russia, reflecting new San Francisco demand for these commodities — a minor but telling sign of change.
- **The Fraser River and Cariboo gold rushes (1858 onward)** transformed the region: ~10,000 miners flooded the Fraser valley within months; **New Caledonia formally became the colony of British Columbia** in 1858, the same year the Company's exclusive trading license expired — Galbraith calls this a fitting historical coincidence, marking "the end of the fur-trade domination of the northwest coast."
- Gold-rush petty traders undercut the Company's monopoly prices in both British Columbia and the leased Russian strip; by 1861 the RAC, admitting it had "neither the power nor the means" to police the leased territory against interlopers, could only offer to send a ship "to take measures of repression" after being notified — ineffective in practice. The lease was extended to 1863 mainly to allow time for reconsideration.
- By 1862, worsening competition (including reports of Indians reselling Company goods to petty traders "at one-tenth their value" in exchange for liquor) led the governor and committee to formally announce **non-renewal** — but a subsequent partial recovery (driven by the second, Cariboo, gold rush and a corresponding decline of petty-trader activity) led them to reverse course again and extend for one-to-two-year terms through 1865–66.
- **1865 Rutkovski negotiations:** RAC agent **A. Rutkovski** proposed either an outright sale of the leased territory to the HBC, or an _expanded_ lease (full mainland and island fur-trade monopoly south of Mt. St. Elias) at **£3,000/year** for 5 years, with Russia retaining Sitka, ice/lumber/fisheries rights on the islands, and acting merely as HBC's trading agent there. The HBC, uncertain about future profitability, countered at £2,000/year; no agreement was reached before the **1867 Alaska Purchase** intervened.
- **Post-1867 American friction:** the Company's steamer _Otter_, attempting to exercise the old Russian-treaty river-navigation right up the Stikine, was blocked by petty but effective American customs harassment (forced 360-mile round trip to Sitka for clearance; charged **$150 "lighthouse dues"** for a nonexistent lighthouse — twice). The British Foreign Office declined to intervene, ruling that only the _boundary_ provisions of the 1825 treaty (not trade privileges) bound the US as Russia's successor — and was in any case preoccupied with the **Alabama claims** dispute with Washington.
- **Chapter/Part II closing synthesis:** even without the Alaska cession, mounting settler/miner competition in British Columbia would likely have forced the Company to abandon the coastal lease anyway — the fur trade, as everywhere else in the book, could not survive contact with a settled, mining, or agricultural economy. The chapter (and Part II) closes with the book's recurring thesis in miniature: British Columbia became British largely because of the exploration and diplomatic groundwork laid by fur traders pursuing profit, not by any deliberate colonial policy.

**Key Figures:**

- **James Douglas** — implemented the 1839 agreement on the ground; later governor of Vancouver Island.
- **Robert Campbell** — explored/established the Yukon posts (Frances Lake, Pelly Banks, Fort Selkirk).
- **Alexander H. Murray** — built Fort Yukon (1847), knowingly inside Russian territory.
- **Baron Wrangell / Adolf Etholine / Ivan Kupreanoff / Alexander Rudakov** — successive RAC governors/officials.
- **A. Rutkovski** — RAC's 1865 negotiating agent in London.
- **Sir George Simpson** — proposed the Crimean War neutrality arrangement (from an earlier 1841 conversation).
- **Edward de Stoeckl** — Russian minister in Washington during the Crimean War period.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Fort Yukon** — HBC post built knowingly inside Russian America (1847–1869).
- **Neutrality agreement of 1854** — Crimean War-era arrangement quarantining the northwest coast from hostilities.
- **Fraser River/Cariboo gold rushes** — the events that ended the fur-trade era on the coast (1858 onward).
- **New Caledonia → British Columbia** — the 1858 transition from fur-trade district to Crown colony.

**Important Dates:**

- **1839–40** — Lease takes effect; standardized coastal tariff introduced.
- **1842** — Simpson-Etholine liquor-trade agreement.
- **1847** — Fort Yukon established.
- **April 3, 1849** — Lease renewed (9 years from June 1850).
- **1854** — Crimean War outbreak; Pacific neutrality agreement (March 22).
- **September 2, 1855** — Allied bombardment of Urup/Petropavlovsk (outside the neutral zone).
- **1858** — Lease renewed; Fraser River gold rush; New Caledonia becomes British Columbia; exclusive trading license expires.
- **1861–63** — Petty-trader crisis in the leased territory; lease extended to 1863.
- **1865–66** — Rutkovski negotiations; short-term lease extensions.
- **1867** — Alaska cession to the United States; _Otter_ incident.

**Significance:** Closes Part II by tracing the Pacific coast monopoly from its commercial peak through its dissolution amid gold rushes and the transfer of Alaska — reinforcing that fur-trade diplomacy (Anglo-Russian) and fur-trade collapse (before settlement/mining) followed the same pattern seen on every other frontier in the book, and sets the stage for Part III's parallel story in Oregon.

# PART III — THE OREGON QUESTION AND ITS AFTERMATH

## Chapter 9: The Oregon Dispute, 1821–1838

_(pp. 177–191)_

**Overview:** Opens Part III by explaining why, unlike the Russian negotiation, the Anglo-American Oregon dispute ultimately went against Britain despite the HBC's near-total commercial dominance of the region — American "manifest destiny" sentiment and strategic interest in Puget Sound outweighed British willingness to fight for a fur-trade district. Covers the diplomacy of the 1820s under Canning and its collapse into two decades of stalemate.

**Key Points:**

- Unlike the Russian dispute, the US had virtually no significant fur-trade economic interest in Oregon after 1825 (land competition) or 1830 (maritime) — yet the US ultimately won the boundary. Key US motives beyond economics: (1) the strategic/commercial value of **Puget Sound's harbors**, recognized from **John Quincy Adams to James Buchanan**; (2) the ideological force of **"manifest destiny."**
- British claims derived, as with Russia, from fur-trade activity: the **Nootka Sound Convention (1790)** arose from a fur-trader/Spanish clash (Meares vs. Martinez); the **1818 "joint occupation" convention** protected NWC trade; after 1821 the HBC became the chief interest shaping British policy through 1846.
- Despite being unpopular domestically as a chartered monopoly in a free-trade era, the Company still commanded British government support against _foreign_ rivals — a recurring theme.
- **British goal:** a boundary along the **Columbia River** from the 49th parallel to the Pacific — sacrificing the (already fur-depleted) Snake country and southern Oregon, while securing "undisputed possession of the whole country on the right [north] bank of the upper Columbia" and free navigation of the river (Canning's stated priorities).
- **1822** — false rumor of an American colonizing party (spurred by expansionist Congressman **John Floyd**) caused brief alarm, then subsided.
- **1824 negotiations:** **John Quincy Adams** instructed minister **Richard Rush** to propose (April 3, 1824, just before the Russo-American treaty) a 10-year mutual non-settlement pact north of 51°/south of 55° — a bargaining tactic Canning saw through. Canning (likely after consulting Pelly) instead proposed the Company's preferred line: 49th parallel to the **Columbia's main northeast branch ("McGillivray's River")**, then down the Columbia to the Pacific. Deadlock resulted.
- **Fort George/Astoria repositioning:** Because Fort George sat on the south (soon-to-be-American) bank, Canning urged voluntary relocation north of the river as a goodwill/strategic gesture — even though Canning privately admitted Britain's original 1818 restitution of Astoria to the US had been done on legally dubious grounds ("absolutely unjustifiable, and will not bear the light of discussion"). Simpson was tasked with the move; after his 1824 and 1828 surveys found the Fraser River unnavigable for this purpose, the Company instead built **Fort Vancouver** (near the Willamette-Columbia confluence, begun winter 1824–25, formally named March 19, 1825 by Simpson to honor Vancouver's discoveries) — chosen partly for good agricultural soil, which later became useful both practically and as a diplomatic argument (settled agriculture strengthening British occupation claims). Fort George was abandoned **June 7, 1825**, and left to decay (visited in ruins by botanist **John Scouler**) until briefly reoccupied in 1829 to compete with Dominis's ships.
- Attempted relocation of **Fort Nez Percés (Walla Walla)** north of the river was abandoned after local Nez Percé chiefs threatened **Chief Trader Samuel Black** — trade dependence on Nez Percé goodwill trumped London's symbolic preferences.
- **December 1825 — Pelly/Simpson letter to Canning:** proposed an even more concessive boundary (49th parallel south along the height of land to where Lewis and Clark crossed, then along the Snake/Lewis River to the Columbia and the sea) — a deliberate opening bid designed to be negotiated up to the Company's real target (the Columbia line).
- **1826 negotiations (Gallatin vs. Huskisson/Addington):** Canning, motivated as much by broader vision of a British Pacific trading empire (quoted 1826 letter to Liverpool anticipating the end of the East India Company's monopoly and envisioning direct China trade via a British NW Coast colony) as by fur-trade interests, pushed for a strong stance — but cabinet colleagues, especially free-trade reformer **William Huskisson** (Board of Trade president and a negotiator), were lukewarm. Huskisson offered Gallatin a compromise triangle around the Olympic Peninsula (Gray's Harbor to Juan de Fuca Strait) — rejected by Gallatin, who in turn rejected British navigation rights on a 49th-parallel-based line. Per Gallatin's own account, Huskisson undermined Britain's negotiating position by essentially conceding that Oregon might "gradually and silently slide into the hands of the United States" and that national pride, not real interest, was the main British stake. Negotiations ended in stalemate; the **1818 joint-occupation convention was extended indefinitely** (August 1827), terminable on one year's notice by either party.
- The renewed convention favored the US: it left the HBC in practical control, but the effective bargaining position could only erode over time, since war was the only way either side could force a decisive settlement, and neither government (in the 1820s) was willing to fight over Oregon.
- **George Canning died the same day** the 1827 convention was signed — symbolically ending vigorous British advocacy for the Company's Oregon claims; no later foreign secretary matched his commitment.
- **Post-1828 policy shift:** governor and committee, regretting their earlier concession of moving posts north of the river, instructed Simpson to maximize occupation on _both_ banks (to have "something to give up" in eventual negotiations) — but resources devoted were minimal (only 224 men/21 officers on the entire Columbia). Only one new post was built south of the river: **Fort Umpqua** (1832). The Company's real energy went instead into the Pacific coastal trade and Russian competition (Chs. 6–8), not southern Oregon occupation — because occupation for its own sake wasn't profitable.
- **Core continued strategy:** discourage American _fur traders_ specifically (per Ch. 5's Snake country "fur desert" logic), while London urged McLoughlin to stop being so hospitable to American _visitors_ — approved aid to genuinely desperate parties (e.g., survivors of the Jedediah Smith Umpqua massacre) but disapproved of open-handed treatment of purposeful colonizers like Wyeth and missionary **Jason Lee** (who arrived with Wyeth in 1834, settled on the Willamette with McLoughlin's active assistance despite London's preference for cool distance).
- Galbraith explicitly rejects the theory that McLoughlin's generosity reflected pro-American political sympathy (sometimes inferred from his later sympathy for the 1837 Canadian rebels) — McLoughlin's motivations were **purely commercial/humanitarian**, not political; he simply didn't grasp (or care about) the diplomatic implications London worried about. This tension is the seed of the McLoughlin-Simpson/London rupture developed in later chapters.
- **Turning point — 1836–38:** the Company's assumption that discouraging fur traders would delay settlement proved false. **William A. Slacum**'s December 1836 visit (sent by Secretary of State **John Forsyth** to gather intelligence for the US) signaled renewed federal interest; more importantly, the small but growing **Willamette Valley American settlement** (by 1838: 28 adult American men including 10 clergy, 6 American wives, plus 23 former Company Canadian freemen) alarmed the Company — quoting **James Douglas**'s warning that these "restless Americans," if their community prospered, could exert "a greater influence than desirable over our affairs."
- This alarm prompted the governor and committee to summon **McLoughlin and Simpson to London** for conferences at the end of 1838, resulting in a new strategy: promote **Company-controlled** agricultural settlement north of the Columbia, to preempt independent American colonization — the origin of the **Puget's Sound Agricultural Company** (Chapter 10).

**Key Figures:**

- **George Canning** — foreign secretary; the Company's strongest diplomatic advocate; died 1827.
- **John Quincy Adams** — architect of persistent American pressure for a favorable boundary.
- **William Huskisson** — free-trade-minded British negotiator whose lukewarm stance undercut the British case in 1826.
- **Albert Gallatin** — American negotiator, 1826–27.
- **John McLoughlin** — Chief Factor, Columbia; criticized by London for excessive hospitality to Americans.
- **Jason Lee** — missionary/settler, Willamette Valley.
- **William A. Slacum** — US government agent/observer, 1836.
- **James Douglas** — warned London about the growing American settlement's influence.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **"Manifest destiny"** — American ideological driver of the Oregon claim.
- **Joint occupation convention (1818, renewed 1827)** — the governing Anglo-American arrangement pending final settlement.
- **Fort Vancouver** — new Columbia district HQ (est. 1825), replacing Fort George/Astoria.
- **Puget's Sound Agricultural Company** — the Company's 1838-launched response to American settlement (Chapter 10).

**Important Dates:**

- **1818** — Joint occupation convention.
- **1821** — HBC-NWC merger; Company inherits Oregon trade.
- **April 1824** — Rush's 10-year non-settlement proposal; Canning's Columbia-line counter-proposal.
- **March 19, 1825** — Fort Vancouver founded.
- **June 7, 1825** — Fort George abandoned.
- **December 1825** — Pelly/Simpson concessive boundary proposal.
- **1826–27** — Gallatin-Huskisson/Addington negotiations; stalemate.
- **August 1827** — Joint occupation convention renewed indefinitely; Canning dies.
- **1832** — Fort Umpqua built.
- **1834** — Jason Lee settles in the Willamette Valley.
- **December 1836** — Slacum's visit.
- **1838** — Willamette settlement reaches ~28 American men; McLoughlin/Simpson summoned to London.

**Significance:** Establishes why the Oregon dispute, despite the HBC's commercial supremacy, was destined to end differently from the Russian settlement — American nationalist sentiment and strategic ambition (not economic competition) were the deciding forces, a dynamic the Company could influence but never fully control. Sets up the Puget's Sound Agricultural Company (Ch. 10) as the Company's belated, ultimately unsuccessful attempt to beat American settlers at their own game.

## Chapter 10: The Puget's Sound Agricultural Company

_(pp. 192–217)_

**Overview:** A case study in the limits of a fur-trading monopoly as a tool of colonization. The Company's attempt (from 1838–39) to counter American settlement by planting its own British agricultural colony north of the Columbia — the **Puget's Sound Agricultural Company (PSAC)** — is shown to have failed both commercially and politically, because the Company's paternalistic, profit-driven structure was fundamentally incompatible with the free land ownership that actually attracted settlers to North America.

**Key Points:**

- **Origins in subsistence farming:** the Company had long encouraged posts to grow their own food; **Fort Vancouver** (chosen partly for its agricultural soil) was highly productive early on — by end of 1828: 153 cattle, 50 goats, ~200 hogs, plus substantial grain and potato harvests — which fed Simpson's hope (Ch. 8) of a barter-supply arrangement with the Russians.
- **The "Oragon Beef & Tallow Company" (1832):** McLoughlin and other Columbia officers, seeing opportunity in Hawaii's hide/tallow trade and cheap California cattle, tried to launch a _private_, _personal_ joint-stock cattle venture (capital £3,000) — separate from the HBC.
- When news reached London (fall 1834), the governor and committee's reaction was to **appropriate the idea for the Company itself** rather than let McLoughlin profit privately — Simpson argued cattle raising could be hugely profitable "if taken up by the Company" but not by "a Joint Stock Association or Individual." They insisted the herds be pastured **north of the Columbia** (since the Willamette was assumed to be lost to the US), suggested searching Whidbey Island/Puget Sound for a healthier alternative to disease-prone Fort Vancouver, and proposed starting with **5,000 cows** at once. Tellingly, Simpson's enthusiasm was undiminished by the earlier failure of similar cattle schemes at Red River.
- McLoughlin, no longer personally incentivized, slow-walked the project — using the 1834 Stikine/Russian crisis (Ch. 7) as a convenient distraction/excuse — and it stalled until 1837–38.
- **1837 license renewal:** with the license set to expire in 1842 but political conditions uncertain (a shaky Whig government, uncertain Conservative sympathy, Ellice's declining influence), Pelly pushed to renew early. His pitch to Colonial Secretary **Lord Glenelg** (Feb. 1837) went beyond the old "peace and order" argument — he now promised the Company would actively promote **agriculture, wool/tallow/hide exports, and settlement of retired servants and other emigrants**, framing this as securing "British interests and British influence" on the Pacific coast. **James Stephen** (permanent undersecretary) got Glenelg to insist the Crown retain the right to found Crown colonies within Company territory as a condition. New 21-year license issued **May 30, 1838** (monopoly against other British subjects only — Americans remained free to trade per treaty).
- This new colonization pledge created a new obligation: the Company now had to defend "British paramountcy" in Oregon through actual settlement, not just fur-trade competition.
- **February 1838 — Linn's Bill:** Senator **Lewis F. Linn** introduced Senate legislation for US territorial government and military occupation of Oregon — alarmed London enough to prompt defensive measures: Simpson instructed **James Douglas** (March 1838) to seize strategic points (Willamette River mouth area, Tongue Point, Multnomah Island) with token cultivation/habitation, partly to _provoke_ an incident that might force British government intervention. Linn's bill failed, but the sense of urgency persisted, feeding into the late-1838 London conferences (end of Ch. 9) that created the PSAC.
- **Legal workaround:** Company lawyers ruled the HBC's own charter forbade investing capital in agriculture, so a **satellite company, the Puget's Sound Agricultural Company**, was created — agents Pelly, Colvile, Simpson; stock open only to HBC shareholders/officers (one PSAC share per £300 of HBC stock held); authorized capital £200,000. **McLoughlin, ironically, was made responsible for managing the very enterprise that had displaced his personal scheme**, compensated with an extra £500/year (from 1869) — his private skepticism is captured in a quoted letter to Douglas predicting individuals might profit from Oregon cattle-raising but the Company itself would not.
- Stockholder response was tepid (Selkirk 164 shares, Harrison 56, Colvile 11, Berens family 22 — essentially only insiders bought in; North American officers bought shares mainly out of obligation, not genuine confidence).
- **Political rather than commercial logic dominated**, as **Andrew Colvile**'s 1841 letter to Simpson makes explicit: encourage settlement north of the Columbia to "anticipate the plans of the American Missionaries," and Colvile openly speculated that the boundary dispute might only be resolved by war, since neither government would willingly negotiate during peacetime.
- **PSAC stations:** **Cowlitz Farm** (Cowlitz-Chehalis portage, ~3,000 cultivable acres; 200 acres plowed and 275 bushels wheat sown in 1839 under **Chief Trader John Todd**) and **Fort Nisqually** (built 1833 as a fur post on the strength of an overly optimistic soil report by **Archibald McDonald**; assigned jointly to the PSAC in 1839 as its main pastoral station). Results were mixed: by 1841 Nisqually had 4,530 sheep/1,000 cattle; Cowlitz produced ~8,000 bushels wheat and 4,000 oats; by 1845 Nisqually held 2,280 cattle and 5,872 sheep. Livestock came mostly from California ("Spanish cattle" — hardy, prolific, but poor meat/milk producers); efforts to improve sheep breeding stock with English merino/Southdown/Cheviot/Leicester imports.
- **Settler recruitment attempts and their failure:**
    - **Willamette Valley Canadians (retired HBC servants):** targeted for relocation to the Cowlitz, both to strengthen British claims north of the river and to remove them from American Methodist missionary influence. The Company used **Roman Catholic missionaries** as a political tool — agreeing to transport priests **Francis N. Blanchet and Modeste Demers** (from 1838) specifically in exchange for their promise to encourage Canadian freemen to relocate to British territory and trade exclusively with the Company. Catholic influence did produce one clear win: Canadians refused to join Americans in petitioning Congress for US jurisdiction over Oregon (unlike two earlier joint petitions) — but most freemen still preferred to stay in the Willamette rather than move to the Cowlitz.
    - **American Methodist missionaries (Jason Lee et al.)** were viewed with suspicion — unlike Presbyterian missionaries (who pledged not to trade furs), the Methodists actively pursued trade and colonization interests, seen by Douglas and London as harboring "secret views, at variance with our interests."
    - **British/Scottish emigrants directly from home:** Edward Ellice touted Scots as superior agricultural emigrants; but the Company's proposed terms (family gets a house, 100 cleared acres, livestock — but the Company keeps the land and takes half of all produce/stock increase) were fundamentally unattractive by North American standards, and Simpson's boasts that "thousands" would apply if only informed were never tested since **no public advertising was undertaken** and no British emigrants were ever actually sent this way.
    - **False alarm — Massachusetts "Oregon Provisional Emigration Society":** Simpson, misled by Jason Lee, believed (based on a March 1839 letter from society secretary **Rev. F. P. Tracy**) that "several thousand" American emigrants were imminent, including a hint the settlers might found an independent Oregon republic. This report — which never materialized — was passed up through the Colonial and Foreign Offices, both of which agreed action might eventually be needed but took none.
    - **Red River migration (1841):** the Company's actual solution — pull "respectable" families from the troublesome Red River settlement (both to shore up Oregon _and_ relieve pressure on Rupert's Land, per Ch. 4). **James Sinclair** led a wagon train of **23 families (121 people)** that left Red River in June 1841 (witnessed en route by Simpson himself, who described the mile-long train of carts). Only **21 families (116 people)** reached Fort Vancouver; **14 (77 people, mostly English half-breeds)** were sent to Nisqually, the rest (French Canadian/half-breed, mostly buffalo hunters rather than farmers) to Cowlitz.
    - Even this small migration (roughly matching the entire American population in Oregon at the time — only 65 American families vs. Oregon's total population of ~500, of whom 350 were Canadian) proved to be **the only migration the Company ever sponsored** — Simpson and McLoughlin decided against further transfers even before results came in.
    - **Failure at Nisqually:** poor sandy soil, inexperienced Red River farmers, sheep scab (500 ewes lost their lambs) — by autumn 1843 the Nisqually families had left for the Willamette (officer **William Fraser Tolmie** called them "insolent and thriftless" without regret). Cowlitz families mostly stayed, likely helped by more fertile soil and the presence of a resident Catholic priest (Demers) — Nisqually's Methodist missionary, Rev. J. P. Richmond, also gave up and left in 1842.
- **Simpson's reversal:** by March 1842 Simpson explicitly warned McLoughlin against a "mania" among Company employees for permission to retire and settle on the coast, fearing serious inconvenience to the fur trade — the Company's colonization policy shifted decisively from encouragement to obstruction of _all_ immigration, British or American.
- **Missed opportunity — Catholic missions:** Chief Factor **James Douglas** argued (with unusual foresight) that the Company should have generously subsidized Catholic missionary transport as a cheap way to buy goodwill and encourage settlement, quoting his own memorable warning that, like the fall of Rome after withdrawing its legions, "there is danger in receding; strength, power and safety are to be found only in a bold advance." But Simpson and the board, wary of missionaries generally (blamed for stirring unrest and criticism of Company practices at Red River), repeatedly slow-walked and eventually only grudgingly assisted Catholic clergy transport (e.g., rejecting Bishop Provencher's 1840 request outright; later cooperating minimally in 1843–45 mainly to avoid alienating the Church, not from genuine enthusiasm). **McLoughlin himself converted to Catholicism in 1842** and personally aided priests despite official policy.
- **Commercial failure, too:** by 1845, PSAC livestock (2,280 cattle, 5,872 sheep at Nisqually) were of poor quality due to inexperienced management; a 1844 London wool shipment was graded wildly inconsistent ("fine" to "very coarse"); Simpson judged the wild, hard-to-collect cattle essentially worthless except as literal "produce of the chase, like the buffalo." Grain output was sometimes insufficient even to meet the Russian supply contract (Ch. 8). **McLoughlin's original private skepticism was thus vindicated** — the PSAC failed as both a political and a commercial venture.

**Key Figures:**

- **John McLoughlin** — originator of the cattle idea (as a private venture), later reluctant manager of the Company's version.
- **George Simpson** — chief enthusiast and architect of PSAC colonization policy, later its chief skeptic.
- **John Henry Pelly / Andrew Colvile** — PSAC agents; Pelly secured the 1838 license renewal on a colonization pledge.
- **James Douglas** — advocated (unsuccessfully) for more generous support of Catholic missions as a cheap political investment.
- **Francis N. Blanchet / Modeste Demers** — Catholic missionaries used as a political tool against American Methodist influence.
- **Jason Lee** — American Methodist missionary; source of Simpson's mistaken belief in imminent mass American emigration.
- **James Sinclair** — led the 1841 Red River emigrant party to Oregon.
- **William Fraser Tolmie** — officer at Fort Nisqually; witnessed the Red River settlers' failure and departure.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Puget's Sound Agricultural Company (PSAC)** — HBC's satellite colonization/pastoral company (est. 1839–40).
- **Farms "on halves"** — the PSAC's paternalistic tenancy system (Company keeps land, takes half of produce/stock increase).
- **Oregon Provisional Emigration Society** — the (largely illusory) Massachusetts group that alarmed Simpson.
- **Linn's Bill (1838)** — US Senate legislation asserting territorial claims over Oregon.

**Important Dates:**

- **1832** — "Oragon Beef & Tallow Company" prospectus (McLoughlin's private venture).
- **1834** — Governor/committee appropriates the cattle-raising idea for the Company.
- **February 1837 – May 30, 1838** — License renewal negotiations; new 21-year license issued.
- **February 1838** — Linn's Bill introduced.
- **Late 1838** — London conferences create the PSAC.
- **1839** — Cowlitz Farm and Fort Nisqually assigned to PSAC.
- **March 1839** — Tracy's letter (Oregon Provisional Emigration Society).
- **June 1841** — Red River emigrant party departs (arrives at Fort Vancouver later that year).
- **March 1842** — Simpson turns against further immigration.
- **1842–43** — Nisqually settlers depart for the Willamette; PSAC revealed as a commercial failure.

**Significance:** Demonstrates the structural limits of the Company as a political instrument: its paternalistic land-tenure system, suspicion of missionaries, and profit-driven caution made it fundamentally unable to compete with the free-settlement model driving American westward expansion — directly foreshadowing Britain's diplomatic defeat in the Oregon boundary settlement covered in the next two chapters.

## Chapter 11: The Oregon Crisis, 1840–1844

_(pp. 218–236)_

**Overview:** Covers the years in which the Oregon dispute escalated from a dormant diplomatic issue into a genuine national crisis, driven not by the Company's declining fur-trade position but by surging American emigration, the "Oregon fever," and shifting British political attention (Palmerston → Aberdeen). Introduces the crucial concept of **"possessory rights"** — the Company's fallback strategy once a Columbia River boundary began to look unattainable.

**Key Points:**

- Opening thesis: Oregon was won for the US not primarily by pioneers or missionaries directly, but by the _political climate_ ("Oregon fever") they created; Galbraith credits the HBC's continuous pressure with keeping the dispute alive as a major diplomatic issue at all (quoting historian **Lester Burrell Shippee**: British interest in Oregon was "centered about the Company").
- **1839 — Linn revives the issue:** Senator **Lewis Linn**'s resolutions (echoing Floyd's from decades earlier, but landing in a changed climate) proposed territorial status for Oregon, 640-acre land grants for settlers, notice of termination of the 1827 joint-occupation convention, and a military regiment for the territory. News reached Pelly (via the _St. John's Courier_, Feb. 1840), prompting an urgent appeal to Foreign Secretary **Palmerston** warning of ruin to both the HBC and PSAC and stressing the broader "command of the Northern Pacific" stakes (deliberately appealing to Palmerston's assertive style). Linn's resolutions failed to pass, but Colonial Office official **James Stephen** noted the dispute was clearly reviving.
- **The Belcher incident:** a critical report from **Commander Edward Belcher** (HMS _Sulphur_) wrongly accused the Company of aiding American missionary-agents undermining British interests; this prompted an unwarranted Colonial Office lecture (via **Lord John Russell**) urging the Company to employ British missionaries instead — Galbraith notes this reveals how poorly informed the Foreign/Colonial Offices actually were about conditions in Oregon.
- **Political drift:** the best chance for a British-favorable settlement was to force a crisis while the Company still held physical dominance and before American opinion hardened irreversibly — but Palmerston, though bellicose in temperament, took no initiating action; his fall from office (**August 1841**) and replacement by the conciliatory **Lord Aberdeen** made eventual American victory "virtually a certainty."
- **Rising local militancy:** a February 1841 Willamette meeting (chaired by Jason Lee) to organize an American government was discouraged by visiting US Navy officer **Lieutenant Charles Wilkes** as premature, but alarmed **James Douglas**, who predicted (correctly) that such efforts would recur.
- **Simpson's 1841–42 pessimism:** touring Oregon in 1841, Simpson found missionary-driven settlement expansion alarming and recommended moving some Fort Vancouver functions north — to the southern tip of **Vancouver Island**. In a private March 1, 1842 letter to the governor and committee (quoted at length), Simpson candidly predicted Britain might ultimately have to settle for a boundary through the **Strait of Juan de Fuca** (ceding Puget Sound and Hood's Canal) rather than the Columbia line — though in simultaneous _official_ correspondence meant for the Foreign Office's eyes, he insisted Britain must never concede territory north of the Columbia. A Company officer at Red River, **Adam Thom**, criticized Simpson for committing such defeatist speculation to paper at all, given the risk of it falling into American hands. This dual-track candor/advocacy exemplifies Simpson's opportunistic communication style noted elsewhere in the book.
- **Fort Victoria (1843):** built on southern Vancouver Island — Galbraith attributes this primarily to ordinary commercial logic (better location for coastal trade, discussed since the 1830s) rather than confirmation of retreat, since the governor and committee's motives differed from Simpson's private pessimism.
- **Lord Aberdeen's priorities:** Aberdeen (foreign secretary from 1841) considered the **Maine–New Brunswick boundary dispute** (resolved by **Lord Ashburton**'s 1842 treaty with Daniel Webster) far more consequential than Oregon, which he saw as low-risk and low-value; his assessment reflected genuine British and American public opinion at the time (Oregon mattered mainly to the Company, missionaries, and a vocal expansionist faction).
- **Webster's private views**: had he been free of Congressional constraints, **Daniel Webster** might have accepted a line giving Britain most of what the Company wanted — his 1842 letter to Everett sketches essentially the eventual 1846 compromise (Juan de Fuca Strait to the Columbia, then along the 49th parallel) — but he left office without formalizing any Oregon settlement.
- **1843 — the turning point year:**
    - **Cincinnati Convention (July 3–5, 1843):** Mississippi Valley state delegates (chaired by **Richard M. Johnson**, former US VP) demanded the full Oregon claim to 54°40′ and a chain of protective forts — a clear sign of hardening sectional/national opinion.
    - **Dr. Elijah White's party (1842)** — first sizable American migration (~100 people); White's self-appointed authority as "Sub-Indian Agent" was rejected by the Company but fed provisional-government agitation.
    - **The Great Migration of 1843:** 500–1,000 settlers (Douglas's informal census estimated 500–700, ~300 adult males based on 300 guns counted), 120 wagons, ~1,600 cattle. Most lacked capital. **McLoughlin extended credit** for seed and supplies to the newcomers — a direct, knowing violation of Company policy, justified (per Galbraith) by the practical reality that a desperate, well-armed body of immigrants could otherwise have simply seized Company supplies by force; many of those helped became genuinely friendly to the Company. Douglas, more cautious, would have preferred pushing penniless settlers on toward California rather than aiding them, though he did not directly fault McLoughlin.
    - **Douglas's clear-eyed assessment** (quoted at length): endorsed **John C. Calhoun**'s view that unresolved jurisdiction would be settled by simple demographic weight in America's favor, and warned Simpson that only decisive British action (peace settlement or war) could prevent an American Oregon from becoming a future naval/privateering threat to British Pacific trade. Also offers a memorably candid, mixed assessment of "these Yankees" — "admirable in some respects, in others detestable" — admiring their frontier rule-of-law pragmatism despite disdaining their manners.
    - **Champoeg meeting (May 2, 1843)** and adoption (**July 5, 1843**) of a provisional government based on the Iowa legal code — Canadians (led by Blanchet's continued influence) initially refused to join but, as their share of the population shrank, both Canadians (**March 1844**, with reservations preserving British-subject status) and eventually the Company itself (**McLoughlin, summer 1845**, "without prejudice" to British sovereignty claims) accepted the provisional government's practical jurisdiction to protect property and order.
- **Origin of "possessory rights":** on **October 23, 1843**, Douglas proposed to Simpson — for the first time on record — that if the Columbia boundary was lost, the Company's treaty rights to its actual physical "improvements" (buildings, farms, etc.) must be explicitly protected, since Congress alone would show the Company no mercy ("our doom is sealed... expulsion without benefit of justice"). Simpson echoed a similar but narrower point (focused on Willamette Falls property) in June 1844. Galbraith credits **Douglas**, not Simpson, as the true originator of the "possessory rights" principle later written into the 1846 treaty — the subject of Chapter 13.
- **Executive escalation — Tyler's December 5, 1843 message to Congress:** President **John Tyler**, angling for renomination, publicly claimed the full 42°–54°40′ territory and called for military posts to protect emigrants — a marked escalation from earlier purely Congressional agitation.
- **Aberdeen's private flexibility:** in March 1844, Aberdeen privately told Minister **Richard Pakenham** he'd personally accept the **49th parallel** as the boundary, provided Britain kept the whole of Vancouver Island — while still refusing to be bullied by American threats of force, and (for the first time) beginning direct consultations with the governor and committee, less out of sympathy for Company interests than to avoid appearing intimidated by the US.
- **Simpson's parallel diplomacy:** cultivated Pakenham and Canadian Governor-General **Sir Charles Metcalfe** directly (via an **Adam Thom** pamphlet and personal correspondence), proposing a free port for the US at southern Vancouver Island or the Strait of Juan de Fuca — a suggestion Pakenham later put to Secretary of State **John C. Calhoun** (Aug. 26, 1844), though its ultimate source (Aberdeen or Simpson) is unclear. Pelly discouraged Simpson from lobbying Pakenham directly on treaty terms for the Company's southern-Oregon property, insisting such matters should go through Pelly to Aberdeen properly.
- **HMS Modeste (July 1844):** an 18-gun sloop under **Commander Thomas Baillie** visited the Columbia for three weeks — a symbolic show of British concern that reassured Company officers, though Baillie's crew showed more interest in leisure than in "the great national interests" (Douglas's wry complaint).
- **Chapter closes** on the eve of the 1844 US presidential election, with **James K. Polk**'s nomination on a platform of Oregon "re-occupation" and Texas "re-annexation" — setting up the decisive final crisis of Chapter 12.

**Key Figures:**

- **Lewis Linn** — US Senator; renewed Oregon agitation, 1839–40.
- **Lord Palmerston / Lord Aberdeen** — successive foreign secretaries with sharply different Oregon priorities.
- **James Douglas** — originator of the "possessory rights" concept; sharpest-eyed Company observer of the crisis.
- **John McLoughlin** — extended controversial credit to the 1843 migration.
- **Dr. Elijah White** — self-appointed US "Sub-Indian Agent," 1842.
- **Richard M. Johnson** — chaired the 1843 Cincinnati Convention.
- **John Tyler** — escalated US claims in his December 1843 message.
- **Richard Pakenham** — British minister in Washington; Simpson's diplomatic contact.
- **Daniel Webster** — moderate US Secretary of State whose private views anticipated the eventual settlement.
- **Adam Thom** — Red River-based Company pamphleteer/critic of Simpson's indiscretion.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **"Oregon fever"** — the surge of American popular/political enthusiasm for annexing Oregon.
- **Possessory rights** — the principle (originated by Douglas, 1843) that the Company's physical property/improvements should be protected regardless of the final boundary.
- **Provisional government (Champoeg, 1843)** — the American settlers' self-organized Oregon government.
- **Fort Victoria** — new HBC depot on Vancouver Island (1843).

**Important Dates:**

- **1839–40** — Linn's resolutions; Pelly's appeal to Palmerston.
- **February 1841** — First Willamette government-organizing meeting.
- **August 1841** — Whig government falls; Aberdeen replaces Palmerston.
- **1842** — Dr. Elijah White's migration; Simpson's Juan de Fuca boundary prediction (March 1).
- **1843** — Fort Victoria built; Cincinnati Convention (July); Great Migration (~500-1,000 settlers); Champoeg provisional government (May 2/July 5); Douglas's "possessory rights" letter (Oct. 23).
- **December 5, 1843** — Tyler's expansionist message to Congress.
- **March 1844** — Aberdeen privately accepts the 49th parallel (with all of Vancouver Island).
- **July 15, 1844** — HMS Modeste arrives.
- **1844** — Polk nominated on an Oregon/Texas expansionist platform.

**Significance:** Shows the crisis reaching the point of no return — American demographic and political momentum overwhelms the Company's commercial dominance, while British leadership (Aberdeen) treats Oregon as expendable compared to more important disputes. Douglas's "possessory rights" idea, introduced here almost as an afterthought, becomes the central mechanism (Ch. 13) by which the Company salvages something from the coming diplomatic defeat.

## Chapter 12: American Victory, 1844–1846

_(pp. 233–254)_

**Overview:** Traces the final, decisive phase of the Oregon dispute — from Aberdeen's private acceptance of the 49th parallel through the actual 1846 treaty — showing how the settlement was driven almost entirely by broader considerations of Anglo-American peace (and fear of simultaneous war with France) rather than by the Company's interests, even as the Company (via Simpson and Pelly) successfully lobbied for the treaty's crucial **possessory rights** protections.

**Key Points:**

- **Why Britain supported the Company despite disliking it:** the Peel cabinet backed HBC rights not from approval of monopoly but because abandoning a British enterprise to American pressure would mean sacrificing British "honour and prestige" — the Company's material interests became inseparable from the government's need not to appear intimidated by Polk's "bellicosity."
- **The French naval threat:** a crucial, often-overlooked factor — **Peel** and the **Duke of Wellington** feared war with France more than with the US, since new French steam warships had rendered Britain's older sailing navy vulnerable (illustrated by **Sir Charles Napier**'s alarmed 1844 letter to Ellice about France's naval superiority at a Portsmouth display). A two-front war (US + France) was a nightmare scenario shaping British caution.
- **Domestic political cover:** Peel's move toward **repealing the Corn Laws** created a tacit understanding with the Whigs that gave the government more room to make an Oregon settlement less favorable to Britain than earlier proposed, without being savaged by opposition attacks over "craven submission."
- **Calhoun's delaying strategy:** as Secretary of State, **John C. Calhoun** skillfully strung out negotiations with Pakenham without ever committing beyond hints (floating, at different times, either free navigation of the Columbia or all of Vancouver Island, never both) — correctly calculating that delay favored growing American strength, though Polk's more impatient expansionism cut against this patience.
- **The Williamson incident (Feb. 1845):** American settler **Henry Williamson** provocatively staked a land claim near Fort Vancouver itself; McLoughlin had his markers removed, Williamson returned with a surveyor, and though the provisional government disapproved his actions, the episode symbolized the more aggressively anti-British character of the 1844–45 migrants compared to earlier settlers (Company officer **Francis Ermatinger**'s complaint about "as lawless fellows as ever can curse a countre" is quoted).
- **Simpson's 1845 London memorandum (March 29, 1845)** — quoted at length — is the direct blueprint for the treaty's eventual protective provisions: proposed the Lewis (Snake) River as an ideal boundary, or failing that the 49th parallel with Vancouver Island and Juan de Fuca navigation secured to Britain, **and explicit treaty protection for the Company's and PSAC's existing possessions** (Cowlitz, Multnomah Island, Willamette water privileges, Columbia/Umpqua posts, etc.). Galbraith notes Douglas had proposed the same core principle back in 1843 (Ch. 11), but Simpson's 1845 memo is what reached Peel and Aberdeen directly.
- **War preparations (spring 1845):** with Polk's belligerent inaugural address and **James Buchanan** as Secretary of State, Peel and Aberdeen genuinely feared war. Simpson proposed a defense plan (regular troops + half-breed riflemen at Red River; naval force and seizure of **Cape Disappointment** at the Columbia's mouth; a 2,000-man half-breed/Indian force under regular army officers) and was authorized **£1,000** and two engineer officers — **Lieutenants Henry J. Warre and Mervin Vavasour** — to travel west disguised as sportsmen/hunters, gathering military intelligence and (per **Father de Smet**'s shrewd observation) preparing to seize Cape Disappointment for Britain in case of war.
- **Simpson's dual agenda:** used his role escorting Warre and Vavasour to simultaneously push for British military posts at Red River and Fort William — partly genuine defense planning, partly using the war scare to bolster Company control over an increasingly restive Red River population (tying back to Ch. 4's free-trade crisis).
- **The Cape Disappointment fiasco:** by the time **Peter Skene Ogden** (assigned to guide Warre/Vavasour) reached the cape (Feb. 1846), American squatters (**Wheeler and McDaniell**) already held valid claims there under provisional-government law; after confused, indecisive correspondence between Ogden, Warre, and Vavasour about authorization, Ogden simply **purchased the claims for $1,000** (plus $200 survey fees), registering the land in his own name — a telling anticlimax to the war-planning mission. Ogden's private assessment of Warre and Vavasour was scathing (quoted: Vavasour a "disagreeable Puppy," prone to "Brandy and Opium").
- **Naval visits as political theater:** **HMS Modeste** (Commander Thomas Baillie, July 1844) and **HMS America** (Captain **John Gordon**, Aberdeen's own brother, Aug. 1845) visited the coast — Gordon in particular showed contempt for Oregon's value (per Douglas's report, he "does not think the country worth five straws" and favored simply arbitrating away disputed claims — Douglas dryly called this "a very wise plan truly... to lose everything we had"). The popular later legend that Britain lost Oregon because Gordon was disappointed by poor salmon fishing is dismissed by Galbraith as baseless.
- **Aberdeen's fixed position:** from spring 1844 onward, privately committed to the **49th parallel with all of Vancouver Island** — reaffirmed almost verbatim in an **October 17, 1845 letter to Peel**, which also (inaccurately, per Galbraith) cited the Company's own move to Fort Victoria as evidence this outcome matched HBC preferences (in fact Fort Vancouver remained HQ for three more years).
- **Signs the settlement was approaching:** Ellice's private correspondence (Jan.–Feb. 1846) predicted a 49th-parallel deal; a **London Times editorial (Jan. 3, 1846)**, likely a government-planted trial balloon, proposed essentially the Gallatin 1826 terms (49th parallel, Vancouver Island, Columbia navigation) as an honorable settlement.
- **The navigation-rights concession was symbolic, not substantive:** Simpson himself argued the Columbia's free navigation would mean little once the boundary was fixed at 49°, since the river wasn't actually used for transport across the mountains (light express traffic only; goods went via the Peace River route instead) — the concession mattered only for "punctilio"/national pride (per American diplomat **Edward Everett**'s own assessment), not real British interest.
- **Political urgency for a quick deal:** **Robert Dale Owen** (March 23, 1846) and Tory insider **Richard Croker** (May 13, 1846) both warned Aberdeen that the window for a Senate-ratifiable 49th-parallel deal was closing fast as American opinion hardened — Croker's letter vividly lists the simultaneous crises Britain might face (American war, French war, Irish rebellion, industrial distress, punitive income tax) if delay continued.
- **May 18, 1846:** Aberdeen authorized Pakenham to present the treaty draft substantially as it was finally signed — Galbraith stresses the **Company had no role** in the core decision for the 49th-parallel/Vancouver Island line, nor in the Article II navigation clause (which simply reused Gallatin's 1826-27 offer).
- **The Company's real contribution — possessory rights:** Pelly's persistent 1846 lobbying (documented in his June 3, 1846 letter to Simpson, quoted at length) secured the treaty's **Articles III and IV**: Article III guaranteed the "possessory rights of the Hudson's Bay Company, and of all British subjects" already occupying land south of 49°; Article IV confirmed PSAC's lands/farms north of the Columbia, with the US government retaining an option to purchase them at fair valuation if deemed of "public and political importance." Pelly had specifically suggested a valuation formula (20 years' purchase on average 1844-46 net profits).
- **Galbraith's sharp critique of the treaty's drafting:** phrases like "possessory rights," "lawfully acquired," and "belonging to" were **ripe for hostile interpretation** by American settlers and officials in a frontier context where such legal language had no prior local meaning — "invitations... to harass the Company," setting up the prolonged disputes covered in Chapter 13.
- **The treaty as sacrifice, not compromise:** despite the Company's still-solid Oregon profitability at the time of signing (Fort Vancouver's 1845–46 net gain: £21,263, seven-year average over £30,000; other Oregon posts: £10,620 net in 1845-46, seven-year average £10,929) and undisturbed operations north of the Columbia (only 15 American families had settled there, mostly near Fort Nisqually, living amicably with Company officers), the treaty sacrificed HBC's Oregon position to the larger goal of Anglo-American peace. American minister **Louis McLane** in London reported (Dec. 1, 1845) that a 49th-parallel deal would be broadly popular in Britain, "complained of principally by the Hudson's Bay Company and those in its interest" — i.e., British public opinion, unlike in Canning's day, simply didn't care.
- **Aftermath:** the Peel government fell shortly after the treaty (over Corn Law repeal), returning **Palmerston** to the Foreign Office — his cynical marginal note on Pakenham's report of American satisfaction ("It would have been strange if the Americans had not been pleased with an arrangement which gives them everything which they ever really wanted") captures Galbraith's own verdict: **not a compromise but a British surrender.** Peter Skene Ogden's bitter closing remark ("put not your trust in Prime Ministers... thank God Peel is out of power") gives the chapter's final, personal note of Company disillusionment.

**Key Figures:**

- **Sir Robert Peel / Lord Aberdeen** — British PM and foreign secretary who accepted the 49th parallel.
- **James K. Polk / James Buchanan / John C. Calhoun** — successive drivers of the American negotiating position.
- **Sir George Simpson** — drafted the key 1845 possessory-rights memorandum; organized wartime defense planning.
- **Henry J. Warre / Mervin Vavasour** — British officers sent west under cover of a sporting trip.
- **Peter Skene Ogden** — guided Warre/Vavasour; purchased Cape Disappointment claims; final embittered commentator.
- **John Henry Pelly** — secured Articles III and IV (possessory rights) through direct lobbying of Aberdeen.
- **Captain John Gordon (HMS America)** — Aberdeen's brother; showed open indifference to Oregon's value.
- **Richard Pakenham** — British minister in Washington; his negotiating missteps (rejecting Buchanan's July 1845 offer) alarmed Aberdeen.
- **Lord Palmerston** — returned to the Foreign Office after the treaty, delivering the sardonic final verdict on it.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Possessory rights (Article III)** — guarantee of the Company's existing property south of 49°.
- **Article IV** — PSAC property protections north of the Columbia, with a US purchase option.
- **Cape Disappointment** — strategic point at the Columbia's mouth, contested/purchased by Ogden.
- **"Punctilio"** — Everett's term for the symbolic-only value of the Columbia navigation concession.

**Important Dates:**

- **February 1845** — Williamson incident.
- **March 29, 1845** — Simpson's memorandum to Aberdeen.
- **April 2, 1845** — Simpson meets Peel and Aberdeen.
- **June–August 1845** — Warre/Vavasour expedition; arrival at Fort Vancouver (Aug. 25).
- **July 12, 1845** — Buchanan's 49th-parallel proposal (rejected by Pakenham).
- **October 17, 1845** — Aberdeen's letter to Peel fixing his negotiating position.
- **December 1845** — Polk's annual message; Simpson predicts "inevitable" war.
- **February 1846** — Ogden purchases Cape Disappointment claims; US rejects arbitration.
- **April 26, 1846** — US Congress passes notice-of-termination resolution.
- **May 18, 1846** — Aberdeen authorizes the final treaty draft.
- **June 15, 1846** — Oregon Treaty signed (implied; the actual signing date, not explicitly given in this chapter's text but standard).
- **Mid-1846** — Peel government falls over Corn Law repeal; Palmerston returns to Foreign Office.

**Significance:** Completes the diplomatic story begun in Chapters 9-11: the boundary was ultimately set by considerations entirely external to the Company (Anglo-American and Anglo-French relations, domestic British politics), but the Company's persistent lobbying secured the "possessory rights" clauses that would dominate Anglo-American relations in Oregon for the next two decades — the subject of Chapter 13.

## Chapter 13: The Possessory Rights Question, 1846–1869

_(pp. 251–282)_

**Overview:** The long, sordid aftermath of the Oregon Treaty's vaguely worded protective clauses — a 25-year saga of squatter encroachment, corrupt Washington lobbyists, congressional indifference, and diplomatic neglect that wasn't finally resolved until an international arbitration commission in 1869.

**Key Points:**

- **Initial calm (1846–1850):** relations were cordial at first — **Lieutenant Neil M. Howison** (US Navy) reported the Company's trade was moderate and beneficial to settlers; **James Douglas and Charles Forrest** even served as judges in the provisional government. But **Peter Skene Ogden** presciently sensed underlying suspicion even before the treaty's ratification.
- **Land claims trouble begins:** Under the **Oregon Land Law of 1845** (one-square-mile claim limit), McLoughlin and Douglas registered Company land around Fort Vancouver in the names of **nine individual employees** (with private quitclaims back to the Company) — a legal fiction that fooled no one. American squatters began encroaching even before ratification; by 1847 Tolmie's warnings to squatters at Nisqually (e.g., to "one Smith") were simply ignored.
- **The treaty's fatal ambiguity:** Article II's Columbia navigation right for "the Hudson's Bay Company, and all British subjects trading with the same" never specified whether it was permanent or tied to the Company's **1838 license (expiring 1859)** — Aberdeen and American minister McLane later gave contradictory accounts of what had been agreed, and the issue was never resolved before signing. Article III's "possessory rights... lawfully acquired" was equally undefined. Douglas and Ogden accurately predicted (quoted at length) that Article II's navigation "concession" was actually a "deceitful clause" that subjected the Company to ordinary US revenue and tariff laws, providing no real trading advantage.
- **Early attempts to sell out (1846–48):** With the Company's position expected only to deteriorate, Simpson and Pelly were willing to sell all rights/property of both the HBC and PSAC for **£100,000** (£70,000 + £30,000) as early as August 1846. An internal valuation (May 1847, by Ogden/Douglas/Work) put just the buildings/improvements at **£87,117**.
- **Forced retrieval of quitclaims:** Simpson ordered Douglas/Ogden to reclaim the individually-registered land titles before any sale — done reluctantly in 1849–50; some officers (Ogden, Forbes Barclay) had to fight for reimbursement of their own out-of-pocket expenses in the original scheme.
- **The George N. Sanders affair (1847–49):** a colorful, ultimately damaging episode. **Sanders**, a well-connected but unscrupulous Democratic operator, first proposed a scheme where investors (headed by **Captain A. C. Harris**) would buy the rights cheap and resell to the government at profit, funded by an HBC advance — Simpson rejected this as all-benefit-to-Sanders. Sanders then became the Company's paid agent (April 1848 contract: Company to receive **$410,000** in US bonds, Sanders to keep everything above that plus 2.5% commission) and lined up support from Buchanan and several senators (Calhoun, Cass, Crittenden, Hannegan, Houston, Breese), plus paid legal opinions from **Daniel Webster** ($5,000) and **Richard S. Coxe** favoring expansive Company rights. Despite the political apparatus, a Senate motion (Aug. 11, 1848) died after a request to independently assess property values, and the effort collapsed at adjournment. Buchanan told Simpson/Berens/Finlayson (Aug. 15, 1848) he'd support $1,000,000 — but only via a formal US-UK treaty (to formally end navigation rights), which was the same obstacle that had blocked earlier efforts.
- **Palmerston's obstruction:** back at the Foreign Office, Palmerston refused (Sept. 1848) to let the Company unilaterally cede Columbia navigation rights, since other British subjects (those "trading with" the Company) were also stakeholders — even though the Company itself considered the right practically worthless (Work and Douglas had already begun using the **Fraser River route** instead, immediately after the treaty). Galbraith's judgment: the Company suffered from _both_ extremes — Aberdeen's excessive eagerness to concede substance in 1846, and Palmerston's excessive refusal to concede a now-empty symbol thereafter.
- **Senate rejects purchase (Feb. 1849)** — only 10 senators in favor, 40 opposed/abstaining.
- **Second Sanders attempt, under Secretary of State John M. Clayton (1849–50):** Clayton floated $700,000 and seemed to make progress (agreeing to bypass the need for a formal UK treaty) but simply let the matter lapse before resigning. Sanders' methods (his exposed lobbying/bribery-adjacent tactics) later badly compromised the Company's credibility in future negotiations. Pelly, uneasy about the ethics involved, let the Sanders contract expire and (while he remained governor) employed no further agents; his successor **Andrew Colvile** was less scrupulous.
- **The Cayuse War (1847–48) and local goodwill:** the Whitman Massacre and resulting war tested but did not break Ogden and Douglas's standing with settlers — Ogden helped secure the release of massacre survivors; Douglas refused (citing lack of authority) to lend Company funds to equip the punitive expedition under the blustering **Colonel Cornelius Gilliam**, risking bitterness, but Douglas and Ogden's personal popularity (illustrated by their generous treatment of provisional-government marshal **Joseph L. Meek**) helped avert violence against Fort Vancouver.
- **Fort Nisqually as the weak point:** lacking a Douglas/Ogden-caliber officer, **William Fraser Tolmie** faced growing squatter pressure from 1847 onward (10 American claimants by 1850) with no real recourse beyond written protest — intended mainly to build a paper record for eventual compensation claims.
- **California Gold Rush (1848–49) — mixed blessing:** stripped Oregon of male population and diverted migration south, temporarily easing land pressure; the Company's Fort Vancouver goods sold at inflated prices (lumber jumped from $16 to $65/thousand feet) even as its own employees deserted for the goldfields, forcing reliance on Indian labor.
- **Territorial governor Joseph Lane** (from March 1849) was consistently friendly to the Company (as congressman and senator afterward), but real policy was set by less sympathetic officials.
- **1850–1869: escalating federal/local harassment:**
    - **Customs abuses:** collector **John Adair** (Astoria, 1849) wrongly taxed goods bound for Company use north of 49° — corrected in 1850 by Treasury Secretary **William M. Meredith**.
    - **Samuel R. Thurston**, Oregon's first congressional delegate — described by historian Hubert Howe Bancroft as a "scurrilous and unprincipled demagogue" — drove a May 1850 Treasury rule requiring all Victoria-Nisqually shipping to detour through Astoria for customs.
    - **Simpson P. Moses**, customs collector at Olympia — extracted information/favor-seeking from Tolmie under false pretenses, then seized the Company's steamer **Beaver** and brigantine **Mary Dare** (Nov. 1851) on flimsy technical pretexts (sugar package weight; "in ballast" entry; landing passengers without prior customs clearance). Treasury Secretary **Thomas Corwin** eventually ordered restitution and the Company got $1,000 compensation for the _Beaver_ seizure.
    - **Oregon Donation Act (1850):** granted 320–640 acres to settlers, triggering a fresh squatter rush; a probate judge began selling "vacant" land within the Fort Vancouver claim itself before Army protection (the site was designated a **military reservation**, protected by the friendly **Colonel W. W. Loring**) stopped it. Nisqually/Cowlitz had no such shield — trespassers grew from 28 (1851) to 50 (1853); an armed confrontation (documented in officer **Edward Huggins**'s account, May 1852) forced a Company survey party to abandon its work at gunpoint.
    - **Robert J. Walker negotiation (1852):** former Treasury Secretary, engaged on Simpson's recommendation; **Daniel Webster** (now Secretary of State) agreed to $1,000,000 but again insisted on a formal US-UK treaty relinquishing navigation rights — the same sticking point. The weak, minority **Derby government** in Britain (1852) could not risk appearing to concede anything; Foreign Secretary **Lord Malmesbury** argued (reasonably) that since the Company's withdrawal would end the navigation right anyway, no formal cession clause was even needed — but Webster died (Oct. 24, 1852) before any resolution, and Walker exited $21,500 richer for nothing achieved.
    - **L. C. Levin / George M. Dallas negotiation (1853):** Simpson, undeterred, tried yet another lobbyist team — raised the asking price to $1,500,000 (a deliberate negotiating inflation to make $1,000,000 look like a "compromise"). Simpson learned from Secretary of State **William Marcy** (Oct. 1853) that Sanders' earlier bribery-adjacent activities were on file at the State Department — a damaging revelation Simpson tried to disown.
    - **Marcy's 1855 hardball offer:** after over a year's delay, Marcy told Simpson (Jan. 15, 1855) the US would pay at most **$300,000** total, dismissing the Webster/Coxe legal opinions as tainted by Sanders' lobbying and arguing rights would lapse anyway at the 1859 license expiration. Simpson, defeated, recommended the board accept if Congress approved — but Senate purchase motions failed in **March 1855** and again in **December 1855** (after President **Franklin Pierce** twice recommended purchase).
- **Post-1855 collapse:** squatters now encroached even on tilled/enclosed land the Company had previously held unchallenged; the **Yakima/Puget Sound Indian wars (1855–56)** fed American suspicion that Company men were arming hostile Indians; **Fort Walla Walla was abandoned (Oct. 16, 1855)** — officers **James Sinclair** and **Dugald Mactavish** submitted an inflated compensation claim ($67,525 claimed vs. ~$8,000 actual loss, by Mactavish's own admission) as a deliberate padding strategy given the expectation of government reduction.
- **Discriminatory taxation:** local assessors valued Company property (for tax purposes) far above what the same officials publicly claimed it was worth for purchase purposes — e.g., Nisqually valued at $150,000 by Governor **Isaac Stevens** but "measurably worthless" per the territorial legislature (yet taxed $6,725.62 in 1855); Fort Vancouver's assessed value nearly doubled from $112,000 (1854) to $197,000 (1855). The Company refused to pay Nisqually/Cowlitz taxes after 1858 as discriminatory.
- **Federal land office manipulation:** initial (1851) instructions to survey only township lines around Company claims (avoiding disputes) were reversed just **10 days after** the Senate's 1855 purchase rejection, ordering surveys extended right up to the Company's actual, never-formally-defined 1846 settlement boundaries — guaranteeing further conflict.
- **1856–57 diplomatic effort:** with the Crimean War over, HBC leadership (**John Shepherd, Henry Hulse Berens, Edward Ellice Jr.**) got sympathetic hearing from Foreign Secretary **Lord Clarendon**, but the US legation was crippled when Minister **John F. Crampton** was expelled (May 1856) for illegal Crimean War recruiting, leaving only a low-ranking chargé (**J. Savile Lumley**) unable to press the issue effectively.
- **Lord Napier (1857 onward)** — the Company's most effective post-treaty diplomat, though personally disinterested; found President Buchanan's Secretary of State **Lewis Cass** offering only $300,000 against the Company's $1,000,000 ask (later negotiated down informally toward $650,000) — but the deal collapsed in July 1858 when **Sanders himself showed Napier a copy of his old 1848 contract**, revealing the Company had once been willing to accept just **$410,000** — undercutting Napier's negotiating credibility and souring even Ellice on the Company's consistency.
- **Military escalation (1858–60):** friction with the Army began over trivial matters (Captain **Rufus Ingalls**' wharf construction dispute with **Dugald Mactavish**) but culminated when, after the Company's British Columbia trading license expired in 1859, **General W. S. Harney** (already notorious for the San Juan Island seizure) ordered the Company (March 1860) to vacate Fort Vancouver, claiming possessory rights had lapsed. Minister **Lord Lyons** protested vigorously to Cass, prompting formal US disavowal of Harney's authority — but leaked correspondence (a letter from Secretary of War **John B. Floyd** endorsing Harney's action) and a December 1860 Interior Department report asserting the same position suggested the repudiation was really just a retreat under diplomatic pressure, not principled disagreement. The Company's **Alexander Grant Dallas** preemptively ordered withdrawal from Fort Vancouver anyway, partly because trade there was no longer profitable (Outfit 1858: net gain of only £29 in American territory overall, saved only by a £2,085 Fort Vancouver retail-shop profit; even Fort Colvile, long the most reliable fur post, netted only £107 and was by 1859 also being invaded by squatters).
- **Final resolution — the 1863 arbitration convention:** with the Lincoln administration preoccupied by the Civil War and worried about Confederate-cruiser tensions with Britain, Secretary of State **William H. Seward** (long privately sympathetic) agreed with Minister Lyons to resolve the dispute via a **joint commission** rather than a Senate-vulnerable treaty — signed **July 1, 1863**. Commissioners: **Charles D. Day** (Montreal, for Britain/HBC) and **Caleb Cushing** (for the US); umpire **Benjamin R. Curtis** (former US Supreme Court Justice, famed for his Dred Scott dissent), agreed upon after Canadian input via **John Rose**.
- **Cushing's decisive argument:** since the Company had just agreed (1869) to sell its _entire_ Rupert's Land charter rights to Canada for only **£300,000 (~$1.5 million)**, its Oregon possessions south of 49° — a tiny fraction of that territory — logically couldn't be worth much either; British commissioner Day conceded this would reduce the likely award.
- **Final award (September 1869):** **$450,000** to the HBC and **$200,000** to the PSAC ($650,000 total) — after nearly two years of hearings and fourteen volumes of testimony.
- **Even payment was delayed further:** Congress attached a rider (approved Feb. 1871) requiring settlement of the companies' disputed Oregon/Washington tax bills (~$61,000+ with penalties) before payment — the State Department ultimately paid the full $650,000 in **September 1871** without deduction, finally closing a 25-year saga.
- **Galbraith's summary judgment:** the possessory rights fiasco resulted from Aberdeen's imprecise treaty language (driven by his rush for peace), decades of British diplomatic passivity (with the notable exception of the 1857-63 period), and the corrosive effect of the Company's own resort to corrupt Washington lobbyists (Sanders especially), which undermined its credibility even when its underlying case was reasonable. The dispute was, in his words, "the price that had to be paid for Aberdeen's 'peace with honor.'"

**Key Figures:**

- **James Douglas / Peter Skene Ogden** — Company officers who preserved goodwill with Oregon settlers through the Cayuse War era.
- **William Fraser Tolmie** — beleaguered officer at Fort Nisqually.
- **George N. Sanders** — corrupt Washington lobbyist whose dealings damaged the Company's later credibility.
- **Daniel Webster** — Secretary of State (1850-52) most sympathetic to a fair settlement; died before concluding one.
- **William Marcy** — Secretary of State who drove the price down to $300,000 (1855).
- **Lord Napier** — Britain's most effective post-treaty envoy (from 1857).
- **General W. S. Harney** — ordered the 1860 Fort Vancouver eviction.
- **William H. Seward / Lord Lyons** — negotiated the 1863 arbitration convention.
- **Caleb Cushing / Charles D. Day / Benjamin R. Curtis** — arbitration commissioners/umpire (1863-69).

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Possessory rights** — Article III's guarantee of the Company's existing property.
- **Oregon Donation Act (1850)** — triggered the major squatter land rush.
- **1863 Arbitration Convention** — the mechanism that finally resolved the dispute.
- **Fort Shepherd** — replacement post built across the border for Fort Colvile.

**Important Dates:**

- **1845** — Oregon Land Law; quitclaim scheme at Fort Vancouver.
- **1846–48** — Early sale attempts (£100,000 asking price); Sanders' first contract.
- **February 1849** — Senate rejects purchase (10-40).
- **1850** — Oregon Donation Act; Adair/customs disputes.
- **November 1851** — Beaver/Mary Dare seizures.
- **1852** — Walker negotiation; Webster's death (Oct. 24).
- **1853** — Levin/Dallas negotiation; Sanders' bribery revealed to Marcy.
- **January 15, 1855** — Marcy's $300,000 ultimatum.
- **October 16, 1855** — Fort Walla Walla abandoned.
- **1857–58** — Napier's negotiations; Sanders exposes his old contract to Napier (July 1858).
- **1859** — HBC's British Columbia trading license expires.
- **March 1860** — Harney orders Fort Vancouver evacuation.
- **July 1, 1863** — Seward-Lyons arbitration convention signed.
- **May 1865** — Commission organizes.
- **September 1869** — Final award ($650,000 total).
- **February–September 1871** — Payment finalized.

**Significance:** Demonstrates the long, costly consequences of Aberdeen's imprecise 1846 treaty language and Britain's subsequent diplomatic inconsistency — alternating neglect and obstruction — while showing how the Company's own resort to corrupt lobbying (the Sanders affair) compounded its difficulties. The eventual 1869 arbitration, resolved only because of Civil War-era Anglo-American caution, closes the Oregon chapter of the book just as the main narrative turns to the Company's parallel unraveling at Red River and its final sale (Part IV).

## Chapter 14: Company Control of Vancouver Island, 1847–1865

_(pp. 283–309)_

**Overview:** A parallel case study to Chapter 10's Puget's Sound Agricultural Company — the Company's 1849–1858 tenure as proprietary colonizer of Vancouver Island, granted by the Crown to preempt American expansion, again demonstrates the structural incompatibility between a fur-trading monopoly and genuine settlement promotion — ending only once the **Fraser River gold rush** made the island self-sustaining and the Crown reclaimed direct control.

**Key Points:**

- **Post-treaty anxiety:** after 1846, London feared American settlers (credited with winning the Oregon dispute) might push further north; **Vancouver Island**, seat of Fort Victoria and the coastal trade, seemed especially vulnerable.
- **Earl Grey** (colonial secretary in the new Whig government) wanted to strengthen British settlement there but — bound by the era's insistence that colonies not burden the Imperial exchequer — needed a private agency to do it at no public cost; he saw the HBC's experience and capital as the obvious (if, per Galbraith, questionable) solution.
- **Galbraith explicitly challenges the "fur-trade conspiracy" thesis** (the standard view that Grey was either a dupe or an accomplice of a cunning monopolist protecting a fur preserve): Vancouver Island had little fur value; the real motive was **frontier-policy logic** — control of the island protected New Caledonia's trade from becoming a base for rival incursions, exactly as with earlier "frontier" defenses. Crucially, **Simpson himself initially opposed** the scheme (writing as late as Oct. 1848 that colonization would fail and only bring the Company odium and expense), as did secretary **Archibald Barclay** and — most persistently — **Edward "Bear" Ellice**, who lobbied Grey directly against it and remained critical for years (a striking 1859 letter is quoted calling the original decision "a most rash undertaking"). This internal disagreement undercuts any simple conspiracy narrative.
- **The decision was really Pelly's alone**, driven by fear that if the Company didn't take the grant, a _rival_ colonizing company might — and such rivals, occupying Vancouver Island or the mainland, could become a launchpad for competitive incursions into Rupert's Land. Pelly initially over-reached, proposing (March 1847) that the Company receive **all British territory north and west of Rupert's Land** — flatly rejected by undersecretary **James Stephen**.
- **The Fitzgerald threat:** **James Edward Fitzgerald**, a well-connected but poorly-capitalized enthusiast (introduced to the Colonial Office via British Museum librarian **Anthony Panizzi**), proposed a Wakefield-style "systematic colonization" joint-stock company (June 1847) — energetic but never able to demonstrate real financial backing. Galbraith stresses the key point: **capital, not conspiracy, decided the grant** — the HBC had it, Fitzgerald's group didn't, and no one else with capital competed for the grant.
- **Parliamentary opposition:** **William E. Gladstone** (a lifelong, almost reflexively hostile critic of the Company as an "anachronism") led attacks on the Vancouver Island grant using Fitzgerald's ammunition; **Lord Monteagle** called it "the most lavish, the most inconsiderate... [grant] ever made by any Colonial Minister"; a Commons censure motion was narrowly defeated (76–58, Aug. 18, 1848).
- **Terms of the Royal Grant (issued January 13, 1849):** the Company became "true and absolute lords and proprietors" of Vancouver Island in free and common socage, for a nominal annual rent of **7 shillings**, but with concessions forced by the parliamentary attacks — land must be sold on "reasonable terms" to all comers, revenue (minus a 10% Company profit margin) applied to colonization, and the grant **revocable within 5 years** if colonization efforts proved inadequate.
- **The land-sale terms effectively guaranteed failure:** £1/acre (a price suggested by the Colonial Office itself, not invented by the Company), 20-acre minimum, and — most restrictive — a requirement to bring 5 laborers per 100 acres purchased. Galbraith notes Fitzgerald's own rejected scheme had comparably restrictive labor requirements, suggesting **both Grey and Pelly may have shared the same naive belief in "systematic colonization,"** not that the Company deliberately sabotaged it.
- **Internal HBC skeptics who _wanted_ colonization to fail:** **Lord Selkirk (the younger)** and **Andrew Colvile** explicitly favored taking the grant precisely to _block_ competitors and slow settlement — Selkirk's May 1848 letter to Pelly (fearing outsiders would "play the devil") and Colvile's later dominance (as effective policy-maker from Pelly's 1850 illness until his own 1856 death) as a genuine restrictionist are documented in detail. Colvile explicitly told Simpson the £1/acre price ensured "you need not be afraid of too many settlers."
- **Governance structure:** Crown-appointed governor + 7-member council; elected assembly for 20-acre-plus landowners. First governor **Richard Blanshard** (1850–51) — an independent lawyer chosen partly to counter charges of Company control — found himself powerless (nearly the entire population were Company employees loyal to Company officers, not him), became convinced the Company was hoarding the best land, contracted the ague, and resigned in frustration. Succeeded (on Pelly's recommendation) by Chief Factor **James Douglas**, who thereafter held the awkward dual role of Crown governor and senior Company officer.
- **Terrible timing:** the Company assumed colonization duties (1849) just as the **California Gold Rush** was stripping Oregon and the whole Pacific coast of available labor — Nanaimo coal miners deserted en masse for California in 1850, since Company wages (£50-60/year with subsistence) couldn't compete with California/Oregon wages ($3-16/day).
- **Feeble advertising effort:** newspaper ads in London and Scottish papers (via agent **John Harthill and Sons**) mostly attracted job-seekers, not the independent farmer-emigrants intended — one correspondent astutely noted farmers wouldn't come unless passage was free, advice the Company ignored.
- **Company self-dealing on land:** Douglas (per Simpson's instruction) marked out ~20 square miles of the best land around Fort Victoria as a possessory-rights claim (ultimately registered as 3,084 acres) — criticized by Blanshard and by Royal Navy officers, notably **Rear Admiral Fairfax Moresby** (1851), who complained the Company charged its own men far less for flour than it charged naval vessels, and that its exclusive-trade privileges were fundamentally "incompatible with the free and liberal reception of an Emigrant community."
- **Simpson's restrictionist instincts revealed in private correspondence:** when Douglas (initially believing genuine colonization was wanted) proposed bringing 100 settlers/year, Simpson cautioned him toward a much slower pace, citing (speciously, per Galbraith) fears of crop failure and disorder in "villages or towns" — invoking the Red River precedent as a cautionary tale. Douglas later conceded he had "probably exceeded the mark."
- **Immigration data confirms the failure:** 1848–52 combined Company-sponsored immigration total: 271 males, 80 females, 84 children (i.e., overwhelmingly male laborers, not the "young married couples" the scheme envisioned) — only three independent purchasers of over 100 acres ever settled, and none prospered. By January 1855, only 11,455 acres had been sold total, of which HBC and PSAC together had bought **8,774 acres themselves** — i.e., most "sales" were the Company selling land to itself.
- **Advertising expenditure tells the real story:** £344 spent on newspaper ads (1849–52) collapsed to a mere £28 6d. (Oct. 1852–May 1853) — genuine promotional effort was minimal and short-lived.
- **Settler backlash:** a November 1850 petition to Blanshard from "the whole body of independent settlers" (really mostly Company-brought Muir family miners plus the Company's own chaplain, **Robert J. Staines**) condemned the Company as grasping and monopolistic. A more damaging 1854 petition — signed by actual Company officers **William F. Tolmie, Roderick Finlayson, and retired Chief Trader John Tod**, again drafted by Staines — reached Parliament via hostile MP **Charles Fitzwilliam** (who had personally clashed with Douglas during an 1853 visit) but made little impression amid Crimean War preoccupations; internally, however, it enraged Simpson, who contemplated punitive transfers for Tolmie and Finlayson (restrained by the calmer governor and committee).
- **Colonial Office disillusionment:** Grey grew skeptical by Dec. 1851 that the Company was seriously trying; his successors were openly hostile — the 1852-55 Aberdeen coalition government included **Gladstone** (Chancellor) and the **Duke of Newcastle** (Colonial Secretary), both longtime Company critics. Newcastle's 1853 inquiry about opening Indian trade to competition was met with the technically-true-but-misleading claim (Colvile) that the Company exercised no "exclusive" trading restriction — since it simply out-competed rivals economically instead, the same tactic used everywhere else in the book.
- **Formal notice of possible surrender (April 15, 1855):** new Colonial Secretary **Sir George Grey** (via undersecretary **Herman Merivale**) invited the Company to voluntarily surrender the grant before its trading license expired, to avoid an unpleasant formal investigation — also prompted by legal advice that the appointed council actually lacked legislative power without an elected assembly (leading to the **first elected assembly, convened August 12, 1856** — dominated by Company-friendly members, but symbolically marking the end of exclusive Company rule).
- **The 1857 Select Committee** (the same House of Commons inquiry central to Ch. 16) took mostly unfavorable testimony on Vancouver Island (notably from Blanshard and Fitzwilliam) and recommended ending Company tenure.
- **The Fraser River gold rush (1858) changed everything:** 30,000-40,000 miners rushed from the US following 1857 gold discoveries on the Thompson River (a Fraser tributary) — most landing at Victoria, transforming it overnight into a boomtown (land prices jumped from £1/acre to £100/acre; public land sales rose from 6,303 acres in 1857 to 30,948 in 1858).
- **Douglas's conflict of interest crisis:** initially tried to control the rush for Company benefit — required mining licenses (Dec. 1857), attempted an exclusive supply-shipping arrangement with the Pacific Mail Steamship Company, and issued a May 8, 1858 proclamation asserting Company trade monopoly with Indians throughout the territory. Colonial Secretary **Sir Edward Bulwer-Lytton** disallowed the proclamation and rejected the shipping deal, rebuking Douglas for exploiting his office for Company benefit — a rebuke reinforced privately by Company governor **Henry Hulse Berens**.
- **British Columbia created (Act of August 2, 1858):** established direct Crown rule over the mainland, automatically ending the Company's exclusive mainland trading rights. Douglas became governor of _both_ colonies on condition of **formally severing all Company ties** — which he did, subsequently maintaining genuine impartiality (to the Company's chagrin), even clashing repeatedly with his own son-in-law **Alexander Grant Dallas** (sent to head the western department) over land claims, steamer charges, and other issues.
- **Compensation settlement:** negotiations for surrendering Vancouver Island governance began Jan. 1858 (Colonial Secretary **Henry Labouchere**); the Company's claimed colonization expenses (£112,810.19) were negotiated down over four years to an agreed **£57,500 (1862)**, though the final surrender indenture wasn't completed until **April 3, 1867** (Company retained some farm acreage and the old Fort Victoria site).
- **Galbraith's closing assessment:** No realistic alternative existed to Company-led colonization before 1858 given British refusal to fund settlement directly and the total absence of any other capitalized colonizing group — but the Company's own commercial instincts (restricting land sales, minimal promotion, self-dealing on the best land) guaranteed the effort's failure independent of external circumstances. Quotes **Edward Ellice** (the government "used" the Company then discarded it once gold made the island profitable) and rising Liberal politician **Robert Lowe** (blaming the Colonial Office's own land-price policy for dooming settlement, not the Company). Galbraith's final verdict: the Company's Vancouver Island failure — like the Red River and Oregon settlement failures — was **"probably inherent in the make-up of a fur-trading monopoly,"** unable to reconcile itself with the free institutions any genuine British colony would demand.

**Key Figures:**

- **Earl Grey** — colonial secretary who initiated the grant to the Company.
- **John Henry Pelly** — pursued the grant, partly to preempt rival colonizers.
- **James Edward Fitzgerald** — rival colonization schemer, lacked capital.
- **William E. Gladstone** — persistent parliamentary critic of the Company.
- **Richard Blanshard** — first (independent) governor, resigned in frustration.
- **James Douglas** — second governor; later resigned Company ties to head both Vancouver Island and British Columbia.
- **Andrew Colvile / Lord Selkirk (younger)** — internal HBC figures who favored the grant specifically to restrict settlement.
- **Sir Edward Bulwer-Lytton** — colonial secretary who curbed Douglas's self-dealing during the gold rush.
- **Alexander Grant Dallas** — Douglas's son-in-law, western department head, frequent antagonist of Douglas post-1858.
- **Charles Fitzwilliam** — hostile MP who publicized settler petitions against the Company.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Royal Grant of 1849** — the Crown's proprietary grant of Vancouver Island to the HBC.
- **"Systematic colonization"** — the Wakefield-derived land-sale model (price + labor requirements) applied to the island.
- **Fraser River gold rush (1857–58)** — the event that ended the Company's governance.
- **British Columbia (est. Aug. 1858)** — new mainland Crown colony, ending HBC's exclusive trading rights there.

**Important Dates:**

- **1846–48** — Pelly's expanding proposals to Grey; Fitzgerald's rival scheme; parliamentary attacks.
- **January 13, 1849** — Royal Grant issued.
- **1850** — Blanshard arrives as governor; first miners desert for California.
- **November 1850** — First settler petition; Tory brings first agricultural settlers.
- **September 1851** — Blanshard resigns; Douglas becomes governor.
- **1852–55** — Slow colonization; Colvile's reports to the Colonial Office.
- **1854** — Second, more damaging settler petition reaches Parliament.
- **April 15, 1855** — Colonial Office invites voluntary surrender.
- **1857** — House of Commons Select Committee; Thompson River gold discovery.
- **1858** — Fraser River gold rush; British Columbia created (Aug. 2); Douglas severs Company ties.
- **1862** — Compensation agreed (£57,500).
- **April 3, 1867** — Final surrender indenture completed.

**Significance:** A structural parallel to the Puget's Sound Agricultural Company (Ch. 10) — reinforces the book's recurring argument that the Company, however sincere some of its leaders' intentions, could never successfully promote genuine free settlement, because its institutional character and commercial interests were fundamentally at odds with it. Sets up Part IV's story of the Company's declining fortunes and eventual sale, as Vancouver Island's loss previews the loss of Rupert's Land itself.

# PART IV — THE LAST YEARS OF THE MONOPOLY

## Chapter 15: The Growth of Unrest at Red River, 1846–1853

_(pp. 311–331)_

**Overview:** Opens Part IV by tracing how the Company tried — and ultimately failed — to use British troops to shore up its collapsing authority at Red River after the 1849 Sayer trial (Ch. 4), while simultaneously fighting off a wave of humanitarian and parliamentary attacks in Britain (the Isbister/Belcourt charges) that questioned the legality and morality of its monopoly altogether.

**Key Points:**

- Restates the core problem from Ch. 4: Red River's free trade "demoralized" the Company's price structure, but the Company lacked the coercive resources to stop it, and appealing to the British government for police support was politically awkward given widespread British hostility to chartered monopolies.
- **The military-aid gambit:** during the 1845-46 Oregon war scare, Simpson saw an opportunity — a British garrison sent to Red River ostensibly to guard against American attack could double as a means of "overawing" the settlement into obedience. Simpson pushed this idea through multiple channels: directly to Foreign Secretary **Aberdeen**; to Governor-General **Sir Charles Metcalfe** (who had his own grandiose, unrealistic ideas about massively reinforcing British North America); and via alarmist reports (e.g., an alleged August 1845 incident where US cavalry supposedly tried to entice Red River half-breeds to settle at Pembina under American protection).
- **Political obstacle course:** Colonial Secretary **Lord Stanley** initially agreed (Dec. 1845) to send 200-300 troops, contingent on Company support funding — but Stanley then resigned over Corn Law politics, replaced by the Company's least favorite possible appointee, **William E. Gladstone** — who nonetheless, to Pelly's relief, did not initially block the troop deployment (interviews Jan.-Feb. 1846).
- **Wellington's obstruction:** the Duke of Wellington, obsessed with home-island defense weakness, insisted troops go via Hudson Bay (not through Canada) and initially agreed to a one-year deployment — but then, after reading Warre and Vavasour's critical assessment of Fort Garry's defenses, tried to block the deployment entirely until proper fortifications were built. This produced a sharp exchange in which Gladstone (via his undersecretary) lectured Pelly on the crucial legal distinction between Crown colonies (which the government was obligated to defend) and a chartered proprietary company's territory (which it was not) — though the government did not ultimately reverse its decision.
- **Troops arrive (1846):** ~400 men of the **Sixth Royal Regiment of Foot** under **Lieutenant Colonel John F. Crofton** sailed via Hudson Bay in June 1846 — before news of the Oregon settlement reached London, meaning the original war-justification had evaporated by the time they arrived; the incoming Whig Colonial Secretary (Lord Grey) let them stay anyway, since they proved effective for maintaining domestic order (quoted: **Alexander Ross**'s enthusiastic 1847 description of newly "quiet & orderly" Red River, crediting "the presence of the red coats").
- **This calm was temporary** — the troops stayed only 2 years (not permanently), and while present, ironically, appear to have stimulated a temporary import-goods glut (from increased mercantile activity supplying the garrison) that later fed post-withdrawal illicit trading pressure.
- **The Belcourt/Isbister petition campaign (1846-50):** a memorial drafted by Catholic priest **Georges-Antoine Belcourt** (977 signatures, June 1846) leveled sweeping (and substantially exaggerated/false, per Galbraith) charges of Company-caused Indian misery, cannibalism, liquor debauchery, and near-£250,000/year "princely Revenue" extracted from Indian suffering.
- **Alexander K. Isbister** — a métis, Company-educated-and-employed-turned-critic, became the London-based spokesman for these charges after apparently souring on the Company around 1846 (possibly influenced by James Sinclair) despite earlier having sought Pelly's personal help securing an academic post. He drew on unreliable secondary sources — the Rev. **Herbert Beaver** (a disgruntled former Fort Vancouver chaplain who'd clashed violently with McLoughlin) and **Alexander Simpson** (a disaffected former chief trader) — to allege the Company traded mostly worthless/harmful goods (quoting Beaver: "over half may be classed as useless, one quarter as pernicious") and vastly increased liquor imports after its 1838 license renewal.
- **Colonial Office investigation:** Earl Grey asked Canadian Governor-General **Lord Elgin** to gather clergy testimony; Elgin's 1848 report (based heavily, it appears, on testimony from Col. Crofton — himself compromised by his cozy Company relationship and hope for Pelly's help advancing his career) sided firmly with the Company, arguing Indian conditions elsewhere were no better and warning that open trade would leave Indians "to the mercy of ... adventurers."
- **Simpson's countersurveillance:** intercepted/exposed Isbister's indiscreet boasting letters (to Alexander Rowand, a sympathetic-but-cautious half-breed physician who forwarded them to the Company) revealing his eagerness to "flay Sir George Simpson alive" — and discredited fellow critic **William Kennedy** (Isbister's uncle) by showing Kennedy had stayed in Company service long after the abuses he described, and had even asked Simpson for a lucrative Labrador trading license after resigning.
- **Legal challenge fails for lack of resources:** the 1849 House of Commons resolution requesting a legal opinion on the Company's charter rights was answered favorably to the Company (Jan. 1850, per the attorney and solicitor general) since it was based on an unrebutted, one-sided Company submission; when offered the chance to formally litigate, neither Isbister nor fellow critic John McLaughlin of Red River could afford to challenge "a great public corporation" in court.
- **Gladstone's August 1848 Commons speech** (during the Vancouver Island grant debate, Ch. 14) repeated Isbister/Wilkes-sourced charges about Company wage/price exploitation and a supposed liquor-import surge (3,800 to 9,000 gallons, 1837 to 1845) — Simpson's rebuttal (accurate per Galbraith's own analysis of Company invoices) showed the markup was 50%, not 80%, and total liquor imports were well under 9,000 gallons, mostly consumed by Company personnel/military garrisons rather than dispensed to Indians (illustrated via a detailed 1841 liquor shipment invoice and missionary **Rev. Peter Jacobs**'s 1849 testimony that Lac la Pluie Indians received only ~4 "glasses" of spirits per year each).
- **Troop withdrawal crisis (1847-48):** Earl Grey notified the Company in May 1847 that the Sixth Regiment would be withdrawn (per its original 1-year mandate and the Company's failure to fortify Fort Garry) — timed badly, coinciding with rumors (fueled by Isbister's own boasting) that the withdrawal was itself proof the Company's authority was crumbling. Simpson pushed for replacement troops, citing (misleadingly, per Galbraith — Simpson's own 1845 report was the _only_ actual evidence) supposed American subversion efforts as the pretext, since the original war-threat justification no longer applied.
- **The pensioner fiasco (1848):** Wellington refused further regular troops without proper fortifications, so the Company settled for a body of **military pensioners** (promised as elite veterans, actually — per officer complaints — an unimpressive, poorly disciplined group prone to mutiny over rations) under **Major William B. Caldwell** (also made governor of Assiniboia). Company had to fund transport, allowances, land grants. Only 42 of 56 in the first contingent were even married, contrary to expectations. Chief Factor **John Ballenden** predicted most would become Company liabilities; **Alexander Ross** blamed the "blackguard Pensioner squad" for actively worsening discipline and public order rather than restoring it.
- **The Sayer trial (1849, detailed in Ch. 4)** exposed the pensioners' complete inadequacy as a police force — notably absent from the courthouse confrontation, with Caldwell admitting they couldn't maintain order if settlers turned violent.
- **Father Belcourt's continued agitation:** after conferences with Simpson seemingly moderated his tone, Belcourt instead secured a transfer to a Pembina mission just across the US border (Diocese of Dubuque) — from this safe haven he intensified attacks, corresponded with Red River leaders (including **J. Louis Riel**, father of the future rebellion leader) urging half-breeds to settle in American territory, and helped incite sentiment ahead of the Sayer trial. Diplomatic protest via Minister **John F. Crampton** to the US government produced no results.
- **Post-Sayer demands:** half-breed leaders pressed for the removal of **Adam Thom**, the Company's unpopular legal adviser/recorder (who had prepared the Sayer prosecution, though Chief Factor Ballenden made the actual decision to prosecute) — Thom stood his ground for years despite threats but finally resigned effective July 1, 1854; separately, Major Caldwell was replaced as council president by **Eden Colvile** (Andrew Colvile's son) in 1850 under settler pressure — both changes that solved nothing structurally.
- **Simpson's deepening pessimism:** by March 1848 he privately predicted the Company would be ruined within a decade if settler free trade continued unchecked; officer **Donald Ross** (Norway House) urged the Company to proactively sell its privileges to the government "rather than await the coming storm" (quoted at length) — echoing Simpson's own 1856 suggestion (Ch. 4) to voluntarily surrender the charter, East-India-Company style.
- **Pelly's private 1848 hint to Grey** that the Company might sell its rights for a **guaranteed 10% return on capital** plus ordinary trading rights (modeled on the East India Company's own dissolution terms) drew no government response.
- **Simpson's 1850s valuation exercise** (at successor governor Andrew Colvile's request): estimated the Company's **140 posts** across all territories at a total value of **£408,000** — Rupert's Land posts £161,500; licensed territory (west of Rupert's Land) £66,500; Canada/Newfoundland £57,500; Oregon £70,500; Vancouver Island £18,000; Honolulu £4,000. With no buyer yet in sight (neither Britain nor Canada was ready), this was largely an academic exercise — but it marks the psychological turning point after which the Company's leadership broadly accepted that the fur-trade monopoly's days were numbered, setting up the negotiations (Chs. 16-18) that eventually produced the 1869 sale.

**Key Figures:**

- **Sir George Simpson** — orchestrated the military-aid strategy and later the Company's valuation exercise.
- **Lord Stanley / William E. Gladstone** — successive colonial secretaries during the 1845-46 troop debate.
- **Duke of Wellington** — obstructed/conditioned the troop deployment.
- **Lieutenant Colonel John F. Crofton** — commanded the Sixth Regiment; later a compromised witness for the Company.
- **Georges-Antoine Belcourt** — Catholic priest; author of the 1846 memorial and later Pembina-based agitator.
- **Alexander K. Isbister** — métis critic and London-based campaigner against the Company.
- **Lord Elgin** — Canadian governor-general; his 1848 report favored the Company.
- **Adam Thom** — the Company's unpopular Red River legal adviser, forced out in 1854.
- **John Henry Pelly / Andrew Colvile** — successive governors who began contemplating sale of the charter.
- **Donald Ross** — officer who urged early voluntary sale of Company rights.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Sixth Royal Regiment of Foot** — the 1846-48 British garrison at Fort Garry.
- **Pensioners** — the ineffective replacement garrison (1848).
- **Isbister/Belcourt memorial** — the 1846 humanitarian petition against the Company.
- **Valuation of 1850s** — Simpson's £408,000 estimate of Company assets.

**Important Dates:**

- **1845–46** — Oregon war scare; troop-deployment lobbying; Sixth Regiment dispatched.
- **June 1846** — Belcourt/Isbister memorial (977 signatures).
- **1847** — Isbister's London campaign begins; Grey commissions Elgin's investigation.
- **May 1847** — Notice of troop withdrawal.
- **June 1848** — Elgin's report favors the Company; pensioner contingent departs England.
- **1849** — Sayer trial; House of Commons legal-rights resolution; law officers' favorable opinion (Jan. 1850).
- **1850** — Eden Colvile replaces Caldwell.
- **1854** — Adam Thom's resignation takes effect.
- **Late 1840s/1850s** — Simpson's valuation of Company posts (£408,000).

**Significance:** Shows the Company's authority at Red River becoming a shell — propped up briefly by British troops for reasons unrelated to genuine defense needs, then collapsing once the troops left — while simultaneously surviving (barely) a serious legal and moral challenge to its monopoly in Britain. The chapter's closing turn toward contemplating a sale of the charter directly sets up Chapters 16-18's account of the Company's final political and commercial unwinding.

## Chapter 16: The Company in British and Canadian Politics during the 1850's

_(pp. 333–354)_

**Overview:** Traces the mounting political pressure on the Company from both Canadian expansionists and the British Colonial Office during the 1850s — culminating in the 1857 House of Commons Select Committee inquiry and the ultimately inconclusive 1858-59 confrontation with Colonial Secretary **Sir Edward Bulwer-Lytton** — showing how the Company survived largely because Canada and Britain each expected the _other_ to take responsibility for Rupert's Land.

**Key Points:**

- **The "frozen wilderness" myth:** most British opinion accepted an exaggerated view of Rupert's Land as uniformly unfit for settlement — ironically undercut by **Simpson's own published travel book**, _Journey Round the World_, which contained glowing descriptions of fertile river valleys (quoted at length) that later embarrassed him when select-committee questioners confronted him with his own words.
- **Canadian press agitation begins:** editors **George Brown** (_Globe_) and **William McDougall** (_North American_), initially picking up the Isbister/Kennedy charges (Ch. 15), gradually shifted toward advocating outright Canadian annexation of the "North West Territories." An amusing anecdote (Nov. 1850) has Brown, in a Toronto bookshop, wildly exaggerating Company liquor-import figures to a skeptical bookseller, overheard by Simpson's informant **Stuart Derbishire**.
- **Two annexationist camps emerge (mid-1850s):** (1) **Toronto business interests** hoping to revive a Montreal/Toronto-based fur trade by breaking the monopoly (e.g., the small, failed 1857 **Lonsdell expedition** to Red River, which fizzled due to internal quarrels); (2) a broader, growing group with no direct commercial interest, simply anxious that Rupert's Land not be lost to American expansion.
- **Demographic anxiety:** Minnesota's explosive growth (6,077 in 1850 to 172,000+ by 1860, statehood in 1858) alarmed Canadians, reinforced by American transportation inroads — the Illinois Central Railway reaching the Mississippi (1856), the first Red River steamboat (**Anson Northup**, 1858), a railroad under construction toward St. Paul, and US postal service extended to Pembina (1857) — all suggesting Red River was being drawn into the American, not Canadian, economic orbit.
- **Executive Council statements (1856-57):** **Philip Vankoughnet** (Council president) publicly asserted Canada's destiny stretched "from Lake Superior and the Hudson's Bay" to the Pacific, denying any charter could give "a few men exclusive control over half a continent" — formalized in a January 1857 council minute claiming the Pacific as Canada's proper western frontier.
- **Land-availability arguments were statistically weak:** Galbraith cites 1856-57 crown land figures showing Canada still held **over 155,000,000 acres of disposable land** (only ~7,000,000 under cultivation) — the real issue was that most remaining land was heavily wooded/inferior, while settlers increasingly wanted open prairie. Company solicitor **John Rose**'s skeptical letter to Ellice (quoted) argued the agitation was driven by "discharged servants of the Coy." with exaggerated claims, and that Canada lacked the administrative/military capacity to govern such a territory responsibly anyway.
- **Internal Company debate on strategy:** Simpson, convinced since the 1840s the monopoly's days were numbered, suggested to new governor **John Shepherd** (1856) selling out before the government realized the rights were nearly worthless — but Shepherd (drawing on his East India Company chairmanship experience) disagreed, arguing the Company should instead go on the offensive: seek license renewal and request troops, rather than signal weakness by offering surrender.
- **Ellice's high-stakes warning to the Colonial Office (1856):** in a vivid metaphor (quoted at length), **Edward Ellice** warned Colonial Secretary **Henry Labouchere** not to "lay the train" toward destroying the Company without first having "some better substitute" ready to govern the territory — stating plainly the Company was willing to sell for "a million of money" but that neither London nor Canada seemed prepared to actually take on the administrative burden.
- **Labouchere's compromise proposal:** possibly cede only the cultivable portions of Company territory to Canada while preserving the Company's monopoly elsewhere — Ellice was open to discussing this, but events overtook the idea.
- **The 1857 Select Committee:** established (Feb. 5, 1857) after Labouchere, anticipating inevitable parliamentary scrutiny, preemptively proposed the inquiry himself rather than let a hostile MP seize the initiative. Nineteen members included both Company defenders (Labouchere as chairman, **Robert Lowe**, **Sir John Pakington**, **Edward Ellice Jr.**) and its fiercest critics (**Gladstone, John A. Roebuck, Charles Fitzwilliam**). Took testimony from 25 witnesses across 550 pages.
- **Committee findings were a mixed but net-favorable outcome for the Company:** largely **vindicated the Company's treatment of Indians** (per testimony from Lt. Col. **John H. Lefroy** and Bishop **David Anderson**), and recommended the Indian-trade monopoly **continue** in areas unsuited for settlement — but sided with critics on the core point that the Company's institutional character was fundamentally incompatible with promoting colonization, recommending Canada annex portions of the chartered territory suitable for farming (contingent on Canada building communications and providing governance) but specifying no clear compensation formula. Simpson's own testimony, trying to now downplay the agricultural potential he'd once praised in print, badly damaged his credibility ("Oh, that mine enemy might write a book!").
- **Canadian testimony was surprisingly moderate:** **Chief Justice William H. Draper** (sent as Canadian observer) and **John Ross** (former Executive Council member) both spoke favorably of Company Indian policy and even urged continuation of the monopoly in non-agricultural areas — Galbraith notes both men had personal ties to Simpson (a Company clerkship secured for Draper's son; gifts to Ross) but argues their moderation reflected genuine Canadian government ambivalence, not corruption — illustrated by Rose's candid letter to Simpson describing Draper's mission as largely a face-saving gesture to "eas[e] off popular clamour" rather than a serious annexation push.
- **1857 Panic and its effect:** the financial crisis (bankruptcies, collapsed land speculation, railway-debt burdens) diverted Canadian attention entirely from the Hudson's Bay question by autumn 1857 — Derbishire gleefully reported to Simpson that "the fire of the enemy against the Company slackens," and the issue went dormant through early 1858, with Brown left as nearly a lone voice.
- **Red River troop deployment (1857):** separately from the parliamentary process, Shepherd used fabricated urgency (an alarmist Simpson letter warning of American military ambitions toward the Saskatchewan valley as an Oregon/Puget Sound rail corridor) to secure a **120-man Canadian Rifles detachment** under **Major George Seton** (arrived Red River Oct. 1857) — Colonial Office insiders privately recognized the real purpose was suppressing internal settler unrest, not defending against genuine American aggression, but Labouchere approved it anyway.
- **William Kennedy's 1857 Red River campaign:** Kennedy (Isbister's uncle, Ch. 15) toured the colony pushing a petition for Canadian annexation/protection from Company "injustices" — a rowdy March 1857 meeting saw Assiniboia governor **Francis G. Johnson** shouted down by young pro-Kennedy attendees (with merchant **Andrew Bannatyne** the lone defender of Johnson's right to speak) but Kennedy's petition gathered only 18 signatures from a ~400-person gathering — support was really just a proxy for pre-existing pro-/anti-Company sentiment, not genuine annexation enthusiasm.
- **The stalled license-renewal negotiation (1858-59):** Labouchere initially proposed renewing the trading license for 21 years conditional on the Company (1) accepting exclusion of any new colonies, (2) excluding Vancouver Island, and (3) submitting the Rupert's Land _boundary_ question to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council — the Company quickly accepted all conditions. But a change of government replaced Labouchere with novelist-politician **Sir Edward Bulwer-Lytton**, who escalated matters by also demanding the Company submit the **validity of the entire charter** (not just the boundary) to the Privy Council — a demand the Company refused as inequitable given its prior acceptance of Labouchere's terms.
- **Lytton's failed brinkmanship:** threatened non-renewal and government-initiated litigation if the Company didn't comply; tried (unsuccessfully) to pressure Canada (via Governor-General **Sir Edmund Head**, and visiting Canadian ministers **Cartier, Alexander T. Galt, John Ross**) to take up the charter challenge itself — but Canada's legislature (via a **Vankoughnet-sponsored address**, April 1859) formally declined, arguing the chartered territory was an Imperial, not provincial, responsibility. This produced a furious **George Brown** editorial (quoted) accusing the Cartier-Macdonald government of secretly favoring the Company's interests by refusing to act.
- **Stalemate resolution:** with neither Canada nor Britain willing to actually initiate legal proceedings (British law officers advised against direct government litigation), Lytton's grand plans collapsed; he fell ill and was replaced (June 1859) when **Palmerston**'s Liberals returned to power. Ellice's dry postscript on Lytton's illness — "Poor Man, he is not likely to be called upon to find a remedy for the mess he has made" — captures the anticlimactic outcome.
- **Practical result — the trading license simply lapsed (May 30, 1859)** without any real change on the ground: the Company continued trading exactly as before in the old licensed territory, since Britain never actually established an alternative government there (a proposed Duke of Newcastle magistrates bill had no practical effect, and Company officers were instructed to refuse any magistrate commissions to avoid conflicts of interest). Simpson's own dry verdict: non-renewal was merely "a side wind method" of letting the government escape a parliamentary embarrassment without changing the actual state of affairs.
- **Lasting psychological effect:** even though nothing immediately changed operationally, the license's lapse signaled that the chartered monopoly's end was now foreseeable — reinforcing the Company leadership's growing willingness to consider selling out on favorable terms, a process that would culminate first in the 1863 sale of the Company itself to new investors, then the 1869 sale of Rupert's Land to Canada (Chs. 17-18). The incoming colonial secretary, the **Duke of Newcastle** (formerly Lord Lincoln, another old Company critic from the 1848 Vancouver Island debate), continued pushing for a transcontinental British communications route — failing in this immediate goal but contributing to the momentum that led to the 1863 and 1869 sales.

**Key Figures:**

- **George Brown / William McDougall** — Canadian newspaper editors driving annexationist agitation.
- **Philip Vankoughnet** — Executive Council figure asserting Canada's Pacific destiny.
- **Henry Labouchere** — colonial secretary who initiated the 1857 Select Committee and first license-renewal terms.
- **Sir Edward Bulwer-Lytton** — colonial secretary whose escalated demands produced stalemate (1858-59).
- **Edward Ellice the Elder** — Company's chief political liaison; warned against destroying the Company without a replacement plan.
- **John Shepherd** — governor who rejected Simpson's early sell-out suggestion in favor of an offensive strategy.
- **William H. Draper / John Ross** — moderate Canadian government witnesses before the Select Committee.
- **William Kennedy** — led the 1857 Red River annexation petition campaign.
- **Sir Edmund Head** — Canadian governor-general pressed (unsuccessfully) by Lytton to initiate charter litigation.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **Select Committee of 1857** — House of Commons inquiry into the Company's territories and governance.
- **Judicial Committee of the Privy Council** — proposed venue for testing the charter's validity/boundaries.
- **License of exclusive trade** — the Company's trading monopoly west of Rupert's Land, lapsed May 30, 1859.
- **"Fertile belt"** — the agriculturally promising portion of Rupert's Land Canada sought to annex.

**Important Dates:**

- **1850–56** — Brown/McDougall press agitation begins and grows.
- **1856** — Vankoughnet's Pacific-destiny statements; Ellice's warning letter to Labouchere; Robert Lowe's Canada tour.
- **February 5, 1857** — Select Committee established.
- **May 1857** — Committee testimony period (25 witnesses).
- **June–October 1857** — Canadian Rifles sent to Red River; Kennedy's petition campaign; Panic of 1857 diverts attention.
- **1858** — Labouchere's license-renewal terms accepted by the Company; Lytton becomes colonial secretary and escalates demands.
- **April 1859** — Canada formally declines to test the charter.
- **May 30, 1859** — Trading license lapses.
- **June 1859** — Lytton falls ill; Palmerston's Liberals return to power; Duke of Newcastle becomes colonial secretary.

**Significance:** Demonstrates the political stalemate that preserved the Company's practical authority through the late 1850s — neither Canada nor Britain wanted to bear the cost and responsibility of directly governing Rupert's Land, so the Company survived by default even as its legal foundations grew shakier. This inconclusive standoff sets up the more decisive negotiations of Chapters 17-18, as mounting pressure (and the Company's own growing willingness to sell) finally produces a resolution.

## Chapter 17: Negotiations Leading to the Sale of the Company, 1859–1863

_(pp. 355–390)_

**Overview:** A detailed account of the tangled railway, telegraph, and colonization schemes of 1859-63 that culminated in the sale of the Hudson's Bay Company itself (not yet Rupert's Land) to a new group of London financiers via the **International Financial Society**, for £1,500,000 — engineered largely by promoter **Edward Watkin** with the encouragement of Colonial Secretary the **Duke of Newcastle**.

**Key Points:**

- **Opening corrective:** Galbraith cites the Company's own later governor, **Lord Strathcona**, arguing that blaming the Company alone for the slow settlement of the prairies is overdrawn — the deeper causes were poor communications and the greater pull of the American West; genuine mass settlement only came after the Canadian Pacific Railway and the exhaustion of free US land.
- **Red River's American orientation:** through the 1850s, postal/steamer/trade links tied Red River increasingly to Minnesota (St. Paul-Pembina mail from 1851, regular steamship service from 1859, a US post office at Pembina from 1857) rather than to Canada — American observer **James Wickes Taylor**'s 1860 quote captures local sentiment ("In 1851, the Governor of Minnesota visited us; in 1859 comes a Steamboat, and ten years more will bring the Railroad").
- **Failed Canadian communications efforts:** the 1857 **Gladman-Hind-Dawson expedition** (funded £5,000 by the Canadian legislature) explored a Lake Superior-to-Red River route but found costs (£50,000-200,000) far beyond what Canada, already financially strained, could support — the project was abandoned.
- **Early transcontinental railway dreamers (1848-52):** **Major Robert Carmichael-Smyth**, **Allan McDonell** (a Company foe allied with Kennedy/Isbister), **Captain Millington H. Synge**, and **Alexander Douell** all proposed transcontinental schemes but lacked real capital or government backing.
- **The North West Transportation Company (1858):** organized by **William McD. Dawson** (with Kennedy and McDonell) explicitly to bypass the Company's consent, secured a Canadian mail contract via a Georgian Bay-Fort William steamer route — but the scheme collapsed due to unreliable service (Simpson documented an absurd case of one route taking from October to March compared to a US-routed letter's one month) and settler preference for the established American mail route through St. Paul; Simpson eventually withdrew Company cooperation (1859), and the project folded by 1860.
- **English capital enters the picture:** the failed **Halifax and Quebec Railway Company** (1858) — backed by figures like **Viscount Bury**, **Samuel Cunard**, and **George Grenfell Glyn** (of the Grand Trunk-linked Glyn, Mills bank) — sought a £60,000 British subsidy matched by colonial governments, but the Colonial Office (under **Bulwer-Lytton**) refused public money for colonial railways, killing the scheme. **Viscount Bury**'s grandiose vision of an all-British Halifax-to-Pacific/India communications link (bypassing the vulnerable Suez route) was the intellectual seed for later, larger schemes.
- **The North West Transit Company (1859):** an Anglo-Canadian amalgamation (Bury, **Sir Allan MacNab**, **Charles Fitzwilliam**, **Robert Benson**, the Glyns) aiming for a Canada-to-Pacific route — Simpson cooperated with an 1859 exploratory mission (Pascoe Glyn, Nathaniel Clayton) specifically hoping their report would _discourage_ investors by revealing the route's difficulty; the scheme died for lack of government subsidy.
- **The Duke of Newcastle's colonial vision (from mid-1859):** genuinely believed in developing British North America's interior, but recognized Canada couldn't afford annexation and Gladstone (Chancellor of the Exchequer) would never fund it from the Imperial treasury — concluded any new settlement must start as a **crown colony** at minimal Imperial cost.
- **Newcastle's 1860 bill:** proposed the Company surrender the Red River/Saskatchewan districts within 5 years, with compensation limited to physical improvements/losses — explicitly **excluding payment for the chartered rights themselves**, treating them as unestablished claims. Governor **Henry Hulse Berens** flatly refused to be "a party to its own confiscation," insisting on recognition of the Company's title. Internal Colonial Office dissent (land commissioner **T. W. C. Murdoch**'s memorable warning that any purchased territory would eventually just fall to American expansion anyway, since it was militarily indefensible; undersecretary **Sir Frederic Rogers**'s objection that denying the Company's "undoubted right" — per the government's own law officers — was unjustifiable) stalled the bill for six months without resolution.
- **Escalating pressure (1861):** rumored (exaggerated) gold discoveries on the North Saskatchewan, and the uncertain state of Anglo-American relations during the **American Civil War**, made Newcastle more receptive to private capital's transcontinental railway/telegraph proposals.
- **The British North American Association (formed January 30, 1862):** brought together Grand Trunk Railway bondholders, intercolonial-railway advocates, and North West Transit Company interests under a common lobbying banner — key figures included **Robert Wigram Crawford**, **Joseph Nelson**, **Thomas Baring**, **George Carr Glyn**, and (crucially) **Edward Watkin**.
- **Edward Watkin's outsized role:** hired by Glyn and Baring merely to fix the ailing **Grand Trunk Railway**, Watkin instead championed a grand vision of a full Atlantic-to-Pacific railway, forging a close alliance with the like-minded Newcastle (a July 1861 meeting) — both men lacked the capital to realize their visions alone and depended on reluctant financiers (Baring, Glyn) who were mainly interested in shoring up the Grand Trunk and securing the more modest intercolonial railway.
- **Canadian maneuvering (1862):** Georges-Étienne Cartier's government, unable/unwilling to fund western annexation or litigation against the charter, proposed (via secretary **Charles Alleyn**, April 1862) that the Company cooperate on a telegraph/road to British Columbia — really aimed at cheap political credit rather than genuine commitment. Company governor of Rupert's Land **Alexander Grant Dallas** (Simpson's successor) saw an opportunity to use this proposal to pressure Britain into eventually buying the Company out, telling Ellice candidly, "I do not mind playing into his [Cartier's] hands so long as it suits our own game" — but his unauthorized direct correspondence with Cartier caused an internal Company uproar (governor Berens and Ellice both objected, for different reasons, to Dallas's overreach and his implicitly anti-settlement framing).
- **The financiers' July 1862 application:** **Thomas Baring, George Carr Glyn**, and others (including **Kirkman Daniel Hodgson**, who was also on the Company's own committee) formally requested land grants (not cash subsidies, since Parliament wouldn't fund those) to build a transcontinental road/telegraph — Galbraith clarifies this was really Newcastle/Watkin's project wearing the "big names'" borrowed credibility, not a genuine Grand Trunk initiative; Baring in fact remained skeptical throughout and only reluctantly let his name be used.
- **The decisive turn — Berens names a price (Nov. 17-18, 1862):** when Newcastle pressed for a substantial land grant (not just a narrow right-of-way) for the telegraph promoters, Berens, exasperated, reportedly burst out (per Watkin's account) that such a grant would "sequester our very tap-root" and asked why, if the promoters were so patriotic, they didn't simply **buy the Company outright** — naming **£1,500,000** as the price (a figure he'd already floated to Murdoch in 1861, and which Newcastle had separately mentioned in the House of Lords in July 1862).
- **Watkin's pivot to purchasing the Company outright:** having failed to secure a British government subsidy (rebuffed decisively by Gladstone in Feb. 1863, who opposed a land-telegraph subsidy precedent even after a fiery Watkin letter — quoted at length — accusing Newcastle's crown-colony preference of masking a deliberate strategy to keep the territory British-controlled rather than let it become "a mere extension of Canada"), Watkin turned to actually buying the Company.
- **Company self-valuation (early 1863):** accountant **Edward Roberts** valued Rupert's Land chartered rights at £1,500,000 and all other Company property (Vancouver Island, British Columbia, Canada, Oregon possessory rights) at £491,748 — total assets nearly £2,000,000.
- **Negotiation and terms (Feb.-May 1863):** Watkin's request to inspect the Company's account books outright was refused (protecting long-guarded secrecy), but a compromise let him view abstracts at Hudson's Bay House; final terms (agreed May 13, 1863): the entire Company stock sold for **£1,500,000** cash, most payable within 2-4 months, with new owners pledging to respect existing officers'/servants' rights.
- **Financing via the International Financial Society:** since the reluctant Baring/Glyn group wouldn't personally raise the full sum, Watkin turned (at Glyn's suggestion) to the newly formed **International Financial Society** (est. May 11, 1863, a broad London merchant-banking consortium, notably excluding Baring Brothers itself) — Robert Benson (the society's president) and representative **Richard Potter** finalized the purchase agreement (June 15, 1863): stock bought at **£300 for every £100** of old par value.
- **The chief factors/traders' outrage:** the "wintering partners" (profit-sharing field officers, per the 1821 union terms) learned of the sale only through rumor — six chief factors led by **George Barnston** wrote in Jan. 1863 asking about reported negotiations, and were told (misleadingly, given what we know was already underway) that surrender of the charter was "not the least probable." Historian **Joseph J. Hargrave**'s description of their reaction — "stupefaction quickly succeeded by...deep indignation" — captures the betrayal felt by men whose careers depended on continuity of the old fur-trade structure.
- **New Company organized:** stock set at **£2,000,000** (quadrupling the old nominal value, after the International Financial Society had already tripled it in the purchase) — new governor **Sir Edmund Head** (on Newcastle's recommendation), with financiers **Richard Potter, Daniel Meinertzhagen, James S. Hodgson, J.H.W. Schröder**, plus continuity figures **Eden Colvile** and **George Lyall** from the old board, and American-born-turned-British fur merchant **Curtis Miranda Lampson** for trade expertise. Even skeptical old **Edward Ellice** grudgingly admitted the new board was "a most respectable and powerful association."
- **The fateful prospectus (June 1863):** promised investors that Company land would be opened to "liberal and systematic" European colonization using existing trading-post infrastructure, without acknowledging the immense practical difficulties (communications, governance, capital) that had doomed every prior colonization scheme in the book (PSAC, Vancouver Island, etc.) — this overselling would generate years of shareholder disputes once profits failed to materialize as promised.
- **International Financial Society's profit:** contributed £200,000 in working capital as a condition of the new board's acceptance, but reaped a gross profit of nearly £300,000 on the whole transaction — though its shareholding (17,140 shares as of March 1864) diminished over time (down to 3,000 by June 1867) as control diffused among 1,700+ new shareholders, who — unlike the old closed proprietary circle — proved frequently critical and contentious at general meetings.
- **Chapter's closing note:** despite widespread 1863 expectations of a "new era," the fundamental problems (British/Canadian reluctance to fund settlement, difficulty of governance, communications barriers) remained essentially unchanged after the sale — only the price tag for eventually extinguishing the Company's rights had gone up, since the stock's value had just quadrupled.

**Key Figures:**

- **Edward Watkin** — chief promoter/deal-maker behind both the transit schemes and the Company's purchase.
- **Duke of Newcastle** — colonial secretary; Watkin's political ally and champion of crown-colony settlement.
- **William E. Gladstone** — Chancellor of the Exchequer; blocked all government subsidies for the schemes.
- **Henry Hulse Berens** — HBC governor who named the £1,500,000 sale price.
- **Alexander Grant Dallas** — Rupert's Land governor whose unauthorized dealings with Cartier caused internal Company controversy.
- **Thomas Baring / George Carr Glyn / Robert Benson** — reluctant London financiers drawn into the schemes.
- **Sir Edmund Head** — new governor of the reorganized Company (1863).
- **George Barnston** — led the chief factors' protest over being blindsided by the sale.

**Key Terms & Concepts:**

- **International Financial Society** — the London consortium that financed the 1863 purchase.
- **British North American Association** — lobbying coalition for transcontinental railway/telegraph subsidies.
- **North West Transit Company / Transportation Company** — rival Canadian-British schemes for western communications.
- **"Wintering partners"** — the chief factors/traders sidelined by the 1863 sale negotiations.

**Important Dates:**

- **1857–58** — Gladman-Hind-Dawson expedition; North West Transportation Company formed.
- **1858** — Halifax and Quebec Railway Company fails.
- **1859** — North West Transit Company formed; Newcastle becomes colonial secretary.
- **May 1860** — Newcastle's compensation-limited bill proposed; Berens refuses.
- **January 30, 1862** — British North American Association formed.
- **April 1862** — Alleyn-Dallas correspondence on telegraph cooperation.
- **July 5, 1862** — Financiers' application for land grants.
- **November 17–18, 1862** — Berens names the £1,500,000 sale price.
- **February 1863** — Gladstone rejects telegraph subsidy; Watkin begins purchase negotiations.
- **May 13, 1863** — Purchase terms agreed (£1,500,000).
- **May 11, 1863** — International Financial Society formed.
- **June 15, 1863** — Final sale agreement completed.

**Significance:** Explains the mechanics of the 1863 sale of the Hudson's Bay Company itself — a transaction driven by railway/telegraph promotional schemes and London speculative finance more than by any coherent government colonization policy — setting the stage for the final chapter's account of how the _new_ Company then negotiated the sale of Rupert's Land itself to the Dominion of Canada in 1869.

## Chapter 18: The End of the Monopoly

_(pp. 391–428)_

**Overview:** The book's culminating chapter — a six-year "triangular" negotiation (1863-69) among the Company, Britain, and Canada, each hoping the others would bear the cost of settling Rupert's Land, resolved only when Colonial Secretary **Lord Granville** issued an outright ultimatum in 1869. Ends with the **Riel Rebellion**, the formal transfer of Rupert's Land to the new Dominion of Canada (1870), and Galbraith's final assessment of the Company's historical role.

**Key Points:**

- **The triangular deadlock's core dynamic:** the Company (now a share-issuing corporation whose 1863 stock price depended on promised land-sale profits) needed compensation; Britain wanted the territory secured for the Empire but refused Imperial-treasury expenditure; Canada wanted the land but refused to pay for it, insisting Britain (which had "created" the Company's privileges) should bear the cost. Four colonial secretaries in succession (Newcastle, Cardwell, Carnarvon, Buckingham, then Granville) all agreed the charter was probably legal and shouldn't be challenged, yet none could break the stalemate.
- **The telegraph subplot (1863-64):** Watkin, now a major shareholder, tried to fast-track his transcontinental telegraph independently of the board's authority (unauthorized agreements with the **Montreal Telegraph Company**'s **O. S. Wood**) — rebuked by new governor **Sir Edmund Head** for overstepping. The Company nonetheless bought ~70+ tons of telegraph wire and sent retired Arctic explorer **John Rae** to survey the route (1864) — but the whole project collapsed when the Canadian cabinet (Feb. 1864) abandoned its funding commitment on a technicality (claiming the new plan omitted the promised road), really just seeking an excuse to avoid expense; the wire sat unused at Red River, Victoria, and York Factory.
- **Head's first land offer (Nov. 1863):** proposed splitting the "fertile belt" 50/50 between the Company and the Crown (with the Company also getting exclusive telegraph-construction rights and a cut of mining fees) — Newcastle rejected this as reproducing the old "Pelly/Ellice" assumption that government must protect a private company's vested rights, countering with a much stingier offer: 1 shilling/acre on land sales up to a **£150,000** cap, plus a share of mining revenue up to £100,000 — a huge gap between the two positions.
- **Leverage attempts on both sides:** the Colonial Office tried (unsuccessfully) to use Canada's (weak) claim that the fertile belt belonged to Canada as heir to France to pressure the Company into concessions — Head cleverly turned this against them by using the "disputed title" claim as an excuse to suspend telegraph construction, which Newcastle (eager for the telegraph) didn't want; the Company, in turn, tried to leverage its manifest inability to police the border (illustrated vividly by the **1863 Sioux refugee crisis**, where Rupert's Land governor **Alexander Grant Dallas** unilaterally let US cavalry cross the border to pursue fleeing Sioux — sharply criticized by the _Montreal Evening Telegraph_ as a de facto surrender of British sovereignty) as proof that only a properly funded government (i.e., one that paid the Company) could actually secure the territory.
- **Head raises to £1,000,000 (April 1864):** just as Newcastle might have responded, he resigned (ill health) and was replaced by **Edward Cardwell**, who took a fundamentally different tack: rather than create a crown colony, he decided Rupert's Land must go directly to **Canada** — informing the Company it would now have to negotiate with Canada, not Britain.
- **George Brown's mission (Dec. 1864):** Canada (with Brown now in the coalition cabinet pushing for confederation and western annexation) sent Brown to London — but as a longtime hostile Company critic he made no progress, dismissively arguing the whole of Rupert's Land wasn't worth anywhere near £1,000,000.
- **The June 1865 Cardwell-Canada agreement:** transferred the entire matter to Canada — Rupert's Land would go to Canada "subject to such rights as the Hudson's Bay Company might be able to establish," with compensation (if any) via a Canadian loan under Imperial guarantee. Crucially, **the Company itself was never consulted** in reaching this arrangement — a fact that outraged shareholders once it became known.
- **Shareholder revolt building:** furious at directors' perceived passivity and the unrealized promises of the 1863 prospectus, an increasingly vocal shareholder faction (including **Edward Watkin**, bankers **Junius S. Morgan** and **George Peabody**) organized outside meetings.
- **The mysterious McEwen affair (Jan. 1866):** a shadowy Glasgow financier, **Alexander McEwen** (probably fronting for Watkin's circle, per Galbraith's analysis), inquired about buying the Company's cultivable land for an "Anglo-American" colonization syndicate — the directors' surprisingly encouraging response (despite the June 1865 agreement technically barring such a sale) was almost certainly a pressure tactic aimed at Canada, and was recognized as such by Canadian officials **Cartier** and **McDougall**, who accused the Company of "concoct[ing]" the scheme purely "for the Stock Market."
- **The London Tavern shareholder revolt (Jan.-July 1866):** ~150 shareholders (about 10% of all proprietors) met and heard **James A. Dodds** deliver a grandiose speech valuing the Company's total assets at **£5,000,000** and urging immediate self-funded colonization — a resolution demanding either £5,000,000 or independent colonization passed unanimously. A follow-up vote of no-confidence in the directors (July 5, 1866 general court) was defeated by mail ballot (122 shareholders/5,308 shares in favor vs. 496/53,942 shares against) — a decisive defeat, but with real minority discontent (roughly 1/5 of voting shares) that the Chairman, **Sir Edmund Head**, had to actively manage by pointing out the practical impossibility of the Company enforcing its own laws/land sales without Crown backing.
- **American expansionist pressure mounts:** **James W. Taylor**, US Treasury special agent in Minnesota (1859-69) and long-time annexationist, used an 1866 congressional commercial-relations inquiry as cover to draft an annexation bill proposing **$10,000,000** compensation to the Company for all of British North America — introduced in Congress (July 1866) by **Nathaniel P. Banks** but withdrawn on Secretary of State **William H. Seward**'s advice to avoid complicating British relations. Taylor's report nonetheless warned that "Americanization" of the Saskatchewan region (driven by Montana gold-rush population growth) was likely regardless of formal government action.
- **British ambivalence toward American absorption:** remarkably, several Colonial Office officials (**Sir Frederic Rogers**, undersecretary **C. B. Adderley**) privately expressed near-indifference to whether Rupert's Land ended up American or Canadian, given its indefensibility and cost — only the desire to complete Canadian federation smoothly kept the British government engaged at all.
- **Border crisis diplomacy:** cross-border Sioux/Indian conflict incidents (1866-67) prompted formal US protests (Minister **Charles Francis Adams**, April 1867, conveying **Seward**'s request that US troops be allowed to pursue hostile Indians into British territory) that embarrassed the Colonial Office — new Colonial Secretary the **Duke of Buckingham** tried to shift responsibility to the Company (which admitted it could do nothing) but Governor **Sir Edmund Head** cleverly used an **Arrowsmith map** to show much of the disputed border country actually drained away from Hudson Bay and thus fell under direct British, not Company, jurisdiction — a technical point that didn't resolve the underlying governance vacuum.
- **Local anarchy at Red River:** with Company authority hollowed out, self-appointed factions competed — **Dr. John Schultz** (editor of the _Nor'Wester_) led a pro-Canada faction; a smaller pro-annexation-to-US group existed; and the almost comic **Thomas Spence** episode (Dec. 1866-Jan. 1868) saw a self-important surveyor stage a tiny, poorly attended meeting to declare a "Grand Confederation" memorial, then later (at Portage la Prairie) proclaim an entirely unauthorized "government of Manitoba" with himself as president, attempting (unsuccessfully) to levy taxes — quickly shut down by a Colonial Office warning about "grave responsibilities," but symbolically demonstrating the total absence of effective authority.
- **Post-Confederation acceleration (July 1867 onward):** the new Dominion government (Buckingham urging Governor-General **Monck** to press for action) sought direct annexation under **Article 146 of the British North America Act**, but law officers advised Britain could not legally transfer authority without both an Act of Parliament AND the Company's consent — creating the **Rupert's Land Act (passed July 31, 1868)**, which authorized (but did not mandate) the transfer once Canada and the Company reached agreement.
- **Ongoing haggling over price/terms (1867-69):** successive proposals — Governor **Earl of Kimberley**'s terms (retain posts + 6,000 acres each, 1 shilling/acre up to £1,000,000 cap, various land-selection rights) were deemed too generous by Buckingham; his own counterproposal (smaller land reservations, capped £1,000,000) was rejected by incoming governor **Sir Stafford Northcote** as inadequate once the Conservative government fell and Gladstone's Liberals (with new Colonial Secretary **Lord Granville**) took power. Canada (via delegates **Cartier and McDougall**) countered by attacking the Company for alleged indifference to 1868 Red River famine relief (a charge Galbraith calls unjust, since the Company had in fact provided relief) and offering a maximum of just **£100,000**.
- **Granville's ultimatum (March 9, 1869):** decisive final terms — **£300,000** payment, Company retains its posts plus **one-twentieth of the fertile belt** (precisely defined boundaries given), Canada to purchase the stranded telegraph materials at cost. Backed by the implicit threat of Privy Council litigation (likely to favor the Company, raising Canada's eventual cost) if either side refused.
- **Both sides accepted reluctantly:** Cartier accepted quickly; McDougall resisted briefly but had no real alternative (even the hostile _Toronto Globe_ praised Granville's evenhandedness). Within the Company, three directors (**Richard Potter, Edward W. T. Hamilton, George Lyall**) wanted to defy Granville entirely, but the majority (led by deputy governor **Sir Curtis Lampson**) accepted, with minor modifications sought (no export duty on furs; one-tenth rather than one-twentieth of the fertile belt) that Canada refused to grant as more than "details."
- **Final shareholder ratification (March 25-April 9, 1869):** a stormy general court meeting (cries of "Treachery!") was adjourned and reconvened, eventually approving the settlement by roughly 2-to-1 — the recording secretary's unusually candid minute acknowledged the minority's argument was "the more cogent" but that practical necessity prevailed.
- **The Riel Rebellion (1869-70):** before the transfer could be completed, Red River **métis** under **Louis Riel**, anxious about their status under incoming Canadian rule (having been left entirely out of the negotiations), organized armed resistance — Canada refused to complete the transfer until order was restored, while the Company argued responsibility for policing during the interim lay with Britain/Canada, not itself; recriminations flew (Deputy Governor Lampson blamed Canadian officials' premature land surveys for provoking the unrest). Granville considered (but avoided) both a one-year period of direct Imperial rule and a Red River plebiscite. The crisis was resolved via the **Manitoba Act**, negotiated directly with Red River delegates; the rebellion "collapsed" May 11, 1870.
- **Final transfer:** payment made via Canadian representative **Sir John Rose**; **Rupert's Land officially became part of the Dominion of Canada on July 15, 1870.**
- **Galbraith's closing verdict:** Both the fur-trade monopoly and the Red River métis who resisted it were, in different ways, "victims of a civilization to which they could not be reconciled" — casualties of the advance of an agricultural-industrial society neither could ultimately withstand. Contrary to shareholder fears at the time, the 1869 settlement proved **highly profitable in the long run**: 20th-century prairie settlement brought the Company huge revenues from land sales and its retail/department-store network, and the fur trade itself remained more durable than Simpson's own pessimistic predictions. Galbraith firmly **rejects the "Stop-the-Way Company" charge** leveled at the Company by contemporary critics: prairie settlement was gated not by Company obstruction but by the absence of adequate communications and the greater pull of the American frontier — Canada annexed the territory "almost as soon as it was able to assume responsibility for effective government," and real settlement only began once the American frontier closed and the Canadian Pacific Railway was built. The Company's role — pursuing profit throughout — nonetheless constituted a genuine, historically important contribution to the expansion and consolidation of the British Empire in North America.

# BOOK-WIDE SYNTHESIS

## The Central Thesis, Restated

Galbraith's argument, sustained across all four parts, is that the Hudson's Bay Company was not merely a commercial enterprise operating within the British Empire but was, in the territories west and north of Canada, the **effective agent of British imperial policy itself**. Its officers' pursuit of profit — not any deliberate colonial strategy conceived in London — determined the boundaries Britain claimed against Russia (Part II) and the United States (Part III), and its commercial dominance later became the chief obstacle Canada had to negotiate around in order to become a transcontinental nation (Part IV).

## The Recurring Analytical Pattern

Nearly every chapter tests the same underlying proposition, first stated in Chapter 1: **the Company's "frontier policy" — aggressive, loss-absorbing price competition — was highly effective against rival fur traders (NWC survivors, the American Fur Company, Kittson, Russian competitors) but essentially powerless against agricultural or mineral settlement** (Red River métis, Oregon settlers, Vancouver Island colonists, Fraser River/Cariboo/Saskatchewan gold miners). Track this pattern as a throughline:

- **Effective against traders:** Canadian frontier (Ch. 3), southern frontier pre-1840s (Ch. 4), Snake country "fur desert" (Ch. 5), Pacific coast (Chs. 7–8).
- **Ineffective against settlers/miners:** Red River free trade and the Sayer trial (Chs. 4, 15), Oregon (Chs. 9–13), Puget's Sound Agricultural Company (Ch. 10), Vancouver Island colonization (Ch. 14), the Fraser River and Cariboo gold rushes (Ch. 8), the final loss of Rupert's Land (Chs. 16–18).

## Three Diplomatic/Commercial Settlements Compared

A useful comparative framework for essay questions:

||Russian boundary (1825, Ch. 6)|Oregon boundary (1846, Chs. 9–13)|Rupert's Land sale (1869, Chs. 15–18)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Opposing party's motive|Fur-trade rivalry (RAC)|Settler nationalism ("manifest destiny")|Agricultural/national expansion (Canada)|
|Outcome for HBC|Favorable (Pelly dictated terms to Canning)|Unfavorable (Aberdeen sacrificed HBC interests for peace)|Mixed (Company got compensation, lost sovereignty)|
|Government's stake|Low (Britain), low-medium (Russia)|Low (Britain) vs. high (US popular sentiment)|Both Britain and Canada wanted to avoid cost|
|Company's leverage|Total (only source of information)|High initially, collapsed after 1843 migration|Declining, but real (Privy Council threat)|

## Key Recurring Figures Across Multiple Chapters

- **George Simpson** (Chs. 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17) — overseas governor 1826–60; architect of frontier policy; increasingly pessimistic about the monopoly's future from the 1840s onward.
- **John Henry Pelly** (Chs. 1, 2, 6, 7, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14) — HBC governor 1822–52; dictated terms of the Russian treaty; secured Oregon Treaty's possessory-rights clauses.
- **James Douglas** (Chs. 1, 5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14) — rose from Columbia district officer to governor of Vancouver Island/British Columbia; originated the "possessory rights" concept.
- **Peter Skene Ogden** (Chs. 1, 5, 7, 11, 12, 13) — led Snake country expeditions; central figure in the Dryad incident and the Oregon settlement's aftermath.
- **John McLoughlin** (Chs. 1, 5, 9, 10, 11, 12) — Columbia district Chief Factor; humanitarian dealings with American settlers that repeatedly clashed with London's diplomatic priorities.
- **Andrew Colvile / Edward Ellice / Henry Hulse Berens** — successive governors/political liaisons managing the Company's relationship with Whitehall through the 1850s–60s.
- **William E. Gladstone** (Chs. 14, 15, 16, 17) — the Company's most persistent and ideologically committed parliamentary critic across two decades.

- **1763** — French removed from Canada (Treaty of Paris).
- **1784** — North West Company formed.
- **1811** — Red River colony founded (Selkirk).
- **1821** — HBC–NWC coalition; British trade monopoly licensed west of Rupert's Land.
- **1824–25** — Russo-American Convention; Anglo-Russian Treaty (boundary with Russian America).
- **1825** — Fort Vancouver founded.
- **1839** — Hamburg Agreement (HBC leases Russian coastal strip).
- **1843** — Great Migration to Oregon; Champoeg provisional government.
- **1846** — Oregon Treaty (49th parallel, Vancouver Island retained, possessory rights guaranteed).
- **1849** — Sayer trial (Red River free trade established in practice); Vancouver Island granted to HBC.
- **1854** — Crimean War neutrality agreement (Pacific coast).
- **1857–58** — House of Commons Select Committee; Fraser River gold rush; British Columbia created; HBC trading license expires (May 1859).
- **1863** — Sale of the Hudson's Bay Company itself to the International Financial Society (£1,500,000).
- **1863–69** — Arbitration and settlement of the Oregon possessory-rights dispute ($650,000 awarded).
- **1867** — Alaska sold to the US; Canadian Confederation (British North America Act).
- **1868** — Rupert's Land Act.
- **1869** — Granville's ultimatum; Company accepts £300,000 + land reservation for surrender of Rupert's Land; Riel Rebellion begins.
- **1870** — Manitoba Act; Rupert's Land formally transferred to Canada (July 15).
