# Unsettled — more notes (to append) From Claude

## The setup (Lord Selkirk)

- The whole thing is Lord Selkirk's project. He bought a big stake in the HBC and got "colonization rights" to ~300,000 km² (called Assiniboia, about 4x the size of Scotland) to settle displaced Highland Scots — at a spot he'd never seen.
- Settlers were basically economic refugees from the Highland Clearances, told they were going to empty, available farmland. It wasn't empty — First Nations, Métis, and the NWC all had claims.
- Selkirk's officers ("Gentlemen") had never been to western North America and were badly unsuited to the diplomacy the situation needed.
- Selkirk only reached Red River in 1817, after the battle. Seized the NWC HQ at Fort William on the way, retook Fort Douglas, gave settlers free title to their farms, signed the Selkirk Treaty (first land treaty west of the Great Lakes). Died of TB in 1820, age 48. The book pins a real share of guilt on him.

## The settlers' ordeal

- 1813 group (~100 Highlanders) hit by typhus on the ship, dumped at Churchill instead of York Factory, forced to winter in arctic hovels.
- Spring 1814: ~50 of them walked across the frozen taiga to York Factory (12-day march) to catch the inland boats.
- The author, Robert Lower, is descended from one of them — Samuel Lamont, a 22-year-old millwright from Islay. The book is built from the eyewitness Selkirk Papers journals.

## Pemmican Proclamation — deeper

- Macdonell issued it Jan 8, 1814 (export ban on all provisions for a year), genuinely fearing famine — second winter of scarcity. But it was an unenforceable fantasy over a territory 4x Scotland.
- Classic soldier move: reached for command + force ("iron fist, face of stone") when the moment called for diplomacy. He didn't understand the world he was in — the Métis, the fur economy, who actually had claims.
- The NWC was angry because pemmican fed their voyageurs and was the basis of the Métis (Bois-Brûlé) economy and way of life.
- KEY: a Nor'Wester, Dugald Cameron, deliberately fed Macdonell a story that Métis hunters were driving the buffalo herds away from the NWC's southern posts, and induced him to ban "running the buffalo" — on purpose, to inflame the Métis. This comes out in 1815 when Robertson gets Seraphin Lamar to confess it (Ch 22); Robertson calls it one of Macdonell's most impolitic acts and says Cameron's sole purpose was to stir up the Métis. So the grievance was engineered.
- (Watch the two Camerons: Dugald = engineered the edict; Duncan = the one who talked the settlers into deserting in 1815.)
- The 1814 standoff actually got defused: NWC partner John McDonald of Garth brokered a win-win pemmican-for-oatmeal swap. The Nor'Westers were businessmen who'd deal; Macdonell was the rigid one.

## The FIRST destruction of the colony (1815)

- Hard winter + Macdonell's short rations and arbitrary rules = settlers fed up.
- Duncan Cameron (NWC) offered free passage to Upper Canada and talked over half the colonists into deserting.
- NWC then turned on the holdouts; gunfire, three wounded, one dead.
- Miles Macdonell surrendered himself to the NWC hoping to save the colony — changed nothing.
- Survivors fled north to HBC posts on Lake Winnipeg. Colony #1 = dead.

## Robertson's rescue and rebuild (1815)

- The author clearly makes Colin Robertson (HBC) the hero of the book — Part III is built around him, "A Wellington in Words."
- He rallied the scattered settlers and led them back to The Forks in summer 1815.
- New governor Robert Semple arrived that fall with 84 more migrants. Colony back in business.

## The 1816 buildup

- Over winter the NWC convinced the Bois-Brûlés their food and way of life were at stake. Cuthbert Grant raised a mounted Métis militia.
- Robertson consolidated Fort Douglas for a siege.
- Robertson vs Semple feud escalated all spring. Semple's smug "my title will cow a gang of illiterate buffalo hunters" attitude was fatal. Part of why he ignored Robertson: deputy Alex Macdonell was jealous and turned Semple against him whenever Robertson left the room (Semple was reasonable one-on-one).
- Robertson finally left for good ~June 11–14, days before the battle — partly principle, partly wounded pride. (The book also notes he misjudged the Métis himself; they reportedly wanted to flay him.)

## The battle (June 19, 1816)

- Semple marched out with ~26 men on foot to confront 60+ mounted Métis. Fatal mismatch.
- Unclear who fired first; consensus leans toward the colonists' side.
- Semple wounded early; his men clustered around him instead of scattering and got cut down by a volley. ~21 colonists killed (incl. Semple) vs one Métis death.
- Then came looting and mutilation of the corpses — the part that drove 150 years of "Seven Oaks Massacre" framing. The book notes battlefield looting was standard for the era (without excusing it).
- Survivors surrendered the fort next day; ~150 people expelled north again, reached safety July 6. Colony #2 = dead.

## Cuthbert Grant — nuance

- His plan was to surprise and surround the colony, not fight on the open plain — the field battle happened because his men were spotted. So "wasn't really his intention that night" is fair for the battle, but destroying/expelling the colony WAS the plan.
- He intervened to stop the prisoners being murdered, against his own side's wishes.
- Book's verdict: bears a large share of responsibility, "excused" not "exonerated."
- Later became Warden of the Plains (HBC) and a founding leader of the Métis Nation.

## Aftermath

- The homeward ship (Prince of Wales) hit ice at Hudson's Strait, turned back, and wintered at Moose Factory on James Bay — didn't escape until Aug 1817. (Robertson actually left for Montreal overland before the ship's final leg.)
- Nobody was ever convicted — Robertson, Grant, and various NWC men all walked. Trials were a mess: no evidence, witnesses hauled thousands of km, unclear jurisdiction. Coltman's official inquiry was a political whitewash (he was himself an NWC shareholder).
- HBC and NWC merged in 1821; monopoly restored.
- Long term: settlement survives, but Métis become the dominant population (6:1 over Europeans by the 1840s) and forge their national identity — feeding straight into the 1870 Red River Resistance and everything after.

## What actually caused it (bigger than "HBC hubris")

1. A commercial death-struggle between two companies (HBC vs NWC) with profits gutted by competition. The colony sat across the NWC's pemmican supply line, so the NWC saw it as an HBC weapon and wanted it gone. The violence was driven largely by the NWC, not just HBC bungling.
2. Deeper cause: colonial contempt. Selkirk and his officers dismissed and trampled the Métis way of life (the edicts being the spark), repeatedly generating the hostility that finally exploded.
3. Personalities mattered hugely — Macdonell's rigidity, Semple's arrogance, Robertson's pride, Alex Macdonell's jealousy. Lower's whole point is that the "good guys vs bad guys" version misses a chain of avoidable human decisions.
4. It's the origin point of the larger story: where trade between Indigenous peoples and Europeans tipped into imperial dispossession.

One-liner: not just "HBC screwed up" — a two-company trade war, inflamed by colonial contempt for the Métis and a grievance the NWC engineered on purpose, executed by proud and inexperienced men, that turned a farming colony into the flashpoint of Western dispossession.
