			- **15. Class without class consciousness.** A theoretical point Burley keeps returning to: she's arguing that HBC servants had a _common identity and culture_ that enabled collective action, without claiming they had nineteenth-century industrial class consciousness. She's pushing back against Ron Bourgeault (who argued the voyageurs were "advanced elements of the working class") on one side and against Brown/Payne (who emphasized vertical household ties and pre-industrial harmony) on the other. The plebeian culture she describes — drinking, rough sexuality, profligacy, suspicion of authority, collective bargaining via foot-dragging and combination — is shared across ethnicities and across the HBC's century, and it's what makes her argument work.
- Retrenchment brought real transformations, there was a fairly steady system till Britain gained quebec, at which point competition put pressure on profits, drove away employees, while moving inland struggled. Eventually the retrenchment of more standard industrial labour practices, more strict hierarchy, and more unified if differing wage scales, slower advancements, and other issues came with it. It allowed for much greater profitability.
	- "Britain gained Quebec" as the start of competition: Burley actually emphasizes the formation of the North West Company in 1804 and the period from 1770 onward (when the HBC was forced inland by Montreal-based "pedlars") as the competitive pressure point. The conquest of Quebec is upstream of that but isn't her framing.

	- **The "moral economy" framing.** Burley leans heavily on E.P. Thompson, Hans Medick on plebeian culture, and the language of moral economy. The most serious conflicts erupted not over wages per se but when superiors "transgressed the moral economy" — bad provisions, cruel officers, breaking customary holiday/Sunday rest, withholding traditional rations of grog. Servants accepted hierarchy but expected reciprocal obligations.
- the legal evolution of the servant's contract (1691 oath → 1795 contract → 1811 short form → 1815/1819 revisions → 1870 Sinclair Young's contract) and the company's eventual, painful discovery in the 1840s–50s that the contract was nearly unenforceable. The Master and Servant Act of 1823 may not even have applied to contracts to serve outside the UK; prosecutions were costly, the men were too poor to pay damages, and the company had to pay to keep them in debtors' prison. By 1859 the committee essentially gave up enforcement and just raised wages and added tea and sugar.
	- Basically, the contract tried ot be used as a tool, but it was hard. In terms of contracts, Canada coulnd't try them, the HBC was sovering, but london wanted cases refrred to canada int he 1800s, before then, as the men were on the caost iwth few options, they often suffered earlier on.
	- on the west coast, the opportunity to go to California meant the contract was much loser to enforce anyway. 
- There was lots of private trade even if the records fail to show it, we see ships smuggling furs inot london, catpains thought they coudl trade, and slowly the right to privatae trade was taken from servants, and then slowly from masters.
	- Burley argues private trade was vastly more widespread than the journals show because officers themselves participated or tolerated it — ships' captains treated it as a customary perquisite, post masters allowed favored servants to trade, and the trade in leather, robes, horses, and shoes flourished despite repeated council resolutions. This is part of her broader point that the company's authority was constantly being undermined from within.
- **Apprentices and charity-school boys.** The HBC drew on parish apprentices and pupils from Christ's Hospital (Blue Coat School) and the Grey Coat Hospital — these "hospital boys" were trained in navigation/mathematics and were essentially groomed as junior officers. Burley uses this to push back on Brown's claim that the apprenticeship system created a unified "vertical" company culture.
	- they were not alwyas paid if young enough, and a goal of bringing families over to have more servants was put in place. Indigenous people were hired but considered inferior so not used really and their kids coulndt be controlled in the same way. 
- **Specific case studies that drive the argument.** A few set-pieces recur as touchstones:
	- The Henley House crisis of the 1750s–60s (servants refused to go inland after Indians killed previous occupants; the committee's authority was effectively broken)
	- The 1811 Brandon House mutiny under Hugh Heney (Orcadians refusing to serve under a Canadian master; William Yorston as a leader who'd been quietly insubordinate since 1797)
	- The 1812 "Glasgow Insurgents" at Nelson Encampment (William Finlay's group; Auld blamed Glaswegian republicanism)
	- The 1842 murder of John McLoughlin Jr. at Fort Stikine and Simpson's "justifiable homicide" verdict — Burley uses this to show the committee feared confronting how unrespected its officers were, and Letitia Hargrave's letters worrying that servants would now think they could kill masters
	- The 1838 _Beaver_ steamer mutiny against Capt. McNeill
	- The 1849–50 Fort Rupert coal miners' resistance under John Muir (the Scottish "independent colliers")
	- The Rev. Herbert Beaver scandal at Fort Vancouver in the 1830s, which the company feared because Beaver was gathering material for a book attacking it.
- **10. Smaller themes worth noting:** the role of religion (Sunday work, Methodist missionaries, the Bishop Provencher temperance society); sexuality and gender (Burley reads against the grain of Sylvia Van Kirk / Jennifer Brown's emphasis on stable country marriages — she finds plenty of casual sex, rape, abandonment, and bigamy in the records); and Simpson's increasing absence from Rupert's Land after 1834 (Lachine), which your notes capture correctly.
	- **Payne as a closer foil than Brown.** You'll see this if you go to the published book: Michael Payne's 1989 Carleton dissertation on York Factory and Churchill is Burley's most immediate sparring partner because his data is closest to hers. Payne argues conflict became more "modern" after 1800 (collective, contract-based, wage-focused). Burley disputes this — she finds individual protest and refusal didn't decline, contract-based justification was rare outside the Norwegian case, and wages were never the dominant issue. Worth knowing if you're citing either of them.
- **11. The "Janus face" of merchant capital.** Burley opens Chapter 1 with a theoretical frame borrowed from Elizabeth Fox-Genovese and Eugene Genovese: merchant capital is conservative because it doesn't transform the mode of production, it feeds off existing ones. This is why the HBC retained paternalistic master-servant relations long after British industrialists had abandoned them — and why it deliberately recruited from "marginal" pre-industrial societies whose existing structures it could parasitize without disturbing. It also explains why the HBC, despite being conservative, was simultaneously an early adopter of "systematic management": merchant capital contains "the customs of the past and the seeds of the future." This is the theoretical scaffolding behind the whole book and your notes don't pick it up.
- **12. The archives-of-repression problem.** Burley spends real space in Chapter 2 on methodology — how to read records produced by officers for officers, against the grain, to recover servant perspectives. She uses Raymond Williams on selective tradition and dominant culture. The Hargrave Papers are striking to her precisely because servants are _absent_ from officers' private correspondence: officers and servants lived in different worlds even on paper. This methodological move (E.P. Thompson's "enormous condescension of posterity" applied to HBC men) is important to how she frames her contribution against the existing fur-trade historiography.
- **13. The sample method.** Worth knowing for citing her data: she read every journal, correspondence book, and ship's log for the first outfit of every decade (1770–1, 1780–1 … 1870–1), plus the surrounding year. That's where the Table 1 numbers come from. So spikes like 1820–21's 68 incidents reflect both real disorder (Simpson's Athabasca campaign generated 28 of them on its own) _and_ the energetic record-keeping of a particular officer in a particular outfit. The data shouldn't be read as a clean time series — your notes touch this re: Simpson at Red River, but the broader point is that her numbers under-count and the trend lines are suggestive, not definitive.
- **14. The 1834 Deed Poll.** A piece of the officer story you don't have: in 1834 a new deed poll let the committee dismiss chief factors and chief traders for the first time (previously they could only retire voluntarily), reduced the number of chief factors, and let the committee elect officers without being bound by council nominations. This further centralized power in London and gutted the wintering partners' independence — they were now, as Burley puts it, "only very highly paid and privileged servants." This is part of her case that the post-1821 officer corps was being proletarianized in its own way even as the gulf with common servants widened.
