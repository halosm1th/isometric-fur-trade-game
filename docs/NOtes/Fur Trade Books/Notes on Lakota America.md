# Notes on _Lakota America: A New History of Indigenous Power_ — Pekka Hämäläinen (Yale, Lamar Series in Western History, 2019)

**Book thesis:** The Lakotas were not passive victims swept aside by American expansion but the builders of a shapeshifting Indigenous empire — a "kinetic empire" of mobile power politics — that expanded _alongside and interpenetrating_ the United States for over a century. Two nations were "born" in 1776: the United States in Philadelphia and the Lakota nation in Pahá Sápa (the Black Hills), and they collided a century later at the Little Bighorn. Hämäläinen insists on contingency over teleology: Lakota history is a "series of geneses" — pedestrian foragers → sedentary farmers → equestrian hunters → nomadic pastoralist empire — driven by a genius for reinvention embodied in the trickster **Iktómi**, the shapeshifter. Against the "romance of the open plains," he argues river valleys (above all the Missouri, **Mníšoše**) were the real theater of Lakota power. The book's counterhegemonic archive is the **winter counts** (_waníyetu iyáwapi_), read alongside colonial records.

## Introduction: Dark Matter of History

- Opens with the symmetry of **1776/1876**: the US born in Philadelphia, the Lakota nation (per American Horse's winter count) discovering Pahá Sápa the same year; at Little Bighorn in 1876 Custer's 7th Cavalry (200+ dead with him) was "brought down by the Lakota empire."
- Lakotas as the "dark matter" of American history — enormously influential, rarely seen on their own terms. Never more than ~15,000 people, yet they shaped the continent's interior (Great Lakes to Rockies), which was **not a periphery** but a center.
- Lakota and US expansions **overlapped and coexisted into the 1850s** across a "yawning mental crevasse": each side was certain of its own supremacy because each valued different things (Lakotas sought resources/relationships; Americans sought land as property). Coexistence failed only when nature — the bison — failed both.
- Method: narrative "of twists rather than arc"; winter counts as Indigenous archive; Iktómi as organizing metaphor for serial reinvention.
- Crucially, the story begins with the **Dakotas in the East** (Minnesota woodlands), who held center stage for a century before the Lakotas emerged as the dominant division; Lakotas went west carrying a full century's accumulated knowledge of Europeans.

## Ch. 1 — A Place in the World (17th century)

- Frame story: the **1695 Montreal embassy** — Tiyoskate (Mdewakanton) with the Sauteur (Ojibwe) chief Zhingobiins, brokered by trader **Pierre-Charles Le Sueur**, before Governor Frontenac. Classic Sioux "pity diplomacy": "a bastard searching for a Father," 22 arrows representing his villages; he asked for Onontio's protection **on Sioux terms** (guns and traders, not commands). Tiyoskate sickened and died in Montreal.
- The **Očhéthi Šakówiŋ** (Seven Council Fires): seven oyátes in three dialect divisions — Dakotas (east), Yanktons–Yanktonais (middle), Lakotas/Tetons (west; from _thítȟųŋwaŋ_, "prairie village"). Society built of **thióšpaye** (flexible kin-bands); bilateral kinship maximized ties; kinship was behavioral (_takúye_) — relatives are made, not just born. Population ~30,000, dwarfing neighbors (30x the Sauteurs).
- Homeland **Mní Sóta**: wild-rice riverine world centered on Mde Wakan (Mille Lacs), "the center of the world"; near-ideal diet; ~60,000 sq mi. Deep origins traced to the Siouan dispersal after Cahokia's collapse.
- Isolation from the iron/gun frontier: Crees (armed via Wyandot middlemen) and later the Cree–Sauteur coalition raided them; the exonym "Nadouessioux" (from _na·towe·ssiwak_, "speakers of a foreign tongue") marked them as beyond the pale of the Algonquian alliance world.
- **Epidemiological luck**: post-1633 smallpox devastated the East (80% mortality among New England Indians; Winthrop: God "cleered our title") but never reached 17th-century Sioux country — isolation protected them and their numbers held the line.
- **1660 Feast of the Dead** at Ottawa Lake with Radisson & Des Groseilliers: the Sioux stage-managed a rebirth ceremony (stripping and reclothing the French, weeping over their heads, first gunpowder demonstration); French visited Mde Wakan ("7,000 men"). Chequamegon boom ("Babylon" trade fairs, Saint-Esprit mission) ruptured ~1670 when Wyandots boiled and ate Sioux envoys (the Sinagos affair) → general league against the Sioux; but the Mississippi border held.
- Perrot's failed mediations; Le Sueur's posts (Pelée Island 1685; **Fort L'Huillier** on the Blue Earth, 1700, under d'Iberville's patronage and the Compagnie des Sioux). When the Crown closed the western posts in 1696, Sioux leverage kept Le Sueur's open ("A dead Sciou ties my arms," a Mesquakie chief complained). Chief Oucantapai: "thou must no longer consider us Scioux, but as Frenchmen" — kinship talk, not submission.
- "Tiny islands" thesis: European posts, not armies, made empires in the interior; Indians competed to host them as commitments of largesse.
- Coda "Hommes de Prairie": the Lakotas as westernmost division — beaver-hunting sojourners on the prairies (1,000+ lodges per Le Sueur), no rice or canoes, already fighting Arikaras by the late 1680s; a people "expiring if not expanding."

## Ch. 2 — Facing West (1700–1750s)

- Two technological frontiers converge: the **horse frontier** spreading north from the post-1680 Pueblo Revolt Southwest (horse = "magic dog": 4x a dog's load, twice the distance) and the **gun frontier** pushing west via HBC and liberalized French trade. The Sioux sat where they met — "a place of vast advantage" — and became the **first interior nation to combine horses with substantial firepower**.
- The **Great Peace of 1701 excluded the Sioux**: Onontio adopted the Iroquois and licensed the Lakes nations to war on the Sioux — the alliance needed a common enemy and the Sioux replaced the Iroquois as its necessary menace. Result: the Sioux turned inward and westward — the true beginning of Lakota expansion. Fort L'Huillier abandoned by 1702; "bastard children" again.
- Sicangu winter counts open here: 1703, bison taken under lake ice; **1708, first horses from the Omahas** (_šúŋka wakȟáŋ_, "sacred/mysterious dog").
- The ladder west: **eight river valleys** (Des Moines, Little Sioux, Floyd, Big Sioux, Red, Sheyenne, James, Missouri) — the paradox of the grasslands is immense space pinned to thin ribbons of water and wood. The valleys also kept the politically headless Očhéthi Šakówiŋ stitched together.
- The **1736 Lake of the Woods massacre** retold from the Sioux side: a targeted killing of **Jean-Baptiste de La Vérendrye** (symbol of his father's 1734 betrayal in arming 600+ Crees) along with Father Aulneau and 19 voyageurs; heads wrapped in beaver skins — a declaration of war on the French–Cree alliance. "Lakotas closed the door" on the French continental empire: the Mandan expeditions of 1738/1742 stayed timid and no French fort ever rose in the far West. Harassment of Fort Beauharnois was conducted "with reflection and design" (Sacred Born). The Sauteurs switched sides to the Cree; Governor Beauharnois privately hoped the Sioux would be destroyed.
- 1750s reconciliation under trader **Marin**: Sioux country became the most viable remnant of the middle ground; castor boom; **first mounted Lakota raid recorded 1757–58**.
- Westward wave: Sicangus/Oglalas seized the **Big Bend** niche of the Missouri against the Arikaras; the Saone–Minneconjou wave pushed north with Cheyenne allies to the Grand River. Corn depots at both ends of the domain solved the carbohydrate problem (protein poisoning on lean winter bison) — "then, and only then, the prairies truly belonged to them."
- By midcentury the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ domain ~100,000 sq mi — second-largest Indigenous domain in North America after the Comanche. A near Dakota–Lakota split over Cree peace terms shows the confederation bending but not breaking — Iktómi shapeshifting as survival strategy.

## Ch. 3 — The Imperial Cauldron (1750s–1800)

- Midcentury configuration: Sioux at the center; a refugee world to the south (Omahas, Poncas, Otoes, Iowas pushed to the middle Missouri); a shrinking villager world to the north.
- **1760 fall of Montreal** = supplier crisis just as Oglalas, starving, begged corn from the Arikaras (1760 pity plea; intermarriage; Oglalas farming under Arikara tutelage — remembered later with a shudder). 1763–65 Oglala civil strife: "They who talk alike fought each other." Pontiac's War strangled trade; Dakota bravado at Green Bay 1763 (Gorrell: "greatest nation of Indians ever yet found") concealed weakness.
- Free trade restored 1767; Peter Pond in the Minnesota Valley 1773 (gift-giving as needles left "on the Counter"); **Dakota–Sauteur peace at Michilimackinac** ended a century-long war with the Mississippi as boundary. **St. Louis founded 1764** — French merchant families under nominal Spain became the new southern supplier.
- Late-1760s nadir: "The great mystery was crazy" (1770–71); warriors defecating beside their lodges in fear. Then the turn: **1771 Oglala–Yanktonai burning of a Mandan village** near Apple Creek opens the Lakota ascendancy; 1770s drought subsistence (drowned buffalo cut from river ice) and equestrian takeoff (horses and horse-envy killings fill the counts).
- **"1776" section**: American Horse's winter count (recited to Corbusier, 1879) makes the discovery of **Pahá Sápa** the _first entry_ — the beginning of history — deliberately paralleling the US founding.
- Revolutionary War: **Wabasha's** British alliance — 1776 state dinner with Governor Carleton in Quebec, an eight-gun salute aboard HMS _Isis_, a general's commission; the failed 1780 British–Indian attack on Spanish St. Louis; guns kept flowing; British traders married into Sioux families.
- **Smallpox 1779–82** (from Mexico City via Comanche trade networks): Arikaras lost ~3/4 (32 villages → 7); Mandans 8 villages → 2 (13 clans → 7); Hidatsas halved; the combined villager population fell from tens of thousands to ~8,000. Cheyennes abandoned the Missouri for full nomadism (only the Masikotas stayed, then were chased toward the Black Hills). Lakotas, mobile and dispersed, lost far less — "were not very dangerous" before the pox, a Mandan elder recalled — and pushed into the vacuum. **Lakota women pitching tipis on the west bank = the first footholds in the "American West."**
- 1780s–90s multifront raiding (the Skinned Penis episode and revenge cycle; the 1787–88 "killed all" attack on a Mandan village; winters so cold crows froze dead in the air). By ~1790 Arikaras huddled in two villages near the Cheyenne River — "captains without companies," ~500 warriors.
- Early 1790s peaces: the **midstream handshake** with the Mandans; the seizure of a 200-mile vacant stretch of the Mníšoše — the **"commonwealth of cottonwood," the single most important expansion in Očhéthi Šakówiŋ history**. The Sun Dance is first observed here. Arikaras re-enter downriver as ambiguous corn depots — "neither allies nor exactly enemies."
- Spain blocked upriver: d'Eglise tolled (1793); **Truteau stopped by the "infernal old man"** (1794); Spain then granted Lakotas privileged trade status as a northern buffer — the same gamble it made with American settlers (Boone, Austin). A British 1796 scheme to send mounted Sioux down the Mississippi came to nothing.
- The pan-Sioux rendezvous moved to the **James River** (1,000–1,200 lodges, 3,000 armed men): horses, lodges, and robes moving east for guns and kettles moving west; Dakotas fed Lakotas price intelligence. The seven fires enjoyed a pooled, 360-degree panorama of empires — which were most useful when "fit to operate but too frail to dictate."

## Ch. 4 — The Lakota Meridian (1800–1830s)

- Geopolitical frame: San Ildefonso (1800), the Louisiana Purchase (1803, $15M, 530M acres at 3¢/acre). "Louisiana" as marketable real estate was **an imperial fiction — Indians controlled almost all of it, the largest portion the Sioux.**
- 1801–02 smallpox again ("Smallpox used them up again") — commerce as disease corridor.
- **Régis Loisel's Fort Aux Cèdres** on Cedar Island: first fixed post in Lakota country. Sicangu blockade politics: chiefs Black Buffalo, the Partisan, Medicine Bull, Manzomani overruled by the "old soldier" **Mato-kokipabi** (the same "infernal old man" of 1795) — motives: middleman profits, revenge on the Saones, and Loisel's refusal of his daughter (a refused offer of kinship).
- **Tabeau among the Arikaras**: the tributary system laid bare. Lakotas treated Arikaras as "a certain kind of serf, who cultivates for them and... takes, for them, the place of women"; barrier camps of tipis blocked bison from the villages; Lakotas fixed prices, sold even bows and arrows to people surrounded by good bow-wood, and simultaneously painted the St. Louis traders as the real exploiters — market and perception manipulation at once.
- **September 1804, Bad River: the Lewis & Clark standoff.** Bowline seizures, thrown tobacco carrots, 200 warriors on the bank, Clark sleepless. Black Buffalo repeatedly overrode the Partisan and de-escalated. The captains failed to grasp that the Sicangus were offering integration into their trade emporium as suppliers — they saw only "predators." Two Lakota agents sat inside the subsequent Arikara councils; Arikara chiefs (Kahawissassa, Pocasse, Piaheto) gambled on an American alliance to escape "the slavery of the Sioux." Fort Mandan winter followed; Lakotas consolidated their grip **in the expedition's wake** (the Oglala encirclement watched by Arikaras with "an eye of sorrow").
- Clark's verdict — Lakotas are "the vilest miscreants of the savage race... the pirates of the Missouri" (he spelled "Sioux" 27 different ways) — and his master map as **cartographic dispossession**: Native nations simply left off, the West rendered empty.
- Aftermath 1806–09: Arikaras seized Gravelines's boat; Manuel Lisa tolled half his goods; the **Pryor expedition defeated** (4 dead; the Mandan chief Sheheke only got home in 1809 via Chouteau, who was told plainly the Arikaras "belonged to Lakotas"; Governor Meriwether Lewis ordered the _Arikaras_ "exterpated" — still not comprehending who ruled the river). Zebulon Pike's 250-word Mdewakanton treaty (1805) was never proclaimed.
- **The Lakota river policy**: mobility, visibility, ubiquity; a 400-mile policed stretch; threats replacing violence; a new leadership type capable of "imperial posturing." Astor's 1811 Pacific expedition paid its toll; agent Boilvin: the Sioux "command the balance of power."
- War of 1812: "All the rivers are to be shut" — Sioux weight thrown largely with Britain in the East while the Missouri stayed a Lakota tollway.
- **1823 Arikara War**: Arikaras attacked Ashley's trappers (enter Hugh Glass); Colonel Leavenworth's punitive expedition brought 700 Sioux allies but fizzled into shelling, parleys, and an empty village — the torn-up cotton bandages entered Lakota lore as a symbol of American military timidity; "abundance of corn" was the Oglalas' only record of it. 1825 treaties arrived by eight wheel boats.
- American Fur Company monopoly by 1827; **Fort Tecumseh rebuilt as Fort Pierre (1831–32) on the west bank, built _for_ the Lakotas** — Maximilian's "supermarket of the steppe" ($80K in stores), an American outpost sitting _inside_ a seasonal Lakota village ("like a little village"; Dorion Jr.'s tipi with scalps fluttering). The steamboat _Yellow Stone_ welcomed by 600–700 lodges (1832).
- Villager endgame: 200 Hidatsas killed in 1828–29; **Arikaras burned out for good in 1832** and driven to the Loup; the 1831–32 smallpox halved the Pawnees, after which a Lakota war party attacked 6,000 survivors who had seven guns among them; Norway rats (via fur-trade boats) devoured villager granaries by the hundreds of pounds of corn a day.
- **The 1832 US vaccination act**: physicians vaccinated most Yanktons/Yanktonais and ~900 Lakotas — and did not even try to reach the Mandans and Hidatsas. "Sioux exceptionalism" bloomed in the American mind (an 1824 army estimate of 60,000 Sioux was perhaps 3x too high — revealingly so).

## Ch. 5 — The Call of the White Buffalo Calf Woman (1830s–40s)

- Opens with **Ptesáŋwiŋ, the White Buffalo Calf Woman**: the gift of the sacred pipe ("With visible breath I am walking"), the red-stone bowl as earth and buffalo, the sacred hoop — tied to the era when Lakotas made their second concerted push west, now from the Missouri, centered on **Pahá Sápa**.
- The Black Hills as both spiritual center ("the heart of everything that is") and material refuge: a high-elevation microclimate catching more rain, holding more pasture and bison — **a sanctuary and a "meat pack"** in drought. Hand paintings of slain enemies mark the violent takeover; access came through the tightening Cheyenne–Arapaho alliance; by the late 1820s Lakotas wintered in Pahá Sápa — "The Black Hills belonged to them."
- **Ways to the West**: eight western tributaries (White, Bad, Cheyenne, Moreau, Grand, Cannonball, Heart, Knife) as timbered avenues; Bull Bear's Oglala campaigns drove the Crows to the Powder River country and the Kiowas south. The mature **tripartite geography** — Missouri trunk line, tributary arterials, Black Hills anchor — defined the Lakota homeland from the 1830s.
- **The "new hybrid"** of bison hunting + horse pastoralism: wild-horse hauls of 40–60 south of the North Platte fault line completed the equestrian takeoff in a few years. Horses as grass-energy converters (gut bacteria digesting cellulose; plains plant biomass ~1,000x animal biomass). The catch: an ~80% horse–bison dietary overlap made the economy structurally self-competing, and horses demanded heavy labor (20 lb grass + 10 gal water per day; a thióšpaye with 500 horses exhausted 3–4 acres daily → constant moves).
- Society and hierarchy: hereditary advantage and _wiča_ ("complete men"); the privileged boyhoods of **Sitting Bull** (uncles Four Horns and Looks-For-Him-in-A-Tent; first war honors at 14; Strong Hearts leader by his mid-20s) and **Crazy Horse/Curly Hair** (b. 1840 into the Hunkpatila elite, Minneconjou mother, mentored by High Backbone) versus **Red Cloud**, who lacked pedigree and spent ~20 years raiding before bidding for leadership.
- "Lords of the Plains": the last villager offensive crushed in 1834–35 (22 Pawnee/Arikara dead, body parts paraded); the **1837 smallpox aboard the unquarantined steamer _St. Peter's_** killed 20,000+ Plains people (Pawnees lost 2,500) while the vaccinated Lakotas were largely spared — "beneficiaries of the catastrophe." June 1843: ~500 Oglalas/Sicangus destroyed the grand Pawnee village at Dunbar's Loup mission (70 dead, 200 horses, half the lodges burned) and recovered the stolen **Cheyenne sacred medicine arrow**, redeemed by the Cheyennes for 100 horses; the village was burned again in 1846, cholera followed in 1849. Omahas lost 73 people in one 1846–47 attack (>6% of the nation) and begged the US to buy their land; Otoes spiraled into whiskey. Driver: the wet cycle of the mid-1820s–mid-1840s ended in severe drought from 1845–46, intensifying raiding.
- Robe-trade boom and its strains: expanded war zone (Crows, Shoshones, Utes, Flatheads, Arapahos — a 500-mile raiding front, "the largest active war zone" on the continent); whiskey as the traders' lever. **1841: Bull Bear (Kiyuksa) killed in a drunken brawl with Smoke's Itéšiča band — reputedly shot by Smoke's 20-year-old nephew Red Cloud** → the enduring Kiyuksa–Itéšiča (Bear/Smoke) split, which proved strategically useful as Oglalas became a frontier oyáte ranging toward the Platte and Powder.
- Political architecture (the chapter's core): tribal councils gained weight; **shirt wearers** (_wičháša yatáŋpikA_, "praiseworthy men," owned by and owners of the tribe) executed council decisions; **akíčhita** marshals and _wakíčuŋzA_ deciders ran camp moves and hunts with coercive power that was **rotated and temporary** — power "shaped into a reflection of themselves: mobile and supple." Men's societies (e.g., Red Cloud's White Owners) counterbalanced councils; generational friction (elders' restraint vs. young men's war-driven advancement) was absorbed rather than suppressed — the same friction that later made Crazy Horse's fall (the Black Buffalo Woman affair) so precipitous.
- Chapter verdict: a **"kinetic empire"** — density under 1 person per 10 sq mi, ruling by connecting and exploiting nodes (valleys, hunting grounds, corn villages, posts, Pahá Sápa), "ranging widely but ruling lightly," controlling resources without controlling people; between the White Buffalo Calf Woman's vision of order and Iktómi's pragmatism lay the imaginative space of Lakota power.

## Ch. 6 — Empires (1840s–1850s)

- US imperial machinery assembled: Calhoun creates the **Bureau of Indian Affairs (1824)** without Congress; the cotton gin, slave capitalism, and **Indian Removal** (~100,000 removed; a "crash course in empire building") train the American imperial mindset; Marshall's "domestic dependent nations"; O'Sullivan's "manifest destiny" (1845); the Mexican War and Guadalupe Hidalgo (half of Mexico taken; cabinet debates over taking it all). The plains, deemed worthless, become a "permanent Indian frontier" — a halfway house the US did not yet need. Senator Benton: only "the white race" received the command to "subdue and replenish the earth."
- Oregon/California overland migration turns the Platte into a national artery; Lakotas slip between the US and its Pacific wealth just as the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ once sat between France and Asia. Fort Laramie bought and militarized (1849); cholera rides the trails.
- **The 1851 Horse Creek Treaty ("Big Issue Winter")**: ~10,000 Indians; Lakotas arrive first, staged as the "grand old Sioux Nation" under Clark's old flag; Fitzpatrick and Mitchell distribute uniforms, medals, and made-chiefs; De Smet baptizes hundreds; $50,000/year in annuities for 50 years promised for overland damage; tribal territories mapped (the federal fantasy of three plains reservation blocks). **Clear Blue Earth refuses a single head chief**: "We can't make one Chief" — diffuse leadership as anti-colonial buffer. Mato Oyuhi (Conquering Bear) made nominal head chief. Each empire left claiming mastery of the same ground — "the two regimes coexisted on separate mental planes" (Lakotas sought resources; Americans coveted land itself).
- Accommodation infrastructure: Bordeaux's and Bissonette's posts, Lakota wives, the **Wágluȟe ("Laramie Loafers")** band as a permanent diplomatic-commercial hinge; Smoke's people gardening at the fort. Lakota–Crow rapprochement after Lone Horn spared the lost Crow party ("was not killed," 1852–53) → a **joint-use neutral zone** between the Black Hills and Powder River under _wólakȟota_.
- Rupture: Congress unilaterally cut the annuity term from 50 years to 10 — Lakotas learned they were seen as wards, not allies. **The Grattan fight (August 1854)**: a Mormon's lame cow, High Forehead's refusal, the drunken interpreter, Grattan's two cannons trained on the village of 600 tipis; **Mato Oyuhi shot down**; Grattan and all 29 soldiers killed (24 arrows in Grattan). Mutilations answered the betrayal; ten winter counts recorded Conquering Bear's death. Then the "Plenty of Money Winter" — Spotted Tail's mail-coach ambush ($10,000 in gold).
- **Harney and Blue Water Creek (September 3, 1855)**: Fort Pierre bought as a base; Twiss's hostile/friendly line at the North Platte; Harney ("By God, I'm for battle — no peace") talked with Little Thunder under an umbrella flag of truce while his dragoons circled the village, then killed 86 (many women and children) and took 70 captives — the worst Sioux defeat in over a century. Spotted Tail, Red Leaf, and Long Chin surrendered at Fort Laramie **singing their death songs**; Harney ("Putinska," White Beard — also "The Butcher," "Woman Killer") dictated the 1856 Fort Pierre terms. Bear Ribs's moral challenge: "To fight me you are able. After you have whipped me you will make my heart glad."
- Aftermath: Lakotas withdrew from the wašíču world rather than submitting — the wašíčus nearly vanish from winter counts; agents (Twiss, Vaughan, Redfield) filed soothing reports of "peace and quiet" that Lakotas themselves substantially scripted; an "empire in denial" faced an empire of equals. The Lakota realm absorbed Cheyennes and Arapahos through kinship ("They are all one flesh") — an empire whose adhesive was _wólakȟota_, loyalty over likeness, with literate mixed-blood traders (Richard, Janis, Ecoffey) as prized kin who could read wašíču documents.
- Closing omens: Raynolds's 1859 Yellowstone expedition — Bear Ribs: "To whom does this land belong? I believe it belongs to me... We are nine nations" — skirted Pahá Sápa under Lakota surveillance and **detected traces of gold**; Twiss warned the West was "reposing upon a volcano."

## Ch. 7 — War (1860s)

- Prologue on **words**: _wólakȟota_ (kinship-peace), _škáŋ_ (the animating energy of movement) — Lakota power had always been built on making the wašíčus listen. Twiss's extraordinary 1859 report pleaded the Lakota case (the dying herds; the chief's "grand tour" speech mapping a century of Sioux geopolitics) even while conceding to "Anglo-Saxon progress."
- Northern radicalization: the Hunkpapa attack on Fort Union (1860); **Bear Ribs assassinated (June 1862)** by Sans Arcs for taking annuities — the friendly/hostile line enforced from the Lakota side.
- **The Dakota War of 1862 in Minnesota**: starvation, Myrick's "let them eat grass" (found dead with grass stuffed in his mouth), Little Crow's reluctant leadership, hundreds of settlers killed, 23 counties evacuated. Pope sent to "utterly exterminate the Sioux." Military tribunals sentenced 303; Lincoln commuted most; **38 Dakota men hanged at Mankato, December 26, 1862 — still the largest mass execution in US history**. Treaties annulled; Dakotas scattered to Canada and west to Lakota country; Little Crow killed in 1863, his remains displayed by the Minnesota Historical Society into the 20th century.
- Hämäläinen's reframe: the "**First Great Sioux War, 1862–68**" — Minnesota to Montana — eclipsed in memory by the Civil War, which was itself a continent-wide crisis of authority; the Union became a "Yankee Leviathan" intolerant of competing sovereignties, the gravest of which was Lakota.
- **Sibley–Sully campaigns (1863–64)**: the botched pincer; the Missouri-crossing massacre of swimming families; **Whitestone Hill** (Sully's charge into a talking camp; ~200 killed, tons of winter meat burned); **Killdeer Mountain (July 1864)** — Sully's hollow square versus 1,600 warriors under Sitting Bull, Four Horns, Gall, and Inkpáduta; Man Who Never Walked's death-song charge on a travois; 1,600 lodges burned; the Badlands harried Sully's retreat ("hell with the fires put out"). For Hunkpapas this was the "**first fight with white men**" — an unnecessary, self-inflicted war that radicalized a generation (including the middle-aged Red Cloud).
- **Sand Creek (November 1864)**: Chivington's butchery of Black Kettle's Cheyennes; frozen survivors placing their hands on Lakota chiefs' heads, asking to be pitied — Lakotas "took in their pain," and the allies burned Julesburg and the Platte road in reply (the grass-burning counter-campaign; mail rerouted via Panama).
- 1865: Connor's Powder River expedition (kill every male over twelve) defeated; the 1865 treaties and the **hostile/friendly taxonomy** as a colonial sorting tool Lakotas learned to exploit.
- **Red Cloud's War (1866–68)**: Carrington's column arriving mid-negotiation ("the White Chief goes with soldiers to steal the road before the Indians say Yes or No"); Forts Reno, Phil Kearny, C.F. Smith; the 1866 Sun Dance war council; They Fear Even His Horses backing Red Cloud, who became _blotáhuŋka átaya_ (supreme war chief). **The Fetterman fight (December 21, 1866)**: High Backbone's plan, the wíŋkte's four rides prophesying "a hundred or more" soldiers, Crazy Horse's decoy ride, ~1,500 warriors annihilating all 81 (Fetterman and Brown apparently shooting each other); "They killed 100 white men at Fort Phil Kearney." Wagon Box and Hayfield fights (1867) taught respect for breechloaders; the forts were besieged into uselessness.
- **The 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie**: the forts abandoned and burned by the warriors; De Smet's mission to Sitting Bull (the Virgin Mary banner; Sitting Bull's confession of the "weight of white men's blood"; Four Horns and Black Moon's grievances; Gall signing at Fort Rice); **Red Cloud waiting until November**, washing his hands with floor-dust before signing, making every American "touch the pen." Terms: the **Great Sioux Reservation** (all of today's western South Dakota incl. the Black Hills, ~48,000 sq mi, for "absolute and undisturbed use"); **Article 16** unceded territory (Powder River country; northern boundary left unfixed — a door open toward the Yellowstone and Canada); **Article 11** hunting rights south to the Republican "so long as the buffalo may range thereon in such numbers as to justify the chase" — sovereignty put on a bison-timer nobody read aloud. A small nation had defeated an industrial behemoth; the US "seemed to have embraced the Lakotas as equals." Sitting Bull answered with a raid on Fort Buford's cattle: the north would accept neither treaties nor reservations.

## Ch. 8 — Shapeshifters (1868–1873)

- The modern arrives: railroads as "the sole solution of the Indian question" (Senator Stewart); Sheridan and **Custer's Washita massacre (November 1868)**; Grant's **Peace Policy** (Ely Parker, Quakers) — imperialism gone managerial: appease, feed, and mold until resistance became impossible.
- The Powder River world at its height: thousand-lodge villages moving leisurely among the Yellowstone tributaries, awash in trade goods (Remingtons, Colts, Navajo blankets, Mexican cornmeal); Red Cloud's trading protocol; **northern Lakota population growing toward 15,000+** while nearly every other Native nation shrank.
- **Red Cloud in Washington (1870)**: the stage-managed tour (Senate gallery, Arsenal, Navy Yard — women covering their ears at the cannon demonstration); Spotted Tail's acid wit ("If you had had as much trouble in your life as I have had in mine, you would have cut your throat long ago"); Red Cloud sitting on the floor, rejecting the _printed_ 1868 treaty when its unread clauses were finally voiced — "I never heard of it and do not mean to follow it"; Red Shirt talking of suicide over the swindle. Spoken words _were_ the treaty. Red Cloud's Cooper Union speech in New York made him a national sensation; the agency he extracted (outside the reservation, near Fort Laramie) became the **Red Cloud Agency**, and his delay-and-vanish diplomacy ("The earth will not move away... there need be no hurry") kept the unceded lands unceded for years.
- **Sitting Bull's northern strategy**: shun the agencies, monopolize the remaining herds. The Fort Peck/Milk River takeover ("they have come to stay" — women arriving signaled occupation); the Métis munitions trade (post-Riel diaspora as gun pipeline); overtures to the HBC at Fort Qu'Appelle; alliance with old Assiniboine enemies — a de facto Sioux domain reaching toward Lake Winnipeg. Rations accepted at Fort Peck not as submission but as imperial consolidation.
- **The Yellowstone confrontations**: Northern Pacific surveys (1872: Pryor Creek, Crazy Horse's white-buckskin taunting run and Sitting Bull's demonstration; 1873: Custer's Seventh on the Yellowstone, the failed baiting, the sharpshooter duel) — then the **Panic of 1873** killed the Northern Pacific (Jay Cooke's collapse; grass growing over the dead railhead at Bismarck). Lakotas appeared, plausibly, to have stopped US expansion cold.
- That same year, in the Little Bighorn Valley, a sickly boy — **Black Elk** — received his Great Vision of the six grandfathers and the dying/flowering nation.
- Consolidation by violence: **Massacre Canyon (August 1873)** — 1,000+ Oglalas/Sicangus killed 70+ Pawnees on the Republican; the Pawnees abandoned Nebraska for Indian Territory (1874–75), followed by the Poncas (1876) and Otoe-Missourias; the Republican herds validated Article 11 claims. Crows, Shoshones, and Utes pressed back to reservations; "the annual Sioux invasion" a fact of life; Blackfoot's taunt: "The Sioux are on the way and you are afraid of them; they will turn the whirlwind back."
- Chapter closes Christmas Day 1873, Red Cloud instructing agent Saville: "All we want is the guns and ammunition, and we want you to get them for us." At their apex, the Lakotas ranged from Lake Winnipeg to the Republican and from the Missouri to the Continental Divide.

## Ch. 9 — Upside-Down Soldiers (1874–1890)

- Winter counts of the early 1870s remain overwhelmingly **Indigenous-centered** (Crow wars, High Backbone's death, ceremonies) — the US was a problem, not yet the world.
- **Custer's 1874 Black Hills Expedition** (1,000+ men, scientists, and reporters) confirmed gold on French Creek: "the thieves' road." The army half-heartedly expelled miners; **Red Cloud: "I am no Dog. I am a man. This is my ground, and I am sitting on it."** The 1875 Washington summit tried to buy the Hills and the unceded lands (Grant offering $25,000; Delano's ration blackmail); the **Allison Commission (September 1875)** collapsed before thousands of Lakotas — Little Big Man threatening to shoot the first chief who signed; Spotted Bear: "Our Great Father has a big safe, and so have we. This hill is our safe" (demands of $70M / support "for seven generations" as de facto refusal).
- The manufactured war: Watkins's November 1875 report; the **January 31, 1876 ultimatum** (report to agencies or be deemed hostile — delivered in impossible winter conditions); the Indian Office handed the problem to Sheridan. Reynolds's March attack on the wrong (Oglala–Cheyenne) village on the Powder squandered surprise; Sitting Bull organized the great sharing of goods for the refugees.
- **Mobilization as kinship**: Sitting Bull's runners; the swelling village (Cheyennes leading, Hunkpapas guarding the rear); agency kin streaming out (Red Cloud Agency nearly emptied of fighting men — Sheridan's own wires recorded it) while officials, blinded by "clannish" stereotypes and ration-inflated agency counts, missed a national mobilization happening in plain sight. Sitting Bull's June **Sun Dance** (50 pieces of flesh from each arm; the vision of soldiers "falling into camp upside down, their hats falling off" — "these have no ears... they are to die, but you are not supposed to take their spoils").
- **The Rosebud (June 17, 1876)**: Crazy Horse's 750-warrior preemptive strike fought Crook's 1,000+ (with Crow/Shoshone auxiliaries) to a standstill through fluid charge-regroup tactics; Crook "declared victory" and retreated, gutting Sheridan's three-pronged plan.
- **The Greasy Grass / Little Bighorn (June 25–26, 1876)**: the thousand-lodge village (6–8,000 people, ~2,000 warriors, ~20,000 horses); Reno's charge broken at the river ("shooting at the sky"; the water turned red); Bloody Knife and Isaiah Dorman among the dead; then Custer's five companies flanked — Gall (his two wives and three children killed early in the fight) axing through with "controlled rage," Crazy Horse's mile-long sweep into the rear, Cheyenne suicide-vow warriors, the final huddle behind horse barricades: **all ~210 with Custer killed**, none alive "for even five minutes." Pȟehíŋ HáŋskA (Long Hair) sent to the next world nearly whole — a gash and an arrow, but unscalped. Victory songs: "A charger, he is coming... When he came, I wiped him out. He did not like my ways; that is why." Lewis Henry Morgan in _The Nation_: Custer's men "experienced the precise fate they intended for the Indians."
- The erasure begins immediately: Secretary Cameron's "pushed westward for centuries" fiction — the deliberate rewriting of Lakotas into primitive victims at the very moment they had beaten the empire; Custer canonized ("a Golgotha").
- **The reckoning**: the Manypenny Commission's "sell or starve" agreement (September 1876) collected **230 signatures — ~10% of adult men, where the 1868 treaty required three-fourths**; Congress ratified anyway (February 1877): the Black Hills and 7.3M acres taken. Agencies militarized; horses and guns confiscated; Crook and Miles's winter attrition (Slim Buttes, Dull Knife's Cheyenne village destroyed); starving bands came in through early 1877. **Crazy Horse surrendered in May with 1,200 people and was bayoneted to death at Fort Robinson (September 5, 1877)** when he balked at the jail. Sitting Bull crossed the "medicine line" to Canada (1877–81), returning to surrender at Fort Buford — his rifle handed over by his young son: "I wish it to be remembered that I was the last man of my tribe to surrender my rifle."
- Reservation colonialism: McLaughlin's boss farmers, Indian police, and courts; Spotted Tail killed by Crow Dog (1881); the **last buffalo hunts (1882–83)**; Sitting Bull with Buffalo Bill (1885); Red Cloud outmaneuvering McGillycuddy via Cleveland; the **Dawes Act (1887)** and the **1889 Sioux Agreement** (Crook's serial signature-gathering) carving the Great Sioux Reservation into five islands and taking 11M more acres — followed instantly by a 20–25% ration cut, crop failure, and epidemics.
- **The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee**: Wovoka's message poured into the hole left by the banned Sun Dance; ~4,000 Lakota dancers by fall 1890; agent Royer's panicked wires; a third of the US Army deployed; **Sitting Bull shot dead by Indian police at his cabin (December 15, 1890)**; Spotted Elk (Big Foot), pneumonia-stricken and seeking refuge, intercepted; **December 29, 1890: Hotchkiss guns above Wounded Knee Creek — at least 270 Lakotas killed, at least 170 of them women and children**; twenty Medals of Honor awarded; frozen bodies thrown into a mass grave at $2 a corpse. The chapter's framing symmetry: the massacre "flowed from" the Little Bighorn and, to some, "demanded" it. Three years later Turner declared the frontier won by ax and plow — writing Wounded Knee out of history even as it happened. "Yet Lakotas would survive as a people."

## Epilogue: The Lakota Struggle for Indigenous Sovereignty (1890s–2018)

- Winter counts fall silent or turn to suicides and hangings — "colonialism working exactly as intended." Allotment fragments the five reservations (Bennett County carved from Pine Ridge, 1909 — the year Red Cloud died: "I must beg for that which I own").
- Mount Rushmore blasted into the **Six Grandfathers** from 1927 (Red Cloud, Sacagawea, and Crazy Horse dropped from early design talk). Citizenship (1924) without rights. Survival strategy: move the Sun Dance and Ghost Dance out of sight; keep the thióšpaye alive inside the cash economy — "Indigenous modernity."
- The **Indian Reorganization Act (1934)**: Collier's well-meant, paternalistic design fit Lakota political culture badly ("In every critical crisis point in history there has always been the rise of the man of the hour"); only Pine Ridge and Rosebud adopted it, deepening progressive/traditionalist, mixed-blood/full-blood splits.
- **Pick–Sloan dams (1940s–50s)**: five Missouri dams drowned the bottomlands — 550 sq mi of Sioux land, 6% of the land base, a third of the people displaced (Lake Oahe swallowing Standing Rock's best land) — despite mass Lakota service in WWII.
- **Red Power**: Vine Deloria Jr.'s _Custer Died for Your Sins_ (1969); Alcatraz; Mount Rushmore protests; Raymond Yellow Thunder; the Trail of Broken Treaties and the BIA occupation (1972); then **Wounded Knee II (1973)** — AIM and Oglala traditionalists versus Dick Wilson's GOON regime and the federal siege: 71 days, the Independent Oglala Nation proclaimed under the 1868 treaty, the Ghost Dance danced openly after 83 years, Buddy Lamont killed. Aftermath of persecution — but the sovereignty movement was reborn (Self-Determination Act, 1975).
- **The Black Hills claim**: Court of Claims (1979) and **US Supreme Court, _United States v. Sioux Nation_ (1980)** — the 1877 taking violated the 1868 treaty and the Fifth Amendment; $122M awarded. **Lakotas refuse the money** (now >$1.3B compounding): taking it would legalize the theft. "It's always been ours. It will always be ours."
- Ongoing sovereignty work: tribal courts and colleges, language revitalization, indigenized governance; Whiteclay's "liquid genocide" litigated shut; Clinton at Pine Ridge (1999), Obama at Standing Rock (2014, "nation to nation"); the UN's 2012 recommendation to return the Hills.
- **Standing Rock / #NoDAPL (2016–17)**: the Dakota Access Pipeline rerouted away from Bismarck's water and under the Mníšoše half a mile from the reservation ("Indians matter less"); the Oceti Sakowin camp of up to 10,000 water protectors, women leaders (LaDonna Brave Bull Allard, Phyllis Young, Joye Braun); Obama's pause, Trump's reversal, the leaks that followed.
- Ends at the **150th anniversary of the 1868 Fort Laramie Treaty (2018)** — a living document of Indigenous sovereignty. Final note: Lakotas endure because they are Iktómi's people, "adapting to the world's shape while remaking it, again and again, after their own image."

## Cross-Cutting Themes

- **The kinetic empire.** Lakota power was built on mobility, kinship (_wólakȟota_), and the control of nodes rather than territory or subjects — capitals, bureaucracies, and standing armies replaced by motion itself (_škáŋ_). Power was deliberately kept temporary and rotating (akíčhita, wakíčuŋzA, blotáhuŋka), which frustrated every imperial attempt to find "the" chief to co-opt — from Onontio to Mitchell at Horse Creek to Wham at the Red Cloud Agency.
- **Rivers, not open plains.** Every phase of expansion followed timbered valleys: the eight rivers to the Missouri, then the eight western tributaries to Pahá Sápa. The tripartite geography (trunk, arterials, anchor) is the book's spatial signature.
- **Disease as geopolitical actor.** The Sioux were shielded in the 17th century by isolation, devastated less than their sedentary rivals in 1779–82 and 1801–02 by dispersal, and _vaccinated_ in 1832 while the Mandans and Hidatsas were not, then spared again in 1837. Every epidemic ratcheted Lakota relative power upward — "beneficiaries of the catastrophe."
- **Words, paper, and power.** Oral diplomacy versus the printed word runs from the 1695 Montreal council to the unread clauses of 1868 ("I never heard of it and do not mean to follow it"), the 230-signature "agreement" of 1876, and the treaty's resurrection in 1973 and 1980. The US won by writing; the Lakotas endured by remembering — winter counts against filing cabinets.
- **Empire in denial vs. empire of equals.** The US refused to see itself as an empire while behaving as one; the Lakota empire absorbed diversity through kinship without hierarchy ("loyalty, not likeness"). Both claimed the same ground on "separate mental planes" until the bison collapse forced the collision.
- **Link to Ray, _Indians in the Fur Trade_:** Ray's Cree–Assiniboine middlemen are the Lakotas' northern mirror image and old enemies — the gun frontier that isolated the 17th-century Sioux was the HBC system Ray maps; the 1780–82 smallpox that Ray shows shattering Assiniboine power is the same epidemic that opened the Missouri to the Lakotas; Todd's 1837–38 HBC vaccination saving the northern Cree parallels the 1832 US vaccination of the Lakotas (and the abandonment of the Mandans/Hidatsas in both stories); Ray's account of the American Fur Company's post-1820s Missouri ascendancy (Fort Union, steamboats) supplies the supply-side of Hämäläinen's Fort Pierre "supermarket of the steppe."
- **Link to Berthelette, _Heirs of an Ambivalent Empire_:** the same Franco-Sioux story from opposite banks — Tiyoskate's 1695 embassy, the Compagnie des Sioux, Fort Beauharnois, and above all the **1736 Lake of the Woods massacre** (Berthelette's largest-ever voyageur loss = Hämäläinen's Lakota declaration of war on the French–Cree alliance); the coureurs de bois who fought _with_ the Dakotas against the Nakoda in the 1710s–20s are the "backcountry specialists" whose métissage Berthelette traces; and both books hinge on the 1780–82 smallpox as the demographic pivot — in Berthelette it clears the ground for Métis ethnogenesis on the Saskatchewan, in Hämäläinen for Lakota ethnogenesis as a plains empire on the Missouri.
- **Historiographic stance.** Against Turnerian erasure and victimization narratives alike: Lakotas as history-makers whose choices (not just US policy) explain the shape of the American West — and whose refusal of the Black Hills money keeps that history legally and morally unfinished.
