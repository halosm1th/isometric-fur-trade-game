# Chapter-by-Chapter Notes

**Book:** Arthur J. Ray, _Indians in the Fur Trade: their role as trappers, hunters and middlemen in the lands southwest of Hudson Bay, 1660–1870_ (University of Toronto Press, 1974).

**Big picture:** Ray is a historical geographer working mainly from Hudson's Bay Company archives (post journals, account books, district reports). His central argument is that the Cree, Assiniboine, and later Ojibwa were not passive victims of the fur trade but active economic agents who repeatedly re-specialized (trappers → middlemen → provisioners) as trade conditions shifted, until the destruction of the resource base (furs, then game, then bison) finally removed all their options and forced the treaty/reserve outcome of the 1870s.

## Ch. 1 — Trade rivalries, inter-tribal warfare, and migration

- Sets up who the Assiniboine and Western Cree were and where they came from. Archaeology: three proto-historic cultural complexes in northern Minnesota/Manitoba/Ontario — Blackduck and Manitoba foci (tentatively = proto-Assiniboine, disputed) and Selkirk focus (generally = Cree). Blackduck sites densest and oldest (~1200 AD) between NW Lake Superior and the lower Red/Winnipeg River area. From ~1400 Selkirk material starts replacing Blackduck in the boreal/mixed forest — i.e., a westward population push out of the Shield began _before_ European contact.
- The Assiniboine are a Siouan-speaking offshoot of the Yankton Sioux. The split's date and cause are murky: they're already named as a distinct group in the Jesuit Relations of 1640, but Indigenous oral tradition (recorded by David Thompson, de Beaurain, etc.) blamed the rupture on the founding of the HBC (1670) — the story being that English guns made the Cree formidable, the Assiniboine sued for peace with the Cree, and thereby became enemies of the rest of the Sioux. Ray thinks the alliance with the Cree probably solidified as hostilities intensified after 1670, even if the Assiniboine identity was older.
- Late-1600s locations: Assiniboine territory ran from the Rainy Lake / Lake of the Woods boundary-waters area northwest to central Saskatchewan (Kelsey found them along the Carrot River to the Touchwood Hills, 1690–91); they were regular visitors to Lake Nipigon/Lake Superior and traded at York Factory after 1670 (400+ came in 1684). The Cree bordered them to the north and east — and crucially the two groups coexisted peacefully.
- Before 1670 both groups fed furs into the French–Ottawa trading network (pulling them eastward, e.g., to du Lhut's Lake Nipigon post of 1684). After the HBC established bayside posts, the pull reversed: their occupation of the lower Nelson basin gave them a chokehold on virtually every canoe route converging on York Factory — any inland group had to pass through their territory.
- Guns are the key variable: ~300 canoes to the Bay in 1684; roughly 420 guns/year traded in the early 1690s ≈ 1 gun per 7 people, maybe 1 usable gun per 4 people allowing for a ~3-year gun lifespan. This armed the Cree–Assiniboine bloc against the Dakota Sioux (south), Gros Ventre/Blackfoot (southwest), and Chipewyan (north).
- Warfare was constant 1670–1870 with the Sioux. The French first tried to broker peace (du Lhut 1678, later La Vérendrye), then cynically used the Sioux to raid the HBC's supply flanks the way the English had used the Iroquois against New France — Sioux raids peaked ~1728, driving Assiniboine as far as the head of the Churchill River.
- Cost of gun dependency: while the French held York Factory (1694–1714) supply ships were irregular; Assiniboine who had "lost the use of their bows" were caught without powder and massacred — James Knight reported bands reduced from ~200 tents to a handful, and ~6,000 men killed along the Cree–Chipewyan frontier (mostly Chipewyan losses).
- Territorial dynamics to 1763: Assiniboine abandoned the Rainy River southeast by the 1730s (though bands like the Eagle-Eyed Assiniboine stayed east of Lake Winnipeg into the 1740s, and they still claimed the Red River forks as their own territory in 1737); expansion turned west-northwest through the parkland corridor (Henday met them all the way to ~111°W in 1755, amicably camping with Gros Ventre and Blackfoot). Cree expansion pushed northwest toward Lake Athabasca until a 1760 peace with the Beaver and Slave; east of Reindeer Lake their advance stopped ~1720 once Fort Churchill (1717) let northern peoples arm themselves and erased the Cree military edge.
- Takeaway: by 1720 the great expansion was mostly done; a relatively stable middleman trading system settled in.

## Ch. 2 — Land and life in the western interior before 1763

- The ecological chapter — foundation for everything later. Three habitat zones: **woodland** (only two big game species, moose and woodland caribou, at modest densities; but rich small game — beaver, marten, lynx, otter, mink, muskrat — plus fish, waterfowl, and wild rice in the southeast), **grassland** (rich big game: bison, pronghorn, mule deer, wapiti/red deer; poor small game outside wooded valleys/uplands), and the transitional **parkland**, which had nearly everything except woodland caribou.
- Resources varied in time as well as space. Irregular fluctuations: drought/wet cycles hammered muskrat (drowned young in high water, froze or died of tularemia in stagnant low water — recovery took ~3 years) and made wild-rice harvests fail about one year in four.
- Seasonal cycles of the three zones were **complementary, not parallel** — the crucial insight. Woodlands: summer plenty (fishing, waterfowl concentrated on lakes/rivers), grim Dec–Feb with scattered game and constant starvation risk. Grasslands: summer abundance (huge rutting herds) but nearly uninhabitable in winter as bison scattered for shelter. Parkland: the inverse — bison drifted _into_ the parkland in winter for shelter, so it was food-rich exactly when the other zones were poorest. Perversely, a _mild_ winter meant hardship in the parkland because bison stayed out on the open plains.
- Archaeology of subsistence: Blackduck/Selkirk toolkits (bow-and-arrow points, bone tools, ceramics, net sinkers) are similar; differences are stylistic. Sites are extensive but shallow — repeated seasonal occupation. Woodland Selkirk sites = fishing camps (fish bone, clam shell); grassland/parkland sites like Stott Mound near Brandon (~1670) are 99% bison bone with evidence of drives over a precipice. Point: local economic specialization + seasonal band movement across two or three zones.
- Early observers confirm the pattern. Jesuits: Assiniboine/Cree "wander," gather at lakes in summer, harvest wild oats (rice); Swampy Cree near the Bay by contrast roamed constantly, purely hunting. La Vérendrye: the Cree called the boreal Shield "sterile country," refused to winter on the lower Winnipeg River (his men feared starving), and the forest margins/parkland were comparatively thickly peopled in winter (his men killed 2–3 moose a day crossing to Fort Maurepas in Feb 1737).
- Assiniboine annual round in the southeast: spring fishing camps + raids into Sioux country; midsummer bison surrounds on the open plains; late summer/autumn (sometimes early winter) whole-family trading treks to the Mandan villages on the Missouri for corn and beans — deliberately slow (6+ weeks for a 20-day distance) because they hunted and dried surplus meat en route, since the Mandan had little meat/fat of their own. These were social/subsistence expeditions as much as trade.
- Two overlapping exploitation cycles emerge from the English travel accounts (Kelsey 1690–92; Smith & Waggoner 1756–64; Pink 1766–67; Cocking 1770s):
    - **Forest–parkland cycle** (Cree + HBC-connected Assiniboine): summer = men canoe to the Bay while families fish at spots like The Pas ("Dering's Point"); Aug–Oct = moose hunting and beaver trapping along the forest edge; Nov–Mar = winter in the parkland with the Assiniboine hunting pounded bison, trapping wolves/foxes; Mar–May = canoe-building camps on the rivers, fishing, waterfowl, spring furs.
    - **Grassland–parkland cycle** (Assiniboine trading via middlemen or the French): winter in parkland pounds; spring sturgeon weirs and war parties; summer on the open plains; late summer/fall Mandan trip.
- The two cycles overlapped in the parkland every winter → constant inter-regional contact and technology transfer (e.g., Cree learned the bison pound from the Assiniboine). Result: broad "ecological flexibility" that would later let these groups switch economies fast when the trade changed. Note Kelsey's 1691 description of a surround on foot — mass bison hunting predates horses and guns here.
- Winter provisioning was a genuine constraint even for Europeans: Hearne nearly starved at Cumberland House (1774–75); Cocking had to send men to winter with the Indians in the parkland to stretch the post's food.

## Ch. 3 — Traders and middlemen

- Frame: Treaty of Utrecht (1713) gives England the Bay permanently. York Factory and Fort Albany dominate; Fort Churchill (opened 1717) skims Chipewyan/Michinipi Cree trade but never rivals York. French response after being shut out of the Bay: cut the HBC off from its hinterland — La Vérendrye's 1730s post chain across southern Manitoba/Saskatchewan. York Factory's fur volumes climb to the early 1730s, then decline erratically to a 1750s low at peak French competition, recovering as the French withdraw.
- 1715 snapshot of York's reach under James Knight: 172 canoes from Misshenipee Cree (upper Churchill), Sturgeon Cree (SE Manitoba), Stone Indians (= Assiniboine, woodland and parkland divisions), Upland Assiniboine/Cree — plus far-travellers: Muscotay (plains people between the Saskatchewan branches, probably Blood/Blackfoot), "Archithinue" (Cree label for Blackfoot/Gros Ventre allies), "Mountain Indians" (39 days downstream, 3 months back — Ray argues Mandan and/or Hidatsa; their 1716 Crow slaves push the Crow–Hidatsa split back earlier than usually thought), "Strange" Indians (possibly Sarsi), and Chipewyan on foot.
- This vast direct-trade catchment (Missouri to Great Slave Lake) collapses after ~1720. Causes: (1) the Assiniboine/Cree enforced a trading blockade by force — Knight's peace treaties kept collapsing, and distant groups thereafter appear only _escorted_ by Assiniboine/Cree; (2) horses spreading north by ~1740 made plains groups abandon canoes; (3) the sheer distance meant provisioning disasters — late supply ships from England (e.g., 1716) delayed departures and whole parties starved going home; the Blood quit the Bay for good after starvation deaths in 1762. When Cocking (1772) invited the Archithinue to bypass the middlemen, they refused flat out: too far, no canoes, fear of starving. Distance + hardship + canoe abandonment handed the Assiniboine/Cree a de facto monopoly — by the 1750s canoe tallies, virtually all traffic at York was Cree/Assiniboine (~50% from the Saskatchewan valley, ~18% Churchill, ~20% Home Guard Cree near the post) plus ~12% Ojibwa.
- Mechanics of trade — the accounting trinity:
    - **Made Beaver (MB)**: unit of account = one prime beaver pelt; all goods and furs priced in MB.
    - **Official standard of trade** (goods prices) and **comparative standard** (fur values): the Indians treated these as customary and fiercely resisted changes, so the company could barely move them even during wartime inflation (1690s letters told factors to squeeze more beaver per gun "carefully avoiding any measures which may disgust them").
    - **Factor's/double standard**: the traders' workaround — short measures on powder, cloth, beads, etc., or demanding extra pelts. The Indians understood and bargained over _this_ because it dealt in discrete objects they could see, unlike abstract weight/measure changes. The gain over the official standard = **overplus**. Overplus rose under monopoly, collapsed in the 1750s under French pressure — a sensitive index of competition.
- **Gift exchange**: mandatory pre-trade ceremony (an Indigenous institution affirming friendship — no gifts, no trade). Factors financed gifts out of the overplus; tobacco, beads, brandy most common. The company also weaponized gifting: outfitting envoys to broker peace, seeding goods to distant bands to lure them down (cf. Kelsey's inventory).
- **Inelastic demand — the master key of the period**: Andrew Graham reckoned an average trader needed only ~70 MB of goods a year plus ~30 MB to "squander." Nomadism + canoe cargo limits capped what a family could use or carry. Consequence: better prices did NOT mean more furs; cheaper goods meant _fewer_ furs were needed, more leisure time at the post ("indolent life" in trader-speak). This is why cut-throat competition later proved so corrosive — it operated on liquor, the one commodity with elastic demand.
- Middleman economics: Assiniboine/Cree mostly bought goods _for their own use_, used them a year or two, then traded them on second-hand to Mandan, Gros Ventre, Blood, Blackfoot, Sarsi, Chipewyan at steep markups (guns 12 MB → 36 MB to Blackfoot; knives 1 MB → 9 MB to Chipewyan). Ray de-mystifies the markup: it wasn't profit-seeking in the European sense — it covered the double standard actually paid, breakage/wear, and the gifts they in turn had to give their own trading partners. Graham noted few middlemen did any trapping themselves.
- French inland posts (1730s–50s) didn't break the monopoly — they sat inside Assiniboine/Cree territory and simply saved some middlemen the trip to the Bay. By 1720 the Cree/Assiniboine were the pivot of the whole system, dictating terms to Europeans and Indians alike, and thereby regulating the pace and direction of material culture change in the interior.

## Ch. 4 — Arms, brandy, beads, and sundries

- Method: reads the York Factory account books (1689–1780) as a window on what goods actually flowed inland, to whom, and why. (This is the chapter with all the trade-volume graphs.)
- **Guns**: peak of 510 sold in 1691, then an erratic long decline; French competition cuts 1730s volumes to ~75% of the 1720s — but crucially there's no rebound after the French withdraw, and no supply shortage or price rise to explain it. Ray's answer: **falling demand**. Guns were mainly _war_ weapons; the initial arms race saturated, and after that sales were mostly replacement of worn-out pieces.
- Who actually used guns for hunting: only the woodland groups, especially the Home Guard Cree — in the forest, close-range kills, quick death, shot not deflected by brush like arrows → real dependence (some had "forgotten the bow" by 1716). Parkland/grassland peoples had good reasons NOT to: no repair facilities inland (York was the only "service centre" with armourers); flintlocks froze in prairie winters (Isham's imagined chief: give us locks that don't freeze); pound and surround techniques killed bison at close range where a gun was worse than a bow (slow to reload, risked stampeding the herd); and arrows were retrievable/free while shot was a recurring cost. Henday 1754: his companions killed 7 bison with bows and wouldn't waste ammunition on moose while buffalo were plentiful.
- Gun-length evidence backs this: Fort Albany (woodland clientele) shifted to handy 3–3.5 ft guns after 1700; York Factory stayed 55–70% 4-ft guns — accuracy for warfare mattered more to parkland customers (skirmish, strike first, retreat to the woods against mounted enemies). Powder/shot volumes also trend down — no sign the population was becoming gun-dependent for food. So the standard picture of instant, universal firearm dependency is wrong; it held only near the Bay.
- **Cloth and blankets**: the mirror image — bought overwhelmingly by the bayside/woodland Cree, and the share grows over time. After the inland trade died in 1774 (Cumberland House opens), York still sold ~75% as many blankets and ~55% as much cloth as before even though fur receipts fell by three-quarters. Ecological logic: early gun adoption + trapping had stripped the Shield forests of large game, so woodland people literally lacked hides for clothing — cloth was necessity there, luxury in the bison-rich parkland (where leather was plentiful and the dry climate made hide clothing comfortable anyway).
- **Metal goods** mixed: knives, files, hatchets spread roughly per-capita everywhere (~70–80% going inland, matching population); ice chisels (for opening beaver lodges) went almost entirely inland — sales collapse to nothing after 1774; kettles steady, evenly spread — too bulky for French canoes to compete on.
- **Luxuries**: beads mostly bought near the post (~75% by the 1760s–70s); tobacco constant to 1774 — HBC's Brazil twist tobacco was unbeatable (the French couldn't match it; Henday: with Brazil tobacco the French "would engross all the trade"), low-bulk/high-value so middlemen carried it deep inland. Alcohol is the outlier: volumes _rise_ with competition (peaks 1753 and 1771) — the reverse pattern of every other good.
- Second-hand trade confirmed on the ground: La Vérendrye watched the Assiniboine swap worn kettles, axes, guns, knives to the Mandan for corn and for Mandan craftwork (painted robes, dressed skins, featherwork — "sharp traders" who cleaned the Assiniboine out). Notably horses do NOT appear among goods the Assiniboine took from the Mandan in 1739, and only European goods flowed south — Ray argues the Assiniboine–Mandan trade was probably essentially a _historic_ (post-contact) phenomenon; the Mandan potsherd at the Stott site more likely arrived with Mandan travellers en route to the Bay than via Assiniboine trade.
- Henday's winter with the parkland middlemen (1754–55) is the set piece: they refused to trap in Blackfoot country (unsafe + unnecessary), laughed at his nagging, wintered easily on bison, then in spring traded their used goods to 200+ tents of Blackfoot for prime furs and some horses. On the way down the Saskatchewan the French posts (Fort à la Corne, Fort Paskoyac) skimmed the best furs with watered brandy, leaving the heavy low-value pelts for the Bay — division of the trade: French took light/prime furs with light luxury goods; HBC got the bulk trade and held customers with tobacco and heavy goods (kettles, guns) the French couldn't transport. Cree also began acting as _agents_, carrying commissions ("directions what to trade for") for relatives who stopped at the French houses.

## Ch. 5 — Migrations, epidemics, and population changes, 1763–1821

- Direction of movement flips: Assiniboine expansion had been NW; from the 1790s the drift turns **south**. Detailed band geographies: Mackenzie (1790s) — westernmost Assiniboine near Fort George, bulk still in the Assiniboine valley; M'Gillivray (1794) names Canoe, Grand River, Strong Wood bands; Alexander Henry the Younger (1808) lists ten bands totalling ~850 lodges, two-thirds concentrated between the Souris and Qu'Appelle valleys, one-third scattered between the South Saskatchewan and Battle rivers; HBC district reports (1815–1822, Fidler and John McDonald) show the same pattern — Assiniboine gone from the Red River valley, everything north of the Assiniboine River and east of the escarpment, and from forested districts. A few relict Woodland Assiniboine bands (Strong Wood, ~60 tents near the McLeod River) survive NW of the North Saskatchewan.
- Cree: NW expansion stalls by 1784, when smallpox (which hit them three years before the Chipewyan) breaks their military power in the north — they give back Portage la Loche / Île-à-la-Crosse country to the Chipewyan by 1790 and pivot **south into the parklands/grasslands**. 1793: Cree annihilate a 16-lodge Gros Ventre band near South Branch House; the terrified Gros Ventre withdraw from the upper Qu'Appelle and lower South Saskatchewan, opening it to Cree/Assiniboine settlement. Plains Cree crystallize as a distinct population: ~110 lodges (Mackenzie 1789) → ~230 lodges by 1815–22 (Carlton 100, Brandon-area bands 130), mostly north/northwest of the Qu'Appelle.
- **Ojibwa replacement**: as Cree vacate southeastern/south-central Manitoba (Lake of the Woods → Lake Winnipeg, interlake, forested Swan River), Ojibwa move in behind them, reaching their NW limit around the Cumberland district by ~1820. Ray's explanation of the puzzle (why would Ojibwa settle lands the Cree abandoned as "exhausted"?): differing economic orientations. The Cree had been middlemen for a century, were indifferent (even disdainful) trappers, and when the inland posts (post-1763) bypassed them they switched to provisioning bison rather than trapping; the Ojibwa were expert trappers, actively recruited by the NWC, and could still pull furs from "exhausted" districts. Different niches → peaceful succession. (He also floats alternates: recovery time-lags, lower Ojibwa densities.) Meanwhile Swampy Cree drifted south from York Factory into Cumberland — counterflow.
- New tension: with Europeans trading directly inland, Assiniboine/Plains Cree can no longer be middlemen to the Mandan/Blackfoot — they now _compete_ with those groups in the same provision markets → the old cooperative trade partnerships (Mandan, Blackfoot) turn hostile; war with the Sioux continues throughout.
- **Smallpox 1780–82**: entered via the Sioux/Missouri villages, raced through the mobile plains populations first (South Saskatchewan by Oct 1781, the forks by Nov, Cumberland Dec, The Pas by end of Jan), reaching the north-central Manitoba woodlands from the _west_. Mortality worsened by responses: the sick were considered doomed and abandoned (starvation finished many), and fever victims plunged into lakes. Thompson put losses at one-half to three-fifths. Assiniboine seem to have lost "only" ~a third (300 lodges 1776 → 200 in 1789) — and interestingly Thompson says _men_ died disproportionately (cooling themselves in rivers), so surviving women/children consolidated into fewer lodges, keeping people-per-lodge high.
- Astonishing rebound: Assiniboine roughly quadruple between 1776 and 1809 despite the epidemic. Then the **1819 measles + whooping cough** double epidemic: Brandon Assiniboine fall from ~5,000 (460 lodges × 11/lodge, Fidler) to ~2,600 (370 lodges × 7) — roughly half; Carlton loses ~42%; adult male deaths again ~42–44% of the toll.
- Cree growth by contrast is sluggish — possibly flat 1780s–1810. Ray's tentative explanation from Fidler's 1815 Red River "census" age pyramids: Cree had the smallest shares in the young and old age brackets (33%/7%) vs Assiniboine (46%/9%) and Ojibwa (40%/8%) — i.e., the fully grassland-adapted Assiniboine and the agriculture/wild-rice-supplemented Ojibwa had more reliable food and could carry more "non-productive" dependents → higher growth potential. Hayden's retrospective figure of 800 Cree lodges in 1776 is far too high. Ojibwa in MB/SK total maybe ~1,400 by 1821.

## Ch. 6 — The destruction of fur and game animals

- Short, grim chapter. 1763–1821 = peak competition = "ruthless exploitation"; by the 1820s much of central/southern Manitoba and Saskatchewan is stripped. Evidence: district reports + Simpson's annual syntheses.
- Fur collapse by district: Lac la Pluie — beaver long gone, reduced to marten/muskrat; Fort Dauphin — marten returns crash from 2,196 (1817) to 366 (1821); Cumberland — trapped out since the 1790s, 37,007 muskrat taken in 1819 as refugee trappers pile in; Carlton — "nearly exhausted in Beaver," only low-value wolves/foxes abound; Île-à-la-Crosse and Lesser Slave Lake also depleted. Simpson closes the Upper Red River department entirely and shrinks Swan River (1824) as unprofitable.
- Not just overhunting — natural shocks compounded it: a beaver epizootic ~1800 (John Tanner found lodges full of dead and dying beaver near Pembina, "red and bloody about the heart," pond dwellers worst hit; Fidler independently reports the same sudden collapse across a huge belt from Athabasca to near Moose Factory); drought-year forest fires (e.g., 1804–05 Lac la Pluie burns killing beaver); water-level swings whipsawing muskrat. Thompson even claimed game and waterfowl vanished with the people after the 1781 smallpox — Ray reads that skeptically (scavenger dogs/wolves may have caught _something_, but the "disappearance" of game was probably partly the collapse of the Indigenous provisioning workforce plus post-epidemic demoralization skewing perceptions).
- Consequence: by the 1820s the trade of southern Manitoba/eastern Saskatchewan hangs on **muskrat alone** — a boom-bust species — so Cumberland's Indians swing between "the most wealthy and independent in the Country" and "the most wretched" (Simpson, 1825).
- Big game: moose and woodland caribou seriously reduced east of Lake Manitoba/Red River by the late 1700s; Lac la Pluie in the 1820s so short of large animals that people lacked skins for clothing and moccasins; Winnipeg district "so much exhausted" Simpson predicted the Indians would have to leave to avoid starvation. Further west conditions were still tolerable: moose/caribou plentiful around Cumberland and Île-à-la-Crosse; parkland districts (Brandon, Swan River, Carlton, Edmonton) still had abundant bison as the dietary mainstay with moose/red deer as backup.
- Net state at 1821: prime furs gone from forests and adjacent parkland; game gone in the eastern forests; game still adequate to the west. The stage for everything in chs. 10–12.

## Ch. 7 — New economic opportunities

- The re-organization of the trade after 1763. Montreal pedlars (post-Pontiac, licensing scrapped 1768) flood inland; HBC finally abandons its wait-at-the-Bay policy and builds Cumberland House (1774); the Nor'Wester coalitions form (first NWC 1779, successors to 1821, plus the XY Co. 1798–1804). Peter Pond reaches the Athabasca (1778) — the richest fur country on the continent.
- Key structural change: once the trade stretches to the Athabasca, **the forest can no longer feed the trade**. Forest resources barely sustained a post (Cocking: Cumberland's locale could support 12 men), let alone the canoe brigades; Thompson's shorter Nelson–Burntwood–Churchill route to Athabasca was useless for want of provisions en route. Solution: a **provision supply network anchored on parkland bison** — HBC: Hudson House (1779), Cumberland converted into a provision depot (fish from Beaver Lake + prairie pemmican + a few potatoes), Norway House (1801) provisioning the Athabasca brigades; NWC: Cumberland depot (300–500 bags of pemmican + 200 kegs of grease down from the upper Saskatchewan yearly), Bas de la Rivière (fed from the Red/Assiniboine/Qu'Appelle) and Rainy Lake (also drawing wild rice and corn locally).
- Scale of the food demand: post rations of 5 lb fresh meat/man/day (Fidler); Henry's modest 41-person Pembina post ate ~63,000 lb of bison in one winter (112 cows, 35 bulls, plus bears, fish, 325 bushels of potatoes); 21 posts on the Assiniboine River alone in 1795 imply ≥1.3 million lb ≈ 3,000+ bison; voyageurs ate ~8 lb fresh meat or 1–1.5 lb pemmican a day; NWC pemmican requisitions reach 644 ninety-lb bags (≈58,000 lb) in 1813. A brand-new economic link is welded between the parkland-grassland and the forest.
- The Indigenous pivot: exactly as the pedlars bypass the middlemen, the provision market opens. The Assiniboine — "rich in furs" with prized beaver as late as 1768 (Graham) — switch fastest to selling dried meat, grease, and pemmican. First clear sign: autumn 1780, Assiniboine burn the prairies around Hudson House _deliberately_ so bison stay away and the post must pay dearly for provisions — they immediately grasp and exploit the companies' food vulnerability. By the 1790s observers agree the Assiniboine were "not beaver hunters" (Mackenzie) and "the worst hunters of any Indians in the North-West" for fine furs (MacDonnell) — their trade was wolves, foxes, buffalo robes, and provisions. Plains Cree follow suit by 1821.
- Provisioner power politics: prairie burning becomes a routine pressure tactic (sometimes backfiring on the local bands themselves); traders at Saskatchewan posts lived under threat of food boycotts and "were obliged to overlook the grossest offenses, even murder" (Franklin expedition); Assiniboine threatened to cut supplies to Forts Augustus/Edmonton to keep ammunition from their enemies.
- Meanwhile forest-dwellers specialize the other way: winter trapping + summer fishing, some as post hunters, and some (Rainy Lake–Lake of the Woods bands) as professional canoe-builders (including the NWC's canot du nord) — but the forest economy could support only a few.

## Ch. 8 — Economic dependency and the fur trade: contrasting trends

- Theme: same institutions (gifts, credit, standards) persist 1763–1821, but competition changes what they _do_ — and woodland vs parkland peoples diverge sharply in dependency.
- **Gift-giving inflates and mutates.** Companies now use gifts to manufacture and prop up "trading captains": the Captain's Outfit (a pseudo-military suit — laced coat, breeches, hat with feathers; lieutenants got lesser suits; the NWC added flags), granted or withheld to discipline bands over debts. Pre-1763 York outfitted ~15 big captains; under competition every small band gets clothed, often with rival chiefs pledged to rival firms — M'Gillivray clothed 22 chiefs in one day at Fort George (1795). Since chiefly legitimacy rested on generosity, companies bid each other up in backing "their" chiefs → cost spiral, wealth becomes a prerequisite of leadership, factionalism and instability inside bands. Net effect of gift escalation + uncollectable credit: the real price of goods falls continuously and goods become geographically ubiquitous.
- **Credit** goes from a managed variable line (Martin's instructions: trust good Indians, trim credit for slow payers so they can clear) to chaos: take debt at one company's post in fall, sell the furs to its rival in spring — double returns. Chief-making was partly a debt-collection strategy; it failed.
- **The alcohol/tobacco trap**: demand for utilitarian goods stayed inelastic (even fell in places), so as effective prices dropped, the slack flowed into the two addictive, giveaway commodities. Advance tobacco parties before a band arrived; traders sending men out to intercept brigades with gifts before rivals could (posts within sight of each other near Brandon); NWC men going straight to hunting camps. Hearne (1775): with the brandy gone, trade simply stopped, whatever else he had. But quality still mattered — Bird (1796) feared the Nor'Westers' improved cloth and guns more than their rum. Brazil tobacco remained an HBC pillar (2–3,000 lb/yr at York; the Blackfoot meanwhile smoked what Henday scorned as dried horse-dung).
- **Regional price geography**: where the NWC or Indigenous middlemen were sole suppliers, goods cost far more (HBC gun 10–14 MB; NWC 20; Cree middlemen selling to Assiniboine 36, even half-worn guns 25–30) and bulky goods (kettles!) were scarce — Henry the Elder's 1776 Assiniboine camp had exactly one kettle, reluctantly surrendered.
- **Divergence**: parkland bands provisioning the posts grew _less_ dependent — bison met most needs, trapping gear (chisels, traps, hatchets) mattered less; Turnor's neat contrast (1779): at Cumberland House the trade is brandy and giveaway sundries because the people have leather and can kill buffalo with bows; at woodland Gloucester House the trade is cloth, blankets, guns, ammunition, ironwork — necessities, "having no Leather in their Country." Woodland Cree/Ojibwa, squeezed by vanishing game AND furs, needed traps, ammunition, cloth just to keep operating → deepening dependency. Account books 1810–14 (Cumberland vs Carlton vs Brandon) quantify it: cloth ≈26% of Indian spending at Cumberland vs 10–13% at the parkland posts; ~15 yd cloth and 2+ blankets per Cumberland family over the four years vs a fraction of that inland; ammunition burned 6–12× faster in the woods; only tobacco ran the other way (>25% of parkland-post trade value). Harmon (1801) on borderland Cree/Ojibwa: they could barely survive now without firearms, axes, kettles — bows nearly forgotten.
- **Horses** (the inter-tribal trade side): two entry routes — west (from Blackfoot/Gros Ventre; absent for Kelsey 1690, present in eastern Alberta for Henday 1754, who bought his first horse near Sounding Creek; abundant in central-Saskatchewan parklands by the 1770s — Pink 1766 met a 16-tent Assiniboine band with many horses who could no longer paddle; Cocking noted bands still gathering birchbark vs bands that had dropped the canoe entirely) and southeast (via the Mandan; first two horses brought to Fort La Reine 1742; still scarce in Manitoba into the 1800s — Henry the Younger's first two horses on the Red River only in 1800, Ojibwa there still canoe-only).
- Horse culture matured late and unevenly: Henday's Eagle Indians used horses only as pack animals; Assiniboine notoriously left herds untended at the first snowfall at some wooded island until spring (vs Blackfoot who moved and fed their stock) → heavy winter die-offs; horse raiding (a status institution) only ripens by the 1790s ("endless source of quarrel" — MacDonell; posts guarding herds day and night in season). The northeastern plains tribes stayed **horse-poor** (Fidler 1815: Assiniboine ~500 horses for ~800 men; Cree ~200; Ojibwa ~100 — about 1 per lodge) — harsher winters + recent woodland origins; dogs remained crucial (Henry counted 230 dog travois in one 35-man band ≈ 13 dogs/family ≈ 2,300 lb capacity). Punchline: horses could have accelerated Europeanization by raising carrying capacity, but didn't — because deepening bison dependence was simultaneously _reducing_ demand for European goods.

## Ch. 9 — Land and life: a changing mosaic

- How the old seasonal-cycle geography (ch. 2) held, bent, or broke across the region, 1763–1821.
- **Lower Saskatchewan (Cumberland–Cedar Lake–The Pas)**: still churning with seasonal movement, now with layered populations. Ojibwa/Swampy Cree newcomers summered at fishing camps (Cedar Lake, Cumberland, Moose Lake) and wintered in the Carrot–Red Deer–Swan valleys among the moose; the resident Cree did the reverse, abandoning those same valleys in winter for parkland bison hunts (Tanner's Ottawa band literally "followed the path of the Crees" onto the prairie mid-winter and stayed a month+). The counterflow worked because the small immigrant bands could live where big Cree winter camps could not. Newcomers also imported **maple sugaring** — by 1812 Manitoba-maple sugar camps lined the Red Deer River, groves held exclusively by (mostly Ojibwa) bands; Cree didn't take it up.
- Fort Nipawi (1794–95, Bird): same pattern — locals gone south for winter; post trade = provisions (moose/red deer meat until early Dec, then bison); the crippling constraint was **transport** — no horses, few dogs, so neither the post nor the Indians could shuttle parkland meat into the forest; hence people had to move themselves to the food. Also a striking mid-winter pan-district encampment in the parkland building a great pound to provision a war expedition against the Gros Ventre — the parkland as the region's surge-capacity larder.
- **North/South Saskatchewan parkland posts**: the posts themselves now sat in the parkland → many Assiniboine/Cree bands cut their forest ties entirely (no more canoe voyages, no more summer fishing camps; summers now in the open grasslands), keeping only the winter parkland-pound routine (M'Gillivray 1794–95: Strong Wood and Grand River Assiniboine pounds near Fort George; big Cree pound camps in the Beaver Hills — some bands so absorbed in pounding they quit trapping, to the traders' annoyance, though most still ran two fur hunts, early winter and early spring). Meanwhile Woodland Cree north of the river still filed out of the "strong woods" each winter to join established pound camps ("we expect no more fur from them during the season, as they idle, playing and eating buffalo" — Henry), living dispersed as isolated families in the forest and aggregating into big camps in the parkland. Traders actively tried to keep bands anchored to the forest for the fur hunt — fighting an adaptation already far advanced. Depleting forest game pushed the winter exodus ever earlier (Carlton 1826–27: leaving in December, "sooner than normal").
- **Upper Assiniboine (Fort Alexandria / Bird Mountain)**: McLeod and Harmon show winter Cree in the Nut/Touchwood hills slipping off mid-winter to the plains for "their beloved Buffalo"; Harmon (Feb 1802): everyone but his hired hunters gone for a couple of months, back by late March, spring fur hunt late April–May 20. Assiniboine wintered around the Qu'Appelle lakes / Good Spirit Lake and pushed south to the Missouri in summer. So here the two-cycle overlap persisted.
- **Southern Manitoba — the break**: by the early 1800s the big cross-boundary seasonal migrations are essentially over. Brandon-district Assiniboine stayed within the parkland/grassland year-round, wintering in the wooded entrenched valleys of the Souris/middle Assiniboine/lower Qu'Appelle and in Moose & Turtle mountains (micro-habitats that replicated the parkland function), summering toward the Missouri. Lower Assiniboine/Red River now Ojibwa country — and the Ojibwa did NOT run the old two-zone cycle: small mobile trapping bands (nuclear family → winter band of a few related families behind a good hunter → short-lived spring hunting band of 2–7 hunters for the final pre-summer fur sweep → summer lakeside fishing villages) — a flexible woodland social machine that made parkland bison unnecessary.
- Why the break happened there: bison range already contracting (herds no longer reached the Red River forks in numbers by 1820, so big winter villages of the old Assiniboine type — 100+ lodges, 700–1,200 people — were unsupportable); population much lower than a century earlier (Ojibwa in the three districts <800; earlier Assiniboine single villages exceeded that); the upper Red River valley, a Sioux–Ojibwa no-man's-land, functioned as a game refuge whose plenty attracted the Ojibwa; and some Ojibwa part-time corn horticulture stored food for winter, removing the need to chase bison. Farther NW the overlap system survived because forest game kept dwindling, bison still entered the parkland seasonally, Cree kept drifting south to replenish the pressure, and traders kept nudging people back to the forests for furs.

## Ch. 10 — The changing demographic picture after 1821

- Migration: the southward drift accelerates. Assiniboine pull out of the middle Assiniboine/Qu'Appelle and prairie Carlton and gravitate to the international border — pulled by American Missouri posts (closer to the retreating herds, and the buyers of bulky robes/hides no one wanted to haul hundreds of miles overland; Simpson 1831 concedes they visit the Americans more than the HBC, and in 1838 decides counter-posts on the Bow would cost too much), and pushed by the 1838 catastrophe (below). By Maximilian (1833) and Denig (1856) the tribe sits between the Cypress Hills/Souris and the Milk/Missouri; by the late 1850s (US report 1857, Palliser 1858) about **three-quarters of all Assiniboine live south of the border**, the Canadian remnant mostly around Moose Mountain.
- Cree expand into everything the Assiniboine vacate. Palliser (1863): Plains Cree territory from the Cypress Hills/Wood Mountain NE to the Qu'Appelle–Assiniboine forks and N to the forest, NW to the Neutral/Beaver hills and Edmonton; **1,920 Plains Cree lodges (~11,500 people)** vs a mere **85 Thickwood (Woodland) Cree lodges (~425)** — a total inversion of Mackenzie's 1789 world where only a third of Western Cree (110 lodges) were plains-dwellers. Ojibwa boundaries barely move (centred east of the future Manitoba border; small trapper colonies as far as Lesser Slave Lake, planted by the companies).
- **Smallpox 1837–38 — the great divergence.** Entered again from the Missouri (Fort Union); Assiniboine caught it at the American posts, fled north spreading it (Carlton by November; Edmonton country too, via the Blood). Blood, Sarsi, Piegan, Blackfoot, Assiniboine lose up to ~three-quarters; Gros Ventre less (recent prior exposure). Assiniboine crash from ~1,200 lodges (Denig, 1838 pre-epidemic; up ~30% since Henry's 1809 count) to ~400 lodges / <3,000 people — two-thirds gone, and this time **no rebound**.
- The Cree were saved largely by **William Todd at Fort Pelly**: on 20 Sept 1837, on nothing more than an Indian report that the American fort's gates were shut against "some bad disease," he guessed smallpox and started vaccinating the next day (60 people on day one, using the newer cowpox vaccine London had supplied years earlier but traders had neglected). He vaccinated everyone who came, **trained Indigenous leaders to vaccinate their own bands** and sent them out with supplies, dispatched vaccine and instructions north (Shoal River/Swan Lake) and west (Carlton — whose own vaccine stock had gone inert — Île-à-la-Crosse, Edmonton). Confirmation only came in late December (a Carlton man died of it at the post). Result: the vaccinated Plains Cree and Parkland/Woodland peoples of MB/SK/eastern Alberta formed a firebreak; the epidemic died out over the winter. Todd's 25 Jan 1838 verdict: mortality "principally confined to the Assiniboins ... nearly all that remain of that once Numerous tribe" were the ~200 vaccinated tents. Denig corroborates (credits vaccination for saving 200 of the 400 surviving lodges). This is the episode my earlier note about "an HBC trader inoculating the Cree" refers to — it was **vaccination (cowpox), organized pre-emptively by Todd in 1837–38**.
- Aftermath arithmetic: Cree keep growing (Maximilian 1833: 600–800 lodges; Hayden 1853: 1,080; Palliser 1863: 1,920 — Denig's ~1,000–1,100 for 1855 may be more trustworthy than Palliser's high count) and by 1863 outnumber the Canadian Assiniboine ~11,500 : 1,000. Blackfoot confederacy numbers (Palliser): Blackfoot 600 [sic], Blood 2,800, Piegan 4,400, Sarsi 1,100.
- **1869–70 smallpox**: same Missouri entry, same Assiniboine vector north — but now no vaccine at the SK/AB posts. Isaac Cowie at Fort Qu'Appelle improvised: harvested lymph from a recently vaccinated Métis child onto bits of window glass and arm-to-arm vaccinated the whole post, the lakes population, and visiting bands, who carried it to the plains — Qu'Appelle and Touchwood Hills people escaped entirely; Red River vaccinated + quarantined. The unvaccinated — Assiniboine, Blackfoot groups, North Saskatchewan Cree — suffered badly, and mass starvation followed the epidemic. 1871 Indian Branch estimates: Cree ~7,000, SK Assiniboine ~500, Blackfoot 4,000, Blood 2,000, Piegan 3,000, Sarsi 200.
- Summary: differential epidemic mortality (a function of trade geography + who got vaccinated) redrew the ethnic map of the prairies — Cree demographic dominance and their occupation of former Assiniboine lands are downstream of 1838.

## Ch. 11 — Declining opportunities in a changing fur trade

- The 1821 HBC–NWC **merger** ends the competitive era and restores (a leaky) monopoly — undermined only along the border by Americans and increasingly by Métis free traders. Simpson's rationalization program follows.
- **Trimming the trade customs**: gift-giving survives but shrinks and shifts to consumables — by the 1860s (Cowie) the ceremony is tea, tobacco, sugar, a feast, the chief's certificate and modest semi-annual gratuities, not lavish clothing-and-rum spectacles. Credit returns to the disciplined pre-1763 model — lend only what a known hunter can repay. Reforms bite fastest in the forests, where dependent Woodland Indians had no alternative buyer; the plains tribes could resist longer.
- **Alcohol**: 1821 Act of Parliament conditions the monopoly on curbing liquor. Simpson halves gift allowances and ends fur-for-liquor sales at once; total ban north of Cumberland House from 1826; on the plains he moves gradually — he openly feared that cutting off rum while the company still depended on Plains provisions might provoke attacks — but by the 1860s the HBC neither traded nor gifted alcohol in the grasslands, holding trade against rum-running free traders on price and assortment instead. (Matches and corrects my earlier note: monopoly = little booze; competition = booze; post-merger = phased prohibition.)
- **Conservation/"nursing the country"** — a remarkable early game-management program: refuse out-of-season (summer) pelts; discourage cub-beaver killing (failed — cub beaver was a delicacy, and starving people ate beaver on sight, hiding the pelts to avoid detection); ban steel beaver traps ("the scourge of the Country," 1822; the ban collapses in northern Ontario by 1848 because Americans supplied traps); rotate the hunt between species with complementary population cycles (wet years 1825–30 = push muskrat, rest beaver/marten; reverse from 1830); rest whole districts by shuffling post locations (close Dauphin/Swan/Red Deer posts, open Fort Pelly, 1824 — beaver rebounding by 1826); and from 1826 hard **beaver quotas** per district in the Northern Department (based on 1823–25 averages, cut 20–50%; no quotas for lower Red River/Lac la Pluie where American proximity made enforcement hopeless; the Saskatchewan district got slack because its beaver came from Piegan raiding/trading south of the line — using American resources to fund Canadian conservation).
- Obstacles were cultural and structural: no Indigenous tradition of multi-year stockpiling ("respect the animal spirits and there will always be game"); no strong coercive band institutions; and above all a **hunting-range (not territorial) land system** — unexploited resources were "free" to neighbours, so one band's forbearance simply fed another's harvest. Simpson's counter-move: **fixed family hunting territories** — feasible only where people had already shifted from mobile big-game economies to small game + fish (widespread by mid-century in the northern forests away from American competition and the colony); Bishop's work suggests trespass norms themselves _emerged_ in response. And none of it worked without monopoly (Dauphin had to reopen in 1827 because settlers/freemen poached the rested district for the Red River petty traders).
- **The Métis** emerge as the pivotal new competitor. Post-merger downsizing (redundant posts closed, Montreal canoe route abandoned) cuts the labour force by up to two-thirds; discharged mixed-ancestry men concentrate in the Red River valley (moved up from Pembina in 1823 to St Boniface and White Horse Plains). Growth: ~500 (1821) → 1,300 (1831) → 2,600 (1843) → 3,250 (1856) → >12,000 (1870). Modeled much of their culture on the Plains way of life; from ~1820 the organized **Red River buffalo hunts** with 900-lb-capacity carts become their economic core — 540 carts (1820), 820 (1830), 1,210 (1840) ≈ up to ~1.09 million lb capacity — and their surplus goes to the HBC and colony. **The Métis take over the provision market the Indians had built**, and by the 1840s supply more pemmican than the company needs; Selkirk-colony flour etc. adds a little more. Meanwhile the merged company's total provision demand in the 1820s–30s (≈500–700 bags/yr) is roughly what the NWC _alone_ had needed in 1813 — a shrunken market now shared with new suppliers. Simpson in 1823 admits the plains tribes' "insolence" reflects a true reading of the company's dependence, and plots to end it via colony agriculture (the Métis hunts did it first).
- **Robes and hides**: the compensating market — but an American one. Robes were bulky; HBC's portage-choked overland routes couldn't carry them profitably (Brandon/Carlton traded robes in single/double digits 1811–15; London told Simpson to stop shipping them; his 1824 workaround was to route even muskrat to Canada via _England_ by ship). The Americans floated them down the Missouri in flatboats (26,000–200,000 robes/yr, 1815–30 estimates; steamboats from 1831; Cowie says still cheaper in the 1860s). So the Missouri posts offered both proximity to the herds and the best prices → the Assiniboine dealt directly with Fort Union etc. The 1844 Métis **Red River Trail cart trade to St Paul** (7,500+ robes in 1856) gave eastern parkland people a southern outlet via Métis middlemen. HBC robe trade grows late and modestly (Edmonton ships 9,090 robes in 1869). After 1870 demand shifts from winter robes to summer **hides** for eastern tanneries — low-skill, so white hunters flood in.
- Bison contraction timeline: Métis pressure empties SE Manitoba early (Métis hunting to the Cheyenne River by the late 1820s); herds seen at Carberry 1852 and on the Brandon townsite 1861; last Souris-country herd 1867, last wild kill there 1883; pemmican scarce along the Saskatchewan below Edmonton by 1852; by the late 1870s herds only north of the Cypress Hills/Wood Mountain; functionally gone from Saskatchewan by the 1880s.
- Chapter verdict: 1821–70 = shrinking options. Woodland peoples, dependent and unopposed by rival buyers, absorb the reforms first and become in effect company trappers; Plains peoples hold out longer on provision leverage + American outlets, but the Métis take their market and the herds retreat — by the 1870s they face the same dependency their woodland relatives met fifty years earlier.

## Ch. 12 — End of a way of life

- Southeastern Manitoba first: the old woodland→parkland winter migrations end (last big one recorded 1823–24 when herds came unusually close); with moose/deer exterminated east of Lake Winnipeg and fisheries unreliable, **the parkland "safety valve" is gone** and destitute Indians drift to the Red River colony. Simpson tries to dam the flow (attaching Netley Creek to the Winnipeg district, keeping Fort Alexander supplied so people had "no excuse" to go), partly fearing depopulation of his trapping districts, but it continues — the colony offers charity **and seasonal farm wages**, often working for Métis relatives at planting/harvest peaks. Simpson hopes wage work will "civilize" (i.e., farm-ify) them.
- Missionary agriculture: Rev. William Cochran's Indian agricultural settlement below Lower Fort Garry (early 1830s) and similar efforts teach farming to some Indians and Métis, but few take to sedentary life — farms are abandoned for the hunt when any alternative appears ("It is easier to hunt than to dig. A bow and arrow are lighter than a spade" — as Ross rendered their reasoning). By Confederation, though, many southern-Manitoba Indians live partly by small corn/potato patches and heavily by harvest labour (Indian Commissioner W.M. Simpson, 1871: Ojibwa and Sioux were the mainstay of the Portage la Prairie grain harvest).
- Saskatchewan/eastern Alberta lag ~30–50 years because the herds last longer. Fort Pitt (1830–31): bison still crossing the North Saskatchewan seasonally; 200+ lodge Cree/Blackfoot summer camps (sun-dance season); big autumn provision trade (Assiniboine parties of 150 men; a 130-lodge Cree band), then dispersal — Assiniboine to parkland, Cree split between northward fur hunts and southern pounds (eight pounds between Carlton and Pitt near Jackfish Lake alone; more in the Beaver Hills). But the northbound Cree trappers keep straggling back **starving** by mid-December — the forest can no longer hold them even for a shortened trapping season.
- The starvation-vs-account-books puzzle: Egg Lake and Fort Pelly journals (1853–54) report "starving" bands who are simultaneously trading in moose and bear skins. Ray's resolution: **food preference and use-conflict**, not sheer absence of food. Bison was "their beloved food" — Harmon's people left abundant moose/red deer to chase distant herds by _choice_; red deer (wapiti) was a famine food (its hard tallow congealed unpleasantly, its meat wouldn't salt and barely dried, its hides were weak — Hearne); and marketing bear skins conflicted with the traditional method of singeing bears whole for cooking, so bears sold as skins stopped being food. Necessity was pushing people to market skins of animals they would once have eaten.
- HBC as grocer: to keep woodland trappers on their lines, the company increasingly _sold them the bison products_ they'd otherwise have travelled for (Riding Mountain House traded 3–4,000 lb of pemmican **to** the Indians in winter 1868–69 to protect its mink/marten/fisher returns), serviced by "flying posts" (mobile winter camps pitched as near the herds as wood allowed — Turtle/Moose Mountain in the 1860s), summer cart trains and winter dog trains (with hired Indigenous fishermen catching jackfish for dog feed), and a rolling relocation of posts as the herds retreated (Brandon House marginal by the 1830s; Fort Ellice fed from the Touchwood Hills from the 1850s; Touchwood eclipsed by Fort Qu'Appelle in the early 1860s). Net effect: woodland Indians now depend on the company for **food** as well as clothing — including "store food" (flour, biscuits) — and become, in effect, company employees trapping on credit against the store.
- The plains endgame: by the 1860s scarcity forces Cree/Assiniboine/Ojibwa deeper into Blackfoot territory, reigniting war; the 1870 summer hunt in southern SK fails outright (herds withdrawn into Blackfoot country); winter 1870–71 sees widespread starvation (Qu'Appelle men snaring gophers); 1871 no better. And this time there is **no fallback**: the fur trade could absorb only a few trappers, and — the chapter's sharpest point — a century of specialization had destroyed the old ecological flexibility. Whereas 18th-century Woodland-Parkland peoples had generalized economies and crossed zones easily, the mature Plains cultures were bison-specialists who had _lost woodcraft_: in the Cypress Hills winter of 1871–72 (a former Blackfoot–Cree/Assiniboine neutral ground, hence still full of red deer and grizzly), Cowie records Assiniboine hunters getting lost in the woods and at least five shot dead by companions who mistook their red calfskin jackets for deer. Once the hills' neutrality broke, Cowie's Métis party alone killed 750 grizzlies and 1,500 red deer in a single winter — the last refuge stripped in a season.
- Political conclusion: 13 April 1871, Plains Cree chiefs council with W.J. Christie at Edmonton House — anxieties about troops at Red River and the HBC–Canada transfer; Chief Sweet Grass's dictated petition to Governor Archibald: we never sold our lands and don't want to; the country is "getting ruined of fur bearing animals"; we are poor and, when we settle, will need cattle, tools, implements, and help — "our country is no longer able to support us." Between 1871 and 1876 (extending to 1877), Treaties 1–7 extinguish Indian title across the grassland, parkland, and bordering woodland. Ray's closing argument: the transition was comparatively peaceful (vs the US plains wars) because **economics did the coercing** — two centuries of fur-trade interdependence made deliberate destruction of Indigenous peoples counterproductive to everyone, but fur-trade-driven specialization plus resource exhaustion (completed _before_ mass settlement arrived) left the Indians to accept reserves out of economic necessity, trusting government promises of help with yet another economic adjustment.

## Cross-cutting themes worth remembering

- **Agency and adaptation**: three successive Indigenous economic strategies — trapper (pre-1670s eastward pull), middleman (1670–1760s, built on the Nelson-basin chokepoint and gun monopoly), provisioner (1770s–1860s, built on the pemmican logistics of an expanding trade) — each a rational response to a changing market, each ultimately undermined (direct trade bypassed the middlemen; the Métis and shrinking herds destroyed the provisioners).
- **Inelastic demand** is Ray's recurring economic engine: because Indigenous consumers wanted a roughly fixed basket, price competition translated into leisure and liquor rather than more furs — the structural reason competition was culturally corrosive and monopoly comparatively benign.
- **Dependency was uneven and environmental**: real dependency (guns→ammunition→cloth→traps→eventually food) developed earliest and deepest where the resource base was poorest (Home Guard/woodland groups); plains groups grew _more_ self-sufficient as they leaned on bison — until the bison went.
- **Disease as geography**: epidemic mortality tracked trade routes and trading allegiances (who visited the Missouri posts; who got Todd's vaccine), and demographic outcomes then drove territorial succession (Cree replacing Assiniboine; Ojibwa replacing Cree).
- **The parkland** is the book's real protagonist: winter refuge of bison and people, hinge between the two exploitation cycles, larder of the continental trade, and the stage on which every group's seasonal round pivoted — its centrality explains both the region's inter-tribal peace (complementary niches) and the depth of the collapse when the herds withdrew from it.
