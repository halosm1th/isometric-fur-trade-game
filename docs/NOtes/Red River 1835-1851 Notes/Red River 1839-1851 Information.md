## Places
- Red River - the main area under consideration, a settlement of 5k people first created by Lord Selkirk when he got the charter, tried to settle Scots there, also settled the Demurons there. However the first settlement was disurpted by the Battle At Seven Oaks in 1816 when Cuthbert grant burned the HBC fort to the ground to support the North-West Comapny. However, once the companies were forced to merge, and the Selkirk estate handed control of hte COlony to the Company, george Simpson, now head of the Companies operations in Ruperts Land, dismissed many employees from the new merged company and giving them the choice of settling at red river or returning home. This led to the second main wave of european colonization and settlement. It was after this period that the Red River council came into being in 1835, the police force was reworked, law courts established, and life began to flourish until the 1849 sayer trial ended the company monarchy-aristocracy, introducing the element of popular power to the colonies mixed constitution.

- Norway House - A the main stopping port on Lake Winnipeg from York to Winnipeg

## Timeline
- **1839**
	- **Change in Religious Leadership**: Rev. D. T. Jones was succeeded by a new reverend.
	- **Possible Marriage**: Robert Logan possibly married Mrs. Ingham.
	- **Russian Agreement**: The Council of the Northern Department made a Russian Agreement
- **1840**
- **Missionary Expansion**: In June, Henry Budd was dispatched to Cumberland House to initiate a missionary station.
- **Benefit Fund**: The Council of the Northern Department created a Scheme for a Benefit Fund.

- **1841**
- **Religious Discord**: Newly arrived Church of England missionaries protested against modifications of the Liturgy, stirring the Presbyterian Scots of Kildonan.

- **1842**
	- **Governance**: Minutes from the Council were recorded, indicating ongoing administrative activities.

- **1843**
	- **Continued Governance**: Similar to the previous year, minutes from the Council were recorded.
	- **Economic Regulations**: Freight and passenger rates were established, indicating economic regulation.
	- **Presbyterian Advocacy**: A committee of Presbyterians, led by Alexander Ross, petitioned for a minister of their own church.

- **1844**
	- **Judicial Records Begin**: The General Quarterly Court of Assiniboia began keeping records, offering a detailed view into legal and social issues within the settlement. These records extend to 1872.
	- **Death**: James Sutherland died.
	- **Board of Works**: William Kennedy was appointed Chairman of the Board of Works.
	- **General Quarterly Court**: The General Quarterly Court was held on November 21st, with Alexr. Christie Esq. as the Governor of Assiniboia. Adam Thom, James Bird, Alexr. Ross, George M. Carey, and John Bunn were Councillors of Assiniboia. The case of _Wm Smith vs Joseph Kirton_ was heard by a jury regarding a charge of criminal conversation. The jury found the prisoner guilty but recommended mercy. The sentence was three calendar months’ imprisonment.

- **1845**
	- **Infrastructure**: A requisition was made for Norway House.
	- **Departure of Rev. William Cockran**: Rev. William Cockran, a significant figure in the religious life of Red River, left the settlement.
	- **Council Meeting**: Minutes of council, differing somewhat from the June 19 minutes, were passed on June 10. These minutes were from a meeting of the Governor and Council of Rupert's Land, held at the Red River Settlement.

- **1846**
	- **Interpreters**: A memo by Alexander Ross regarded interpreters in Neil McDonald's case, General Court, Nov. 29.
	- **Bridge Work**: An order was placed on Alexander Ross by Gary and Pruden for Rowland's Creek bridge, Nov. 6.

- **1847**
	- **Return of Rev. William Cockran**: Rev. William Cockran returned, suggesting a dynamic shift in religious leadership.
	- **Military Presence**: A decision to withdraw the Red River garrison was made, but it was too late to be carried out that year.
	- **Ecclesiastical Restructuring**: The Diocese of the North-West was established, with Joseph-Norbert Provencher as Bishop.
	- **Bridge Work**: An order was made on Alexander Ross for work on public roads, July 5.
	- **Cheese Prize**: An order came from the chairman of the Committee of Economy for a prize awarded for the production of the best cheese, Oct. 26.
	- **Jail Account**: A gaol account was attested by Governor Christie, Feb. 17.
	- **Public Works**: A public works account is recorded for Nov. 15.
	- **Salaries**: Vouchers for salaries were paid to Magistrates Bunn and Grant, Sept. 1.
	- **Council Attendance**: Major Crofton attended the Council for the first time on June 15.

- **1848**
- **Troop Withdrawal**: The troops embarked at Red River for York Factory in June, reducing direct British military presence.
- **Bishopric Formalization**: The Diocese of the North-West was formalized as the Bishopric of the North West, held by Joseph-Norbert Provencher.
- **Bridge Repairs**: An order to Alexander Ross was issued for repairing a bridge, May 17.
- **Supplies for prisoners**: A detailed statement of supplies for prisoners is recorded for May 15.
- **Fulling Mill**: An order to Alexander Ross was issued for boards for the fulling mill, Aug. 31.

**1849**
- **Parliamentary Inquiry**: A Select Committee of the British House of Commons conducted an inquiry into the Hudson’s Bay Company's stewardship of its North American territories.
- **Establishment of Rupert’s Land See**: The See of Rupert’s Land was created, and Rev. David Anderson was appointed Bishop, marking a formalization of Anglican religious authority.
- **Arrival of Bishop Anderson**: Bishop David Anderson arrived in Red River, reinforcing Anglican presence.
- **Trade Monopoly Discontent**: Considerable excitement occurred in the colony due to the Company's trade monopoly, signaling rising tensions.
- **British Response to Discontent**: Alexander Ross's letter to Caldwell in March, regarding discontent in Red River, prompted the British government to send troops as a warning to the United States, highlighting geopolitical concerns.
- **Sayer Trial**: There was a report of the Sayer trial in the Records of the General Quarterly Court of Assiniboia, and an account by Alexander Ross in the Ross Papers.
- **Council Meeting**: Ross wrote to Caldwell in March regarding discontent in Red River.
- Rev. John Smithurst is noted as being in Red River.

**1850**
- **Colvile's Arrival**: Eden Colvile and his wife arrived in Red River on August 11, indicating a shift in administrative leadership.
- **Demographic Composition**: The Red River Settlement had a population of over five thousand, comprising distinct communities such as the Scottish colony of Kildonan, the French and métis colonies, and the Scottish and Orcadian half-breed colony, underscoring its diverse social fabric.
- **District Reorganization**: The District of Assiniboia was re-organized into three judicial districts, each with a panel of magistrates. Among those nominated were six Canadians and métis.
- **Departure of Rev. John Macallum**: Rev. John Macallum is noted as gone, indicating changes in religious personnel.
- **Clerical Presence**: The clergy in the Settlement numbered seven, including Revs. William Cockran, John Chapman, Robert James, J. Smithurst, Robert Hunt, A. Cowley, and J. Hunter, illustrating the breadth of religious administration.
- **Council Meeting**: On September 5 and October 16, Colvile presided at two meetings with Caldwell present.

**1851**
- **American Delegation**: Governor Ramsey of Minnesota and his secretary, J. W. Bond, accompanied by Rev. John Black, visited the Colony from Pembina in September, suggesting growing ties and external interest.
- **Law Amendment Committee**: The Law Amendment Committee was submitted to the Council of Assiniboia by Recorder Thorn in May.
- **Battle at the Grand Coteau**: The Battle at the Grand Coteau occurred in July.

This timeline reflects a period of substantial transformation at Red River, characterized by shifts in governance, religious developments, challenges to established economic structures, and increasing interactions with the external world. The detailed notes from council meetings and court sessions provide a glimpse into the day-to-day administration and legal proceedings.

Here is a timeline of events involving the Métis, Red River, and the Hudson's Bay Company (HBC) between 1839 and 1851, drawing upon the provided sources and our conversation history.

- **1830s-1840s: Métis Grievances and Assertion of Rights:** Disputes arose between the HBC and the Métis over the Métis right to free trade as early as the mid-1830s. The Métis title to their land was not recognized, and they did not have a voice in government.
    
- **1839:** George Simpson, the Governor of the North, was in charge of the HBC.
    
- **1844:** Alexander Christie returned to replace Finlayson.
    
- **February 1846:** Meetings took place at Andrew McDermot's house in the Red River Settlement where a petition was drafted in French, outlining the claims of settlers, Indians, and Métis. This petition, bearing 977 signatures, was sent to London, leading to an extensive inquiry into the HBC's position.
    
- **1848:** According to "London Correspondence Inward From Eden Colvile, 1849-1852," A. Christie wrote to Simpson on January 31, indicating ongoing communication and likely coordination between key HBC figures.
    
- **1849: The Sayer Trial and Free Trade:** The trial of Guillaume Sayer occurred in May. French Canadians and half-breeds gathered, with armed men moving to and fro. The population expressed discontent, and an element of popular power was introduced to the colonies' mixed constitution.
    
- **1849: A Shift in Leadership:** Eden Colvile was made Governor of Rupert’s Land on January 3. In June, English settlers expressed discontent with Caldwell in a petition presented to Simpson.
    
- **1850-1851: Freedom of Trade and Shifting Power Dynamics:** The source "London Correspondence Inward From Eden Colvile, 1849-1852," alludes to the Company accepting freedom of trade and a settled population at Red River as established facts. The source, "Law, Life, and Government at Red River, Volume 2_ General Quarterly Court of Assiniboia, Annotated Records, 1844-1872," says that the HBC and the Assiniboia government emerged relatively unscathed from the ordeal of the 1857 enquiry. This indicates an ongoing negotiation between HBC authority and the growing demands of the Red River population.
    

This timeline illustrates the evolving relationship between the Métis, the Red River Settlement, and the HBC during a period marked by increasing Métis activism, challenges to the Company's authority, and the beginnings of political and social change within the Red River community.
