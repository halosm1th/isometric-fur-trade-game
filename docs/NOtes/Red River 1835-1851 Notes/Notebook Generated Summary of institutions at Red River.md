The year 1849 can be described as a turning point in the Red River Settlement because of events that stirred agitation for free trade and brought about significant changes in the power dynamics between the Hudson's Bay Company (HBC), the Métis, and private traders. While "democracy" in the modern sense may not have fully arrived in 1849, the events of that year laid the groundwork for increased self-determination and challenged the HBC's authority.

Several factors and events contributed to this shift:

- **The Sayer Trial:** The trial of Pierre Sayer in May 1849, for illicit traffic in furs, became a focal point for challenging the HBC's trade monopoly. The Métis rallied in support of Sayer, demonstrating their discontent with the Company's control over the fur trade. Although Sayer was found guilty, the recommendation for mercy by the jury was seen as a victory for the Métis and a blow to the HBC's authority.
- **Freedom of Trade:** The Sayer trial is significant because the Company was virtually accepting the right of the half-breeds to trade in furs and was accepting them as competitors in pursuit of the Indians and their furs.
- **Rise of Private Traders**: The emergence of private traders and their challenge to the HBC's monopoly contributed to the changing dynamics. These traders sought new economic opportunities and agitated for a freer market.
- **Influence of Norman Kittson**: The arrival of Norman Kittson, an American trader, at Pembina, just south of the Red River Settlement, provided an alternative market and supply source for the colonists, further weakening the HBC's economic grip.
- **Métis Identity and Agency**: The events of 1849 highlighted the growing sense of Métis identity and their willingness to assert their rights. The Métis sought greater inclusion in the colony's governance and a recognition of their distinct needs and interests.

While these events marked a shift towards greater self-determination and challenged the HBC's authority, it is important to note that the Red River Settlement in 1849 still fell short of modern democratic standards.

- The Council of Assiniboia, the local government, remained dominated by HBC appointees and lacked representation of the Métis population.
- The concept of universal suffrage was still unknown.
- The HBC retained significant control over the settlement's economic and political life.

Nevertheless, 1849 was a pivotal year in the Red River Settlement's journey toward greater autonomy and democratic participation. The events of that year laid bare the tensions between the HBC's traditional authority and the growing desire for self-determination among the settlement's diverse population. The agitation for free trade, the assertion of Métis identity, and the challenge to the HBC's monopoly all contributed to a shift in the balance of power and paved the way for future political and social reforms.
