**Chapter notes**

- **I. The Core Narrative: The "Ambivalent" Empire**
    - **Definition:** A French empire that grew "in fits and starts," guided by imperfect information, fragmented by local interests, and often neglected by metropolitan administrators.
    - **The "Crucible":** Métis identity was forged in the tension between localized Indigenous sovereignty and these erratic French imperial ambitions.
    - **Outcome:** The emergence of a new Indigenous culture, language, and nation—the Métis—was an _unintended_ consequence of French-Indigenous trade and diplomacy.
- **II. Key Historical Figure: François Jérôme dit Latour**
    - Used as a "vignette" to illustrate _métissage_ (cultural hybridity).
    - **The HBC Perspective:** British traders like William Tomison and Matthew Cocking dismissed him as a "poor looking small man" or an "old ignorant Frenchman."
    - **The Reality:** Jérôme was a highly successful independent trader with deep roots. He was married to a Cree woman, spoke the language, and commanded a large flotilla. He was a "backcountry specialist" who operated independently of elite control.
- **III. Defining the "Canadiens"**: The author makes a sharp distinction between two classes in New France:
    1. **Colonial Elites:** Governors, officers, and seigneurs (approx. 1% of the population). They looked toward the Atlantic and Versailles for prestige and credit.
    2. **Canadiens:** Non-elite settlers (voyageurs, _coureurs de bois_, farmers, artisans). They looked _west_ for opportunity. They had no attachment to metropolitan France and stayed in North America after the 1760 British conquest.
- **IV. Theoretical Frameworks**: The author uses several academic concepts to explain why the French state could not control the Northwest:
    - **James C. Scott & "Legibility":** The state’s attempt to simplify and rationalize a complex reality (often through maps) to exploit wealth.
    - **"Imperial Overstretch":** Information overload at the Bureau des Colonies that prevented ministers from making effective decisions.
    - **"Friction of Terrain":** The vast, difficult geography of the watershed allowed "non-state peoples" (Canadiens and Indigenous groups) to evade state control.
    - **Richard White’s "Middle Ground":** A shared cultural space based on power balance and "creative misunderstanding."
    - **Author’s Critique:** Berthelette joins "post-middle ground" scholars, arguing the watershed was primarily an **Indigenous political space** where kinship—not monarchs—represented the true structure of power.
- **V. Métissage and the Rise of the Métis**
    - **Métissage:** A creative process of cultural and ethnic mixing (clothing, religion, mode of warfare).
    - **Challenging "Red River Myopia":** Traditional history focuses on the Red River Valley in the 19th century as the "cradle" of the Métis. Berthelette argues their roots extend much further back into the 18th-century Hudson Bay watershed.
    - **Ethnogenesis:** The process of a group of people becoming a distinct ethnic nation.
- **VII. Geography: The Four Regions**
    1. **Hudson Bay Lowlands:** Vast wetlands near the bay.
    2. **Canadian Shield:** Rocky, boreal forest landscape; home to fur-bearing mammals.
    3. **Aspen Parkland:** A "transitional biome" between forest and grassland; rich in aquatic life and bison.
    4. **Northern Great Plains:** The "sea of grasses" where bison fueled the pemmican trade.

- **I. Context: The Reorganization of New France (1663)**
    - **The Iroquois Wars:** Mid-17th-century conflicts with the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) fractured Indigenous societies (e.g., the Wendat dispersal in 1649) and left the French colony vulnerable.
    - **Royal Intervention:** In 1663, Louis XIV and his minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert rescinded the charter of the Compagnie des Cent-Associés and made New France a royal colony to prevent its collapse.
    - **New Institutions:** Established a governor general, an intendant (civil/legal official), a bishop, and the Sovereign Council.
    - **Military Arrival:** 1,200 men of the Carignan-Salières Regiment arrived in 1665 to secure the colony.
- **II. The Governance Dilemma: Rank vs. Ability**
    - **The Elite Bias:** The new structure favored the noblesse d’épée (nobility of the sword) and noblesse de robe (nobility of the gown). Access to the fur trade was regulated via congés (licenses) mostly granted to the military elite.
    - **Backcountry Specialists:** Despite legal restrictions (like the 1681 edict forbidding trading in Native villages), "ordinary" Frenchmen—voyageurs and coureurs de bois—became indispensable.
    - **The Conflict:** The French Crown feared these "itinerant traders" would cause a moral and economic collapse, yet relied on their skills in language, diplomacy, and exploration to maintain an imperial foothold.
- **III. The Society of Jesus (Jesuits) as "Apostles of Empire"**
    - **Roles:** Jesuits acted as colonial lobbyists, military advisers, interpreters, go-betweens, and spies.
    - **Conversion as Policy:** Religious conversion was a tool for extending French sovereignty. Native groups often viewed baptism as a "sacred pledge of friendship" to secure trade, particularly for iron and firearms.
    - **Information Management:** Jesuits contributed to Colbert’s "state intelligence system," sending empirical "relations" of their travels back to Paris and Rome.
    - **The "Sault Sainte-Marie Massacre" (1674):** Crees sabotaged a peace conference between the Ojibwe and Dakota to protect their trade position. The massacre highlighted the limits of Jesuit authority in the interior.
- **IV. Key "Rogue" Agents of Empire**: The chapter highlights three case studies of men who bridged the gap between French and Indigenous worlds:
    - They became adopted sons integrating into indigenous kinship networks, while becoming clients of powerful patrons, travelling overseas to visit the court and vie for promotions and patronage.
    - **1. Pierre-Esprit Radisson & Médard Chouart des Groseilliers**
        - **Achievements:** Integrated into Indigenous society (the 1659–60 Feast of the Dead); learned to use kinship and "the discourse of pity" to form alliances.
        - **The Defection:** After being arrested/fined by Governor d’Argenson for illegal trading, they defected to England.
        - **Legacy:** Their knowledge of the Northwest led to the founding of the **Hudson’s Bay Company (HBC)** in 1670. They later returned to French service briefly with the Compagnie du Nord.
    - **2. Daniel Greysolon Du Lhut**
        - **Status:** A petty nobleman and "non-traditional" coureur de bois.
        - **Diplomacy:** Acted as a peacemaker between the Dakota and Great Lakes allies (1679). He helped coordinate Indigenous armies to support French military campaigns.
        - **Economic Strategy:** Established posts at Lake Nipigon (e.g., Fort La Tourette) to intercept trade bound for the HBC. He often exaggerated his success to appease his patrons in France.
    - **3. Nicolas Jérémie dit Lamontagne**
        - **Role:** An interpreter and clerk who rose to become Governor of Fort Bourbon.
        - **Kinship Ties:** His success was rooted in his understanding of Algonquian culture, aided by his (annulled) marriage to an Innu woman, Marie-Madeleine Tetaouiskoué.
        - **Value to the State:** He provided essential intelligence on the economic potential of the Hudson Bay region (whaling, minerals, muskox wool).
- **V. Indigenous Sovereignty and Kinship**
    - **Kinship as Power:** In the Hudson Bay watershed, family ties—not monarchs—were the primary social structure. The French only exercised influence by being "incorporated" into these systems.
    - **The Protocol of Generosity:** Trade was an expression of kinship. A failure to be generous (e.g., the caribou hunters in 1712 who refused to share with starving Crees) resulted in violent reprisals and the severing of alliances.
- **VI. Conclusion: The Treaty of Utrecht (1713)**
    - **Imperial Setback:** The treaty ended the War of the Spanish Succession and forced France to surrender its claims to Hudson Bay, Newfoundland, and Acadia to England.
    - **Surrender of Fort Bourbon:** On September 5, 1714, the French garrison surrendered the fort to the HBC (Captain James Knight).
    - **Summary Lesson:** The period showed that while roguish "backcountry specialists" could build an on-the-ground empire through kinship and local knowledge, the central state in Versailles could undo decades of progress in an instant for the sake of European peace.

## Chapter 2

- **I. Political Shift: The Regency and the "Long Peace"**
    - **The Treaty of Utrecht (1713):** A "humiliating defeat" for Louis XIV. France lost claims to Hudson Bay, Newfoundland, and peninsular Acadia.
    - **The Regency (1715–1723):** After Louis XIV’s death, the Duke of Orléans became Regent. He moved the court to the Palais-Royal in Paris and established the **Conseil de Régence**, which decentralized power into various councils.
    - **The "Long Peace" (1713–1740):** A period of recovery for both Britain and France. France focused on consolidating Louisiana and expanding the fur trade beyond the Great Lakes (pays d’en haut) without provoking war with Britain.
- **II. The Information State and "Legibility"**
    - **The "Reporting Hierarchy":** A system where information became the currency of the ancien régime. Administrators, missionaries, and "backcountry specialists" traded intelligence for promotions and honors (p. 71).
    - **The Goal:** To make western North America "legible" (rationalized and simplified) for the state to extract wealth.
    - **"Imperial Overstretch":** Despite the flow of maps and reports, French ministers were often overwhelmed by "information overload," preventing them from using the knowledge effectively (p. 9).
- **III. The Obsession: The Western Sea (Mer de l’Ouest)**
    - **The Theory:** French geographers (the **Delisle family**) propagated the existence of an inland sea flowing into the Pacific. This was an "imperial fiction" that drove French exploration westward (p. 76, 99).
    - **Key Actors:**
        - **Guillaume Delisle:** A "cabinet geographer" who mapped North America from Paris based on diaries and reports, rarely leaving his office.
        - **Abbé Jean Bobé:** A "broker of information" who connected geographers with missionaries and traders to substantiate the Western Sea theory.
        - **Governor Vaudreuil:** Promoted a northern route to the sea to keep the project within his jurisdiction of Canada rather than Louisiana.
- **IV. Backcountry Specialists: The Source of Intelligence**
- The French state relied on men at the lower rungs of society for geographic and ethnographic data:
    - **Jacques de Noyon:** In 1688, he reached Rainy Lake. His interviews with Intendant Bégon in 1715 provided the first detailed routes for the westward push.
    - **Father Pierre-François-Xavier de Charlevoix:** Dispatched by the state to investigate rumors of the sea. He interviewed voyageurs and coureurs de bois, concluding that the sea was likely between 40° and 50° latitude.
    - **Jean Viennay-Pachot:** A veteran coureur de bois praised as the most knowledgeable man in Canada regarding Indigenous languages. He provided Charlevoix with physical evidence (seashells) of the Western Sea.
- **V. "Rogue Colonialism" and the Limits of Empire**
    - **Definition:** The influence of individuals on the ground who pushed colonial frontiers in their own self-interest, often ignoring official policy (p. 77).
    - **Ambivalent Agents:** Voyageurs and coureurs de bois occupied a liminal space between "imperial service and imperial subversion."
    - **Example: The Dakota Alliance.** While state-led expeditions like that of **La Noue (1717)** failed to establish a peace between the Ojibwe-Cree and the Dakota, "rogue" coureurs de bois succeeded.
    - **Success through Kinship:** Coureurs de bois achieved an alliance with the Dakota through **marriage bonds** and adoption into thióšpayes (extended family groups). They lived and fought alongside the Dakota, refusing to return to the "civilized" Saint Lawrence colony (p. 116).
- **VI. Conclusion: The "Unintended" Empire**
    - By the 1720s, the French presence in the Hudson Bay watershed was a "patchwork" of unofficial trade and kinship ties.
    - **The Conflict:** The interests of coureurs de bois (who wanted to trade and raid on their own terms) often clashed with Vaudreuil’s "Pax Gallica" (French-mediated peace).
    - **Summary:** Despite official failures to find the Western Sea or enforce a monopoly, these roguish Canadiens ensured that French influence permeated the western interior through the most powerful infrastructure available: **Indigenous kinship networks.**

## Chapter 3

- **I. A Radical Shift in Governance: Elite Hierarchy**
    - **Post-Vaudreuil Era:** Following Governor Vaudreuil's death in 1725, the new Governor **Beauharnois** (1726–1748) moved away from Vaudreuil's pragmatic policy of co-opting "backcountry specialists."
    - **The Elite Bias:** Beauharnois adhered strictly to the ancien régime hierarchy. He favored French elites and noble officers over experienced but lower-class Canadiens.
    - **The Commission:** In 1729, he commissioned **Pierre Gaultier de Varennes, sieur de La Vérendrye**—a nobleman and military officer—to establish the "Western Posts" and find the Western Sea.
- **II. Intelligence and the "Birchbark Map"**
    - **Auchagah’s Map:** While commanding a post on Lake Superior, La Vérendrye met a Cree chief named **Auchagah**, who drew a charcoal map on birchbark showing the waterways to the West.
    - **Institutionalization of Knowledge:** This map was copied by military engineers and sent to Paris, where it was incorporated into the official French geographic "fantasy" of the Northwest Passage (p. 106).
    - **Naive Leadership:** Despite having this map, La Vérendrye was "naive and untested" in western diplomacy. Beauharnois chose him for his noble rank, ignoring more qualified men like Nicolas Jérémie or Jacques de Noyon.
- **III. The Reality of the "Native Ground"**
    - **The War Zone:** La Vérendrye entered a region defined by a long-standing war between two powerful Indigenous coalitions:
        1. **The Northern Coalition:** Cree, Monsoni, and Nakoda (Assiniboine).
        2. **The Southern Coalition:** Očhéthi Šakówiŋ (Dakota) and the Zhaagawaamikong-Ojibwe.
    - **The 1731 Mutiny:** Upon reaching Grand Portage, La Vérendrye’s engagés (hired laborers) mutinied. They were terrified of the "strenuous portage" and the risks of entering a war zone (p. 111).
- **IV. "Implicating the French": Indigenous Agency**
- Indigenous leaders strategically manipulated La Vérendrye to serve their own military goals:
    - **The 1734 Ruse:** Cree and Monsoni leaders pressured La Vérendrye to send his eldest son, **Jean-Baptiste**, on a raid against the Dakota.
    - **The Trap:** During the battle, the Cree/Monsoni warriors shouted, **"It is the Frenchman!"** at the Dakota. This was a calculated move to force the Dakota to view the French as enemies and sever their trade ties with them (p. 118).
    - **The Result:** The French were now "entangled" as co-belligerents in an Indigenous war they did not fully understand.
- **V. The Lake of the Woods Raid (1736)**
    - **The Massacre:** In retaliation for French support of the Cree, a Dakota war party intercepted Jean-Baptiste’s fur brigade on a small island (Massacre Island).
    - **Casualties:** They killed and beheaded Jean-Baptiste, a Jesuit missionary (Father Aulneau), and 19 voyageurs.
    - **The Message:** The Dakota wrapped the decapitated heads in beaver robes to mock the "French love for beaver" and punish them for meddling in regional politics (p. 121).
- **VI. Debt and the Indigenous Slave Trade**
    - **Financial Failure:** La Vérendrye was a "war profiteer" and a "lackluster explorer." He amassed a massive debt of **40,000 livres** (p. 127).
    - **Profit Over Peace:** To pay his debts, La Vérendrye ignored Beauharnois’s orders to seek peace (Pax Gallica). Instead, he voraciously traded **Dakota slaves** captured by his Cree/Monsoni allies.
    - **The Sabotage of Diplomacy:** His participation in the slave trade made it impossible to broker a peace with the Dakota, who (rightly) viewed the La Vérendrye family as their enemies.
- **VII. Conclusion: The "Imperial Setback"**
    - **Failure of the Elites:** Berthelette argues that French elites failed as builders of empire in the watershed. They tried to impose the ancien régime order on a space where they actually had no control.
    - **Status vs. Reality:** La Vérendrye’s "frequent bungling" and focus on the slave trade (a "sea of beaver" instead of a "Western Sea") demonstrated the weakness of French imperial control in the 1730s and '40s.
    - **Indigenous Sovereignty:** The period proved that Indigenous peoples were the true masters of the region, using the French as "pawns" in their own geopolitical struggles.

## **Chapter 4: Canadiens at the Western Posts, 1731–1743**.

### **I. Overview: Ability vs. Nobility**

- **Context:** While the previous chapter focused on the failures of the elite Pierre Gaultier de La Vérendrye, Chapter 4 highlights the "aptitude and agency" of ordinary _Canadiens_ (voyageurs and _coureurs de bois_).
- **The Conflict:** Governor Beauharnois initially tried to restore authority to military elites and military officers, eschewing _Canadiens_. However, crises forced him to rely on the _Canadiens'_ superior knowledge of Indigenous languages, customs, and terrain.
- **Key Argument:** Despite being excluded from the patronage networks of the _ancien régime_, ordinary _Canadiens_ were the actual backbone of the French presence in the West.

### **II. Cultural Intermediaries: René Bourassa**

- **Survival at Lake of the Woods:** In 1736, René Bourassa _dit_ La Ronde survived a Dakota attack that killed 21 others. He was saved by the intercession of his female Dakota slave, who redirected the warriors toward a "worthier target"—Jean-Baptiste de La Vérendrye.
- **Pragmatism in Diplomacy:** Bourassa, a notorious former _coureur de bois_ and smuggler, provided intelligence to Beauharnois. He successfully counseled the governor to disengage the French from the Cree-Dakota conflict, a pragmatic move that the "uptight" military elite struggled to execute.

### **III. Language and Diplomacy**

- **Linguistic Immersion:** _Canadiens_ acted as essential interpreters.
    - **Louis Galloudek:** At age 16, he was left with the Mandan for six months to learn their language (_Nų́ų́ʔetaa íroo_).
    - **Pennesha Gegare:** A deserter from the Illinois Country who lived among the Mandan and integrated into their kinship networks.
- **Diplomatic Protocol:** _Canadiens_ mastered Indigenous metaphorical discourse (e.g., "boiling the kettle" for warfare) and the use of **wampum belts/cords** as mnemonic devices for contracts and treaties.
- **"Native Style" of Speech:** Success depended on adopting an Indigenous style of rhetoric—full of hyperboles and allusions—to gain legitimacy within local social and commercial networks.

### **IV. The Search for Mineral Wealth**

- **Imperial Obsession:** The French state was driven by "dreaming about mineral wealth" (gold, silver, copper).
- **Rumor vs. Reality:** Voyageurs were sent as ambassadors to scout for mines.
    - In 1734, voyageurs returned from Lake Winnipeg with ore specimens.
    - Later analysis suggested the "silver" was likely hematite or red iron, common and not profitable for transport.
- **The Power of Misinformation:** Even false rumors provided by Indigenous hosts and reported by _Canadiens_ (like the "Mountain of sparkling stone") drove imperial imagination and justified continued westward expansion.

### **V. Provisioning the Western Posts**

- **Indigenous Survival Skills:** Contrary to fears of starvation, _Canadiens_ were proficient hunters and fishermen. They learned Indigenous techniques for catching sturgeon and curing meat without salt (using smoke or sunlight).
- **Labor Contracts:** Voyageur contracts often included a specific clause requiring the employee to "hunt and fish, if there is need."
- **Reciprocity:** Being able to provision their own posts allowed the French to fulfill the social obligations of "the pot"—feasting Indigenous visitors to maintain alliances.

### **VI. Labor and Agency of the Voyageurs**

- **Role Hierarchy:**
    - **Milieu (Middleman):** Least prestigious; responsible for paddling and carrying bales.
    - **Devant (Foreman) & Gouvernail (Steersman):** Higher-paid skilled roles responsible for navigating and setting the rhythm.
    - **Guide:** The most senior role; made critical decisions about rapids and portages.
- **Economic Motivation:** Voyageur salaries (150–1,000 _livres_) were 2–3 times higher than wages in the Saint Lawrence valley.
- **Resistance and "Side-Trading":**
    - Voyageurs frequently engaged in illicit "side-trading" for personal profit, undercutting official merchants.
    - **Agency:** Voyageurs used desertion, working slowly, and theft as bargaining tools to challenge the expectations of their masters.

### **VII. Conclusion: Heirs of an Ambivalent Empire**

- **Adapting to the "Native Ground":** _Canadiens_ were more relaxed and tolerant than military officers, often marrying _à la façon du pays_ (in the custom of the country) and adhering to local norms.
- **The Foundation of a New World:** While the elite searched for a "Western Sea" that didn't exist, the _Canadiens_ and their Indigenous families were busy creating a "culturally hybrid French-Indigenous social world" that would eventually lead to the rise of the Métis Nation.

## Chapter 5

### **I. Context: The Collapse of the "Golden Age"**

- **The Turning Point:** The War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) ended the "Long Peace" and "Golden Age" of New France.
- **Imperial Decline:** British naval blockades disrupted the flow of trade goods, making it impossible for the French state to maintain "gift diplomacy" and the official _Pax Gallica_ (French-mediated peace).
- **The Power Vacuum:** As official Troupes de la Marine officers withdrew or became ineffective, a "Canadien ascendancy" took place, where ordinary traders established a social world independent of the colonial government.

### **II. The Failure of Elite Officers (Post-La Vérendrye)**

- **Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre:** Commandant (1750–1753) who prioritized self-enrichment through the liquor and slave trades.
    - **Lack of Credibility:** Unlike _Canadiens_, he lacked a reputation as a warrior or diplomat among the Cree and Nakoda.
    - **Staged Attack (1752):** 200 Nakoda warriors "overwhelmed" Fort La Reine. Though Saint-Pierre claimed he saved the fort with a firebrand over the powder magazine, it was likely a staged "cultural fiction" by the Nakoda to humiliate him for his un-kin-like behavior.
- **Niverville at Fort Paskoya:** Suffered from malnutrition and illness, highlighting the dependency of French elites on Indigenous and _Canadien_ support for survival.

### **III. The Rise of the _Gens Libres_ (Freemen)**

- **Autonomy:** Between 1746 and 1750, the Western Posts lacked official commandants, allowing _voyageurs_ to move from contractual employment to total autonomy.
- **Precursors to the Métis:** These independent traders (estimated at 200 by 1759) lived semi-permanently in the watershed, forming the core of a new French-Indigenous community.
- **Challenging the HBC:** These traders moved "en dérouine" (traveling directly to Indigenous camps) to intercept furs, causing a significant drop in HBC trade returns at York Factory and Fort Albany.

### **IV. Processes of _Métissage_ (Cultural Hybridity)**

- **Sartorial (Clothing) Hybridity:**
    - **"Cultural Cross-Dressing":** _Canadiens_ adopted Indigenous clothing for practicality—oversized linen shirts, breechcloths (_brayet_), mitasses (gaiters), and moccasins.
    - **Symbolism:** Clothing allowed individuals to navigate different cultural spaces (e.g., Louis Primeau donning European clothes to re-enter a "European space" at York Factory).
- **Gastronomy and "Fusion Cuisine":**
    - Diets blended European staples (brandy, biscuits) with Indigenous foods (pemmican, bison meat, wild rice, sturgeon).
    - **Religious Adaptation:** The Pope classified beaver as "fish" due to its aquatic nature, allowing _Canadiens_ to eat it during Lent.
- **Religious Syncretism ("Lived Catholicism"):**
    - In the absence of priests, _Canadiens_ performed their own baptisms, marriages, and burials.
    - **Sacred Geography:** They respected Indigenous _manidoog_ (spirits) at sites like Oiseau Rock and Mackinac Island, blending Catholic prayers with Indigenous offerings (tobacco).

### **V. Kinship and Marriage _à la façon du pays_**

- **Marital Unions:** Unions between _Canadien_ men and Indigenous women were _bona fide_ marital bonds that created essential "tender ties" and commercial alliances.
- **Indigenous Sovereignty:** Local Indigenous peoples set the guidelines for these interactions; the French were _incorporated_ into Indigenous social structures rather than the other way around.
- **The "Proto-Generation":** These unions produced children who took their fathers' last names but lived in their mothers' homelands, creating the demographic foundation for the future Métis Nation.

### **VI. Case Study: François Jérôme _dit_ Latour**

- **The Quintessential Intermediary:** Jérôme (known as "Saswee" to the Cree) straddled both worlds. He was a successful merchant who had been in the watershed since 1743.
- **Reputation:** He was noted by HBC observers for his "great influence" and for living like an "Indianized Frenchman."
- **Hospitality:** Even when France and Britain were at war, Jérôme extended hospitality to HBC servants (like Smith and Waggoner), proving that interdependence was the real power in "Indian Country."

### **VII. Conclusion: The Heirs of an Ambivalent Empire**

- **A New Legacy:** While the French state failed in its "mad dream" (_rêve fou_) of finding the Western Sea, it unknowingly set in motion the ethnogenesis of the Métis.
- **Durability:** By the 1750s, the French-Indigenous social world was no longer dependent on the French Empire for its existence. It had become a cohesive, self-sufficient culture rooted in the landscape of the Northwest.

---

### **I. Overview: The Myth of the Vanishing Frenchman**

- **The Scholarly Shift:** Traditional history suggested that the French presence in the Northwest vanished after the Seven Years' War (1754–1763). Berthelette argues that while the _military elite_ withdrew to France or Quebec, the _voyageurs_ and _coureurs de bois_ remained.
- **A Salient Legacy:** The failed imperial search for the "Western Sea" left behind a human infrastructure. The _Canadiens_ remained embedded in Indigenous kinship networks, creating a "dynamic French-Indigenous social world" that did not depend on the French Empire for survival.

### **II. Continuity in the Post-Conquest Era (1760–1770s)**

- **The Arrival of "Pedlars":** When British and Scottish traders (e.g., Alexander Henry the Elder, James Finlay) arrived from Montreal after 1767, they found well-established French-Indigenous communities already in place.
- **The Necessity of Canadiens:** British newcomers had no knowledge of the interior waterways. They were forced to employ _Canadiens_ as guides, interpreters, and "backcountry specialists."
- **The Langlade Family Example:** Based in Michilimackinac, the Langlades (who were French-Odawa) successfully navigated the transition to British rule by leveraging their Anishinaabe kin networks to trade in safety.

### **III. Case Study: François Jérôme _dit_ Latour ("Saswee")**

- **Career Longevity:** Jérôme was active in the watershed from 1743 through the 1770s.
- **Post-Conquest Success:** He partnered with British merchants like James Finlay to establish the **François-Finlay Fort** (near Nipawin, Saskatchewan).
- **Kinship Recruitment:** Jérôme recruited his nephew Pierre from their home parish of La Prairie, showing how the fur trade was a kinship-based enterprise that bridged the St. Lawrence Valley and the Northwest.
- **The "Indianized Frenchman":** HBC officials (like Thomas Hutchins) noted that the success of _Canadiens_ like Jérôme was due to their ability to live like Indigenous people—adopting their customs, language, and egalitarianism.

### **IV. Case Study: Louis Primeau ("Nick’a’thu’tin")**

- **Cultural Fluidity:** Primeau lived with his Cree family for six years before seeking employment with the HBC in 1766.
- **The HBC’s Dilemma:** The HBC valued his "mastery of the Indian language" but feared his independence. He eventually deserted the HBC in 1773 to return to the Montreal "pedlars."
- **Geographic Recognition:** His influence was so great that "Primo's Lake" was named after him on official maps by Philip Turnor.

### **V. The Smallpox Epidemic (1780–1782) and Ethnogenesis**

- **A Demographic Pivot:** The epidemic (originating in Mexico) wiped out one-half to two-thirds of some Cree and Nakoda bands (e.g., the "Basquia Indians").
- **The Power Vacuum:** The massive population decline of Indigenous groups left a vacuum in the Saskatchewan River trade corridor.
- **Creating the Métis Nation:** The epidemic helped create the necessary conditions for the rise of the Métis. _Canadien_ traders and their bicultural offspring (the "proto-generation") became the dominant players in the local trade and established permanent communities like **Île-à-la-Crosse**.

### **VI. Rethinking Métis History**

- **Red River Myopia:** Berthelette challenges the idea that the Red River Valley was the _only_ site of Métis emergence. He argues the Saskatchewan River valley was a primary "road" to Métis peoplehood.
- **Political Self-Consciousness:** While biological "mixing" (_métissage_) had occurred for a century, the late 18th century saw these groups begin to assert a distinct political and collective identity—the **Métis Nation**.
- **Role of Women:** Indigenous women were the "centripetal force" that incorporated outsider males into regional worldviews rooted in family obligations and relatedness.

### **VII. Conclusion: Heirs of an Ambivalent Empire**

- **The Acts of Creation:** The Métis were the unintended outcome of French imperial ambitions.
- **The Crucible:** The tension between localized Indigenous sovereignty and French imperial projects formed the "crucible" in which Métis identity was forged.
- **Final Transition:** By 1825 (as seen in the Epilogue), families like the Jérômes had moved to the Red River Settlement, bringing with them a multilingual, bison-hunting culture that would define the Métis Nation in the 19th century.

## Epilogue

### **I. The Jérôme Family: A Case Study in Intergenerational Vocation**

- **The Patriarch (Pierre Jérôme):** The epilogue opens in 1821 at Fort Carlton, Saskatchewan, with the death of Pierre Jérôme (nephew of François Jérôme _dit_ Latour). He was over 80 years old and had been a seasoned interpreter for the North West Company (NWC).
- **Continuity:** Pierre arrived in the watershed in 1769. His son, **Martin (père)**, and grandson, **Martin (fils)**, both followed him into the fur trade as interpreters and guides.
- **Loss of Ties to the East:** By the third generation (Martin fils), the family had no remaining ties to the Saint Lawrence valley. They were born in the West, lived in the West, and were referred to by HBC officers as "Native of Fort des Prairie" or "half Breed."

### **II. The Nature of the "Ambivalent Empire"**

- **Metropole vs. Frontier:** The French presence was an "ambivalent empire" defined by the tension between:
    1. **Idealized Projects:** The "mad dreams" (_rêve fou_) of ministers in Paris and Versailles (finding the Western Sea, mineral wealth, and the Northwest Passage).
    2. **Local Realities:** The personal interests, kinship ties, and survival strategies of _Canadiens_ and Indigenous peoples on the ground.
- **Failure of the Elite:** Noble officers like La Vérendrye often failed because they tried to impose the rigid, hierarchical protocols of the _ancien régime_ on a region they did not understand.
- **Success of the _Canadiens_:** Ordinary _voyageurs_ and _coureurs de bois_ were the true "holders of the keys to colonialism." They succeeded not by conquering, but by integrating themselves into the Indigenous social world.

### **III. Rejection of the "Middle Ground"**

- **Native Ground:** The author argues that the Hudson Bay watershed was never a "middle ground" (as defined by Richard White).
    - A "middle ground" requires an equal balance of power and a mutual desire for accommodation.
    - In the watershed, the French were almost always at the mercy of their Anishinaabe, Cree, Nakoda, and Dakota partners.
    - Instead of accommodation, there was **incorporation**: the French were absorbed into female-centred Indigenous kinship networks.

### **IV. The Birth of the Métis Nation**

- **The Crucible:** The 18th-century interactions between _Canadien_ men and Indigenous women acted as a crucible.
    - The "proto-generation" of children took their fathers' names but lived in their mothers' homelands.
    - These families eventually intermarried with one another, creating distinct communities separate from both European and First Nations societies.
- **The Hammer and Anvil:** If the 18th century was the "crucible" that created the people, the 19th century provided the "hammer and anvil" that forged **national consciousness** through external threats:
    - The Selkirk Settlement.
    - The Hudson’s Bay Company monopoly.
    - The expansion of the Dominion of Canada.
- **Migration to Red River:** In 1825, the Jérôme and Letendre clans moved to the Red River Colony. This mirrored a larger pattern where Métis families moved from the fur trade corridors of the Saskatchewan River to Red River to exploit the economic niche of the **buffalo hunt and pemmican trade**.

### **V. Final Conclusion**

- **The Unintended Legacy:** The French ministers and explorers failed in every one of their stated imperial goals (no Western Sea, no gold, no Northwest Passage, no total trade monopoly).
- **The Enduring Impact:** Their true, permanent legacy was the unintended creation of a new Indigenous culture, language, and nation—the **Métis**, who remain the "heirs of an ambivalent empire."

## Apendix

Based on the provided text (pages 252–255), here are the notes for the **Appendix: The Hudson’s Bay Company and the Anglo-Métis**.

---

### **I. Defining the Anglo-Métis Identity**

- **Terminology:** Also referred to as "half-breed" or "country-born."
- **Cultural Markers:** Unlike the Catholic, Michif-speaking _Bois-Brûlés_ (French Métis), the Anglo-Métis were primarily:
    - Protestant.
    - English or Bungi-speaking.
    - Settled in the Protestant parishes of the Red River (St. Andrews and St. Clements).
- **Livelihood:** While they participated in the buffalo hunt, they were more likely to be farmers and merchants.

### **II. Early HBC Policy: The "Monastic" Era (1670–1770)**

- **Official Celibacy:** The London Committee initially demanded celibacy from its servants and strictly forbade fraternization with Native women.
- **The Elite Exception:** Despite the rules, heads of posts (governors and chief factors) like **James Isham** and **Moses Norton** often took Indigenous wives.
- **Education and Assimilation:** Prior to 1770, "mixed-blood" sons were often sent to England for schooling before returning to work for the HBC. Daughters were usually assimilated into their mothers' Cree families. Consequently, these children were not yet seen as a distinct ethnic group.

### **III. The Inland Shift (Late 1770s)**

- **Trading _En Dérouine_:** As the HBC moved inland to compete with Montreal traders, servants began visiting Indigenous camps directly.
- **Large-Scale Intermarriage:** This move led to widespread intermarriage between lower-ranking HBC servants and Indigenous women, creating deeper social and kin relations.
- **Informal Workforce:** The children of these unions lived primarily among Indigenous bands and worked informally as provisioners for the HBC.

### **IV. The Role of Native-Born Daughters (c. 1800)**

- **Abandoning Celibacy:** By 1800, the HBC officially dropped its non-fraternization policy due to a massive need for labor.
- **Desirable Partners:** Native-born daughters became preferred wives for incoming HBC apprentices.
    - **Skills:** They possessed essential survival and fur-trade skills (preparing furs, making snowshoes) learned from their mothers.
    - **Patronage:** These marriages linked new apprentices with established company veterans through "mutual ties of sentiment."

### **V. The Turning Point: The 1821 Merger**

- **Simpson's Reforms:** After the HBC and NWC merged in 1821, Governor George Simpson shuttered "superfluous" posts and laid off over half the workforce.
- **Racial Stratification:** The remaining hierarchy became more stratified and racially exclusionary, making it harder for "country-born" laborers to advance.
- **Displacement to Red River:** Lacking employment, many mixed-descent families moved to the Red River Settlement to seek land, housing, and education.

### **VI. Unity within the Métis Nation**

- **Challenging the Conflict Narrative:** Berthelette disputes the idea (proposed by historians like Frits Pannekoek) that there was deep-seated hostility between Catholic and Protestant Métis.
- **Co-operation:** He points to shared activities that bridged the cultural gulf:
    - The annual buffalo hunt.
    - Overland freighting expeditions (Red River carts).
    - York boat brigades.
    - Intermarriage between the two groups.
- **Political Solidarity:** Anglo-Métis joined the _Bois-Brûlés_ in crucial political struggles, including the **Sayer Trial of 1849** (free trade) and the **Red River Resistance of 1869–70** (land rights).

### **Conclusion**

## The Anglo-Métis are not a separate history but an "integral part of the Métis Nation and its national narrative," emerging alongside the French-speaking Métis as heirs to the same complex fur-trade legacy.

# Chapter-by-Chapter Notes (Full-Text Read-Through)

**Book:** Scott Berthelette, _Heirs of an Ambivalent Empire: French-Indigenous Relations and the Rise of the Métis in the Hudson Bay Watershed_ (McGill-Queen's University Press, 2022). McGill-Queen's Studies in Early Canada series; derived from his U. Saskatchewan PhD under Robert Englebert.

**The argument in one paragraph:** From 1663 to 1782 the French empire tried repeatedly to make the Hudson Bay watershed an imperial space — for furs, for alliance, and above all for a fantasized Western Sea route to the Pacific. Every official project failed, because the watershed was and remained an Indigenous political space where kinship, not monarchy, was the structure of power, and because the empire's most effective operatives — ordinary Canadien voyageurs and coureurs de bois ("backcountry specialists") — served their own interests as often as the Crown's ("ambivalent agents of empire"). The oscillation between elite-led failure and Canadien on-the-ground success produced, wholly unintentionally, the book's real subject: a durable French-Indigenous social world in the Northwest whose "proto-generation" of children became the Métis. The empire's only permanent legacy was a people it never planned.

## Preface — La Vérendrye and the Roots of the Métis People

- Autobiographical frame: Berthelette is himself Red River Métis from St Vital, Winnipeg (Berthelet ancestors dispossessed of river lots at Pointe-à-Grouette/Sainte-Agathe after the Manitoba Act, 1870; he also claims Lagimodière/Gaboury — Riel's grandparents — among his ancestors). His MA began as an effort to _debunk_ La Vérendrye as commemorated founder-figure: instead an "ethnocentric imperial agent, lackluster explorer, and war profiteer" who trafficked in Dakota slaves.
- The pivot: more important than La Vérendrye's character is that his projects set in motion processes whose unintended outcome was a new people. The first Métis historian, A.-H. de Trémaudan (writing with the Union Nationale Métisse and elders who had been with Riel), already tied Métis origins to the voyageurs of the La Vérendrye expedition — so the 18th-century origin claim has roots in Métis oral tradition, not just archives.
- Positions the book against most Métis historiography, which anchors ethnogenesis at Seven Oaks (1816) or the Sayer trial (1849) and treats the pre-1760 period as prelude.

## Introduction

- Opens with the Tomison/Cocking vignette (1767, 1773): HBC men dismiss François Jérôme dit Latour as a "poor looking small man" / "old ignorant Frenchman," yet he commands a six-canoe flotilla, sits amidships like a leader, travels with his Cree wife and son, dines familiarly with his men (no social distance — which scandalized Cocking), keeps no night watch. The HBC's incomprehension of métissage is the book's recurring foil.
- **Key definitions:**
    - **Canadiens** = non-elite settlers (voyageurs, coureurs de bois, clerks, runaway soldiers, habitants) defined less by birthplace than by _orientation_: they looked west, not across the Atlantic. Elites (~200 seigneurial families, ~1% of a 35,000 settler population in 1725) were embedded in Versailles patronage, Marine promotion ladders, and La Rochelle/Bordeaux credit; they went home to France after 1760. Canadiens had no such ties and stayed.
    - **Ambivalent empire** = an empire that grew "in fits and starts," guided by imperfect information, built on a contested Indigenous borderland, fragmented by local interests, periodically neglected — yet at key junctures perceived as immensely valuable in Paris.
    - **Backcountry specialists** = the Canadiens whose language skills, kinship ties, and geographic knowledge the state repeatedly tried (and failed) to co-opt.
- **Theory toolkit:** James C. Scott's "legibility" (the state simplifying reality through maps/censuses to extract wealth) and non-state spaces / "friction of terrain" (the watershed as a zone where Natives and Canadiens evaded state power — though unlike Scott's Zomia hill peoples, Canadiens kept working colonial patronage channels back east); Kenneth Banks's "imperial overstretch" (information overload paralyzing the Bureau des Colonies); Dawdy's "rogue colonialism"; and a "post-middle-ground" stance — contra Richard White, the watershed never had the balance of power or mutual accommodation a middle ground requires. The French were _incorporated_ into female-centred Indigenous kinship systems, not accommodated as equals. Kinship, not monarchs, structured power.
- **Métis historiography:** follows Peterson, Andersen, Gaudry & Leroux in defining Métis by self-ascription and political consciousness, NOT mixedness ("Métis-as-mixed" rejected; Andersen: recognize "Métisness," not "mixedness"). Not all mixed-descent people became Métis. But against "Red River myopia," Berthelette argues the ethnogenesis story starts in the 18th-century watershed — the Saskatchewan valley was one of the "many roads" to Red River.
- Geography: four physiographic zones (Hudson Bay Lowlands, Canadian Shield boreal forest, aspen parkland, northern Great Plains) and three river systems that structure the narrative (Winnipeg River/boundary waters; Red-Assiniboine; Saskatchewan — "the backbone of the fur trade economy"). Lake Winnipeg as the "beating heart" of the watershed; Rockies as the study's western wall.
- Terminology table (as in my intro notes above): Nakoda for Assiniboine ("stone enemy" being an Anishinaabe exonym), Očhéthi Šakówiŋ for the Sioux (Dakota/Nakota/Lakota divisions), Cree dialect groups (Nêhiyawak /y/, Omushkego-Nehinuwak /n/, Asiniskaw Īthiniwak /th/), Monsoni = Moose doodem of the Anishinaabeg.

## Ch. 1 — The Battle for the Hudson Bay Watershed, 1663–1714

- Context: Iroquois Wars shatter the Wendat (1649) and expose New France's fragility → 1663 royal takeover (charter of the Cent-Associés revoked; governor general, intendant, bishop, Sovereign Council; 1,200 Carignan-Salières troops in 1665 — though ethnohistorians credit Anishinaabe and Wendat warriors, not the regiment, with actually turning back the Haudenosaunee).
- Royal government hardens hierarchy: rank now trumps ability. Congés (licenses) go to elites; the 1681 edict forbids trading in Native villages; yet Colbert's compact-colony vision (mercantilism, agrarian Saint Lawrence core, fear of depopulating France, furs worth <7% of West Indies exports — 100K–600K livres/yr vs ~100M livres of Antillean exports by the 18th c.) is constantly undone by the pull of the west. Colbert backs the Compagnie du Nord (1682) only grudgingly, to spite the English.
- **Jesuits as "apostles of empire"** (McShea): lobbyists, interpreters, spies, chroniclers; conversion doubles as sovereignty-extension and francisation — official ideology saw intermarriage as _assimilation_ (one-way dilution of "Native blood"), not métissage (Havard; Gaudry & Leroux). Sault Sainte-Marie mission = gateway to the watershed; Jesuit Relations feed Colbert's information state.
- **Sault Sainte-Marie massacre, 1674:** Ojibwe convene a peace with Mdewakanton Dakota (releasing 80 prisoners); visiting Crees, fearing a Dakota-Ojibwe alliance would cut them out of the Great Lakes trade, sabotage it — attacking the Dakota envoys in the French chapel. Ten Dakota ambassadors and 40+ Ojibwes/Crees die; the mission burns. Result: a generations-long Cree-Nakoda vs Očhéthi Šakówiŋ war, and proof of Jesuit powerlessness in Indigenous geopolitics.
- **Radisson & Des Groseilliers:** the alternative reading of 1659–60 — Ojibwe oral tradition has the two "Caesars" rescued from starvation (eating their own wool blankets) and nursed at Zhaagawaamikong; the Feast of the Dead at Lac Courte Oreilles was Ojibwe-organized, with the Frenchmen as fortunate witnesses, not orchestrators. Arrested and fined by Gov. d'Argenson for unlicensed trading → defection → HBC charter (2 May 1670, Rupert's Land). Lured back to the Compagnie du Nord (Hayes River post, 1682); Radisson's Cree kinship diplomacy is the model of the genre — the weeping "discourse of pity," "I want to be your son," the robe-covering adoption ceremony. After Colbert's death (1683) kills his patronage prospects, Radisson re-defects (1684) and hands the Hayes posts and even his French crew to the HBC — his adopted Cree father's loyalty followed _him_, not any flag or company. Empire in the watershed waxed and waned with two rogues' career moves.
- **Du Lhut:** petty noble turned illegal trader; 1678–79 winters at the Sault; plants the royal arms in the Dakota village of Izatys (Mille Lacs, 1679); brokers (and inflates) a Cree-Nakoda-Ojibwe-Dakota peace at Fond-du-Lac, sealing it with arranged intermarriages; rescues Hennepin (1680); denounced by Intendant Duchesneau as chief of 500–800 coureurs de bois, then rehabilitated — La Barre makes him ambassador/peacemaker (1683) and he delivers hundreds of Great Lakes warriors to the campaigns of 1684 and 1687. His Lake Nipigon posts (incl. Fort La Tourette) were _advertised_ (by him and by cartographers de Fer and Jaillot) as blocking the Nakoda/Cree route to the Bay — a geographic fiction, since the real artery was the Hayes-Nelson via Lake Winnipeg (cites Ray & Freeman here). The posts were really a private Lake Superior profit machine (800 used beaver robes at Michilimackinac, 1684; his brother La Tourette turned away 1,300 disappointed Native traders in 1687 for want of goods). Classic Dawdy "rogue colonialism." Also knew when not to push: nearly came to blows with voyageurs who looted castor gras offerings from the sacred trees below Saint-Anthony Falls.
- **Nicolas Jérémie dit Lamontagne:** the ability-over-nobility counterexample. Interpreter/clerk at Fort Bourbon (= captured York Factory, taken by d'Iberville 1694), ambush leader (holding off an HBC landing with 14 men, 1696), author of a mercantilist prospectus of the region (walrus ivory, muskox wool — he had stockings knitted from it "finer than silk," beluga oil ~600 barrels/yr); his (annulled) 1693 marriage to the Innu woman Marie-Madeleine Tetaouiskoué signals his Algonquian fluency. When he visited France in 1707 the Swampy Cree refused to deal with anyone else ("the natives insist on the return of Jérémie with incredible enthusiasm") — so the ministry, haunted by the Radisson precedent, rushed him back as _governor_ of Fort Bourbon (1708–14).
- Kinship as hard constraint: the 1712 caribou-hunters episode — eight Frenchmen feast beside a starving, powderless Cree camp and refuse to share; the Crees kill seven of them as kin-breakers (Isham's later version blames attempted rape of Cree women — either way, violation of country custom = death). Commandant Martigny accidentally shoots a chief's child and becomes persona non grata. Demography made the rules: Crees in the thousands, Frenchmen in the dozens.
- Conclusion: Utrecht (1713) hands the Bay to Britain; 5 Sept 1714 Fort Bourbon surrendered to Knight and Kelsey. Versailles undoes decades of on-the-ground work "in an instant" for peace in Europe — the ambivalent empire in miniature.

## Ch. 2 — Transatlantic Networks, Backcountry Specialists, and French Imperial Projects, 1715–1729

- Post-Utrecht/post-Louis XIV reorganization: Regency of the Duke of Orléans (1715–23), court to the Palais-Royal, ministries become councils (Conseil de Régence, Marine Council). France reads Utrecht's Article 10 narrowly — only the _shoreline_ and Strait went to Britain, so the interior watershed is fair game via the Great Lakes. The "Long Peace" (1713–40) = New France's "Golden Age": recovery, Louisbourg, expansion beyond the pays d'en haut, all without provoking Britain.
- **The Western Sea as imperial fiction:** the Delisle cartographic dynasty (Claude, then Guillaume — a géographe de cabinet who never left Paris) hypothesizes an inland sea at 43°–45° with a strait to the Pacific and a rich city (Quivira) on its shore, kept secret from rival mapmakers for years. **Abbé Jean Bobé**, a Lazarist at emptied-out Versailles, is the "broker of information" connecting Delisle to missionaries (Le Marie in Louisiana), officers (Sabrevois), and coureurs de bois; his own map names the whole Trans-Mississippi West "Bourbonie" and claims it connects to Tartary — complete with captive-Chinese-woman stories, Israelite migration theories, and Raudot's claim that Dakota "contains some Chinese pronunciations." Le Sueur's second-hand Dakota tale of silk-robed men in stone fortresses 15 days west; Lahontan's dubious "Rivière Longue" (mapped from a Mozeemlek slave's deerskin drawing — Bobé begged Delisle to erase it); Jérémie's "frizzy-haired" visitors telling of a west-flowing river near Lake Winnipegosis; and **Jacques de Noyon's** 1715 Bégon interviews (recalling his 1688 Kaministiquia–Rainy Lake wintering, plus a Nakoda tale of a war road to a nation of dwarves) — all of it, however quixotic, drives policy. Charlevoix's fact-finding tour (interviewing voyageurs; praising coureur de bois **Jean Viennay-Pachot**, with his seashell "evidence," as the most knowledgeable man in Canada) concludes the Sea lies at 40°–50° and recommends a Sioux Country post — a jurisdictional win for Governor Vaudreuil (Canada) over Louisiana, engineered partly out of his feud over the 1717 transfer of the Illinois Country.
- Point: whether the Sea existed was almost irrelevant; ambiguous knowledge sufficed to drive an "extra-Atlantic" global-empire dream and made the watershed a prized asset. The "reporting hierarchy" (Banks) traded information for honours; the state's legibility project betrayed its own fantasies.
- **Vaudreuil and the coureurs de bois:** knowing the state couldn't police the interior, he reframed illegal traders as assets — 1716 amnesty (First Fox War service: Bisaillon, Pachot); congés to Guillet at Témiscamingue to intercept Fort Albany/Moose traffic; the frank admission that coureurs de bois "are not accustomed to working the land, nor would they ever adjust" so they "must be looked upon as useful for the colony." (He'd already used 70 coureurs de bois + 30 Mohawks to hit Fort Albany in 1710.)
- **La Noue's state expedition (1717–21) fails** — posts planned for Kaministiquia, Rainy Lake, Lake of the Woods, Lake Winnipeg; he never gets past Kaministiquia. Why: the Ojibwe-Cree vs Dakota war (ongoing since 1674), Dakota memories of French violence against the Mesquakie (Fox Wars), and — crucially — Louisiana-based coureurs de bois already arming the Dakota, giving them the economic freedom to refuse official French overtures. Pachot's 1718 embassy is answered by a Dakota raid on Kaministiquia (17 dead).
- **Rogue foreign policy:** coureurs de bois married into Dakota thióšpayes (Wahpeton oral tradition of a 50-brides exchange; the 1708 John K. Bear winter count noting "a white man joined in a sacred ceremony"; Lusignan's mission to repatriate them — they refused to leave their wives), and then _fought alongside the Dakota against the Nakoda_ — the HBC's trading partners — in at least ten raids 1718–29, culminating in a full assault on Fort Albany itself (June 1729, four night attacks, incendiary device, one coureur de bois shot by Rowland Waggoner). All of this flatly contradicted the official Pax Gallica and the Regency's Anglo-French détente — yet it carved out the very French presence the state wanted. HBC returns at Albany fall from 15,900 MB (1725) to 11,000 (1735); factors report Natives "clothed in French clothing" everywhere.
- Coda: the groundwork (kinship, not conquest) is laid; Fort Beauharnois goes up in Sioux Country (1727). Backcountry specialists are now "holders of the keys to colonialism."

## Ch. 3 — Pierre Gaultier de La Vérendrye and the Western Posts, 1731–1743

- Regime change: Vaudreuil dies (1725); Maurepas (minister of marine from 1723, defensive-empire strategist) installs **Beauharnois** (1726–48) precisely because he has _no_ Canadien connections. Beauharnois reverts to strict ancien régime protocol: elites over experience. In 1729 he commissions La Vérendrye — noble, Malplaquet veteran (nine wounds, a year as POW), commandant of the poste du Nord — to build the _postes de la Mer de l'Ouest_. Passed over: de Noyon (62) and Jérémie (61), the only living Frenchmen who actually knew the country. La Vérendrye's proposal to bring _Abenaki_ guides (from the Maritimes!) reveals his naivety; even Beauharnois balked.
- **Auchagah's map:** the Cree trader's charcoal-on-birchbark route map (plus testimony of Cree leaders Pako, Lefoye, Petit Jour, Tacchigis, La Marteblanche) is copied on parchment by military engineer Chaussegros de Léry, shipped to Paris, and folded by Buache into the Delisle world-picture — Indigenous geographic knowledge institutionalized into the imperial fantasy. Financing: Crown pays almost nothing; Montreal merchants form the Associés de la Mer de l'Ouest (9 partners incl. La Vérendrye, son Jean-Baptiste, nephew La Jemerais) with a 3-year regional monopoly.
- Reality on the ground = a two-coalition war: **Cree-Monsoni-Nakoda (north) vs Dakota + Zhaagawaamikong-Ojibwe (south)** — the latter a recent compact giving the Dakota access to the La Pointe post and the La Pointe Ojibwe access to the bison grounds. The 1731 engagé mutiny at Grand Portage (fear of the 14-km portage and the war zone) forces a winter at Kaministiquia; Jesuit Mesaiger salvages a party to build Fort Saint-Pierre (Rainy Lake, 1731); then Fort Saint-Charles (Lake of the Woods, 1732, HQ) and Fort Maurepas (lower Red River near Lake Winnipeg, 1734). The Nakoda initially avoid the French — coureurs de bois had armed the Dakota against them and spread word "the French wanted to eat them."
- **"It is the Frenchman!" (1734):** Cree/Monsoni leaders pressure La Vérendrye to send Jean-Baptiste on a raid against the Yankton/Yanktonai. He agonizes ("Who could tell whether my son would ever return?"), consults his veteran voyageurs (who shrug — Frenchmen have always gone to war with allies), sings the war chant, shows his Malplaquet scars, and consents. Mid-battle the Cree/Monsoni shout "It is the Frenchman!" at the enemy — a deliberate ruse (as Beauharnois later grasped) to brand the French as co-belligerents and sever any French-Dakota commerce. The French are now pawns in an Indigenous war.
- **Lake of the Woods raid (June 1736):** ~90–100 Očhéthi Šakówiŋ warriors first capture merchant René Bourassa (spared when his Dakota slave redirects them to a "worthier target"), then surround Jean-Baptiste, Father Aulneau, and 19 voyageurs on Massacre Island: all 21 killed, scalped; the heads wrapped in beaver robes to mock "the French love for beaver"; Aulneau left kneeling with an arrow in his side; Jean-Baptiste headless, his back scored, a stake in his side. Largest single loss of voyageur life in Northwest history (bigger than anything in the Pemmican War). Aftermath: Fort Saint-Charles fortified in terror; summer operations paralyzed; Saint-Pierre's garrison abandons Fort Beauharnois in Sioux Country (May 1737) after Dakota scalp dances outside the palisade (one warrior wearing Aulneau's Jesuit seal as an ear trophy; chief Oüakantapé coldly announcing all was done with "reflection and design"); the Dakota also break with the Zhaagawaamikong-Ojibwe (who likely refused to attack the French), pushing that group into the northern coalition.
- **Vengeance politics:** Beauharnois, with the army bogged down against the Chickasaw and the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ numbering 24–30,000, orders reconciliation. But Crees/Monsonis/Nakoda had _adopted Jean-Baptiste as a chief_ and demanded La Vérendrye avenge him; his refusal made him incomprehensible — a father who wouldn't avenge his own son. Monsoni ogimaa **La Colle** ("The Glue") strips him of standing: "It is no longer you who are taking any part in it; it is I and the chiefs of the three tribes."
- **La Colle's raid and the slave trade (1741–43):** declaring "the Sioux are only good to be eaten," La Colle leads 200 warriors, kills ~70 Dakota, takes ~200 captives (the column stretching 800 feet into Fort Saint-Charles; Beauharnois: "more slaves than packages [of fur]"; possibly a mourning-war response to the 1737–39 Rainy Lake smallpox — Hackett). La Vérendrye, 40,000 livres in debt, buys and ships Dakota slaves to Montreal via Michilimackinac _in defiance of Beauharnois's explicit ban_; sons follow suit (Louis-Joseph "the Chevalier" gifting/selling baptized slave boys at Michilimackinac; taking his slave Étienne to France in 1761). Officers Marin and Verchères return slaves on orders; La Vérendrye never stops, even boasting of slaves procured. The 1742 Montreal conference (Oüakantapé confronting Beauharnois amid enslaved kinsmen working the town; four slaves returned) is undermined from the west — slavery becomes the Cree-Monsoni-Nakoda coalition's _device_ to make any French-Dakota peace impossible. Maurepas's verdict: La Vérendrye sought "a sea of beaver," not the Western Sea.
- Chapter thesis confirmed: the elite failed; Indigenous coalitions steered the French; imperial sovereignty was never actualized.

## Ch. 4 — Canadiens at the Western Posts, 1731–1743

- The mirror chapter: same years, seen from below. Even hierarchy-obsessed Beauharnois had to lean on Canadiens in crises. **René Bourassa dit La Ronde** — notorious smuggler (fined 500 livres in 1722 for running furs to Albany NY; colluded with Montreal's governor Longueuil in 1729) — survives Lake of the Woods thanks to his Dakota slave, then debriefs Beauharnois in 1737: identifies the raiders' composition (Prairie vs Lake Sioux, plus some La Pointe Ojibwes) and counsels _disengagement_ — let the coalition war proceed, pull the French out. Beauharnois follows this pragmatic line.
- **Language & diplomacy:** sixteen-year-old **Louis Galloudek** (indentured 1738) is left six months with the Mandan to learn Nų́ų́ʔetaa íroo after La Vérendrye's interpreter deserts; returns (escorted by two Nakoda as promised) with intelligence of horse-mounted western visitors, a Crow/Kiowa trading captain with a Spanish bridle, stone-housed Spaniards who "prayed to the great Master of Life in books" — plugging the Western Posts into the continental horse-and-gun trade. Deserter-turned-coureur-de-bois **Pennesha Gegare** (fled Fort de Chartres, Illinois, up the Missouri) supplied similar embedded expertise informally, with kin ties among Otoe, Omaha, Pawnee, Hidatsa, Arikara. Both may have married in; both likely learned Plains Sign Talk. Colonial officials hated depending on such men ("mostly lower class people, who are illiterate") and depended on them absolutely. Mastery of "Native style" rhetoric (Raudot 1709: "full of hyperboles, allusions"), war metaphors ("boiling the kettle," "eating enemies"), kin metaphors ("one and the same people"), and wampum protocol (a Wendat chief, 1703: "We were spoken empty words, that is to say without wampum") was the price of admission to diplomacy.
- **Mineral rumours as imperial fuel:** 1734 Lake Winnipeg Cree bring a slave + wampum asking for a post; La Vérendrye sends two voyageurs as ambassadors with instructions to scout mines and timber; they return with ore the HBC had thought silver — almost certainly hematite/red iron (Black Island deposit), worthless to haul east. Yet by the 1750s Bellin's maps show a "Mountain of sparkling stone following the report of the Natives" on the Rivière de l'Ouest. Lineage of the trope: Cartier's quartz "diamonds" ("faux comme les diamants du Canada"), Law's Mississippi Bubble, Le Sueur's "four thousand pounds of greenish dirt." Rumour (Dowd, Mapp) as a driver of empire.
- **Provisioning:** starvation fears proved wrong — voyageurs became the posts' primary provisioners (unlike the pays d'en haut, where posts were resupplied from Montreal). Net-fishing camps on Lake of the Woods took 4,000+ whitefish in a winter "at no expense"; Cree sturgeon weirs (La Barrière aux Esturgeons) taught technique; bison fed Fort Saint-Charles all winter 1736–37; contracts contained explicit "hunt and fish, if there is need" clauses (Beauvais, 1734) — precursor of the later hommes du nord vs mangeurs de lard distinction. Self-provisioning is what let the French keep "the pot" boiling — the feast obligation ("without the help of the pot you cannot have friendship") on which alliance depended.
- **Labour history:** canoe hierarchy (milieu → devant/gouvernail → guide, the last making life-or-death portage/rapids calls — e.g. guide Alexandre Bissonet's contracts); wages 150–350 livres, up to 500–1,000 for specialists (2–3× Saint Lawrence wages); officers as passengers performing a "theatre of authority" (Podruchny) — Jean-Baptiste was "not as accustomed to fatigue as you"; old-timer voyageurs prized (the 77-year-old Giguière still snowshoeing after deer); no hard divide between fur trade and farming careers (seasonal "pendular" colonization — Havard). **Resistance:** endemic side-trading (Lorimier ruining merchant Lamarque's returns, 1739; voyageur Jean-Baptiste Pion suing La Vérendrye for 500 livres withheld _because_ he'd gone off trading on his own, 1741 — the Maugras/Nolan partnership dissolves that year), mutiny (Grand Portage 1731), refusal (nobody would walk to the Mandan with him in 1737), desertion into Indigenous communities. Canadiens simultaneously made empire possible and set its limits.

## Ch. 5 — Métissage and Kinship: Voyageurs and Coureurs de Bois, 1743–1759

- Frame vignette: Smith & Waggoner (HBC, 1757) hosted at Fort Bourbon by François Jérôme — meat, brandy, tobacco, no bread — and his astonishing line: "What if the King of England and the King of France are at wars together, that is no reason why we should, so let us be friends." Interdependence as power in Indian Country.
- **War of the Austrian Succession (1740–48; King George's War in America) ends the Golden Age:** Louisbourg falls (1745), the Gulf blockaded, trade goods scarce and expensive, gift diplomacy crippled, voyageur contracts and congés plummet (his graph: notarized contracts collapse in the late 1740s and again in the Seven Years' War), HBC returns _rise_ (39,505 lb beaver 1746–47 → 52,716 lb 1747–48; Albany 8,900 MB early 1740s → 12,100 by 1749). Beauharnois's planned 1745 French-Indigenous assault on the James Bay posts is quietly abandoned — the empire can't even mount a raid.
- **Elite failure, final act:** de Noyelles (1744–46) arrives with half La Vérendrye's trade goods, can't renew kinship, hears the Ojibwe have "accepted the hatchet from the English," resigns in fear. The posts then sit without a commandant **1746–50 — the hinge of the book**: four years in which voyageurs pass from contract labour to total autonomy. **Saint-Pierre (1750–53)** is the type-specimen of failure: liquor profiteer (3,000 livres of brandy in his cellar at death; Élisabeth Bégon: brandy "very much in fashion at the Western Sea, thanks to Saint-Pierre"), slave trader, no warrior/diplomat reputation, plans to personally _lead_ a Cree beaver hunt (culturally absurd). The **staged Fort La Reine "attack" (22 Feb 1752)** — 200 Nakoda overwhelm the fort; Saint-Pierre's tale of single-handedly repelling them with a firebrand over the powder magazine; no one hurt, nothing taken — reads (via White's "cultural fictions") as Nakoda theatre: punishment and humiliation for un-kin-like behaviour, calibrated to keep reconciliation possible. They burn the fort four days after he leaves it in their care. Niverville meanwhile lies bedridden with malnutrition at Fort Paskoya — Natives felt no obligation to feed officers who brought no goods. Duquesne's 1753 report to Rouillé: total failure.
- **The gens libres precursor:** coureurs de bois estimates climb (7 west of Superior in 1700–01 → ~30 at Mackinac under Beauharnois → **~200 in the watershed by 1759, per HBC York Factory estimates**). They build unofficial posts everywhere — Cedar Lake, Moose Lake, north Lake Winnipeg, lower Saskatchewan, Fort à Blondeau & Pointe-aux-Trembles (Assiniboine-Souris confluence), Portage du Île, Fort Bourguignon — nearly _doubling_ the French footprint; they trade en dérouine straight into camps; HBC returns sag and factors wail (Isbister 1743: "all Indians... clothed with French cloth"). Some even solicit _contraband trade with the HBC itself_ (Jérôme's letter to York Factory offering "a little private trade" — and asking them to fix his broken oboe, the first musical instrument recorded in the watershed; the 1746 letter Robson glimpsed proposing to buy English heavy goods — guns, kettles, hatchets — that French logistics couldn't carry). HBC counters with inland winterers: Henday's 1754–55 trip (guided by Attickasish) to court the "Archithinue"/Niitsítapi, then Smith & Waggoner's five trips (1756–63), Batt, etc.
- **Métissage catalogued:**
    - _Dress:_ "cultural cross-dressing" both ways (Shannon; White) — Henday's trader "dressed very genteel yesterday... to day he is dressed like the natives excepting his white ruffled shirt"; summer voyageur kit = oversized shirt + brayet, barefoot; winter = capote, mitasses, moccasins, toque; Natives adopting French jackets, caps; cloth & sewing gear ≈ 60% of trade merchandise (Sleeper-Smith). Miskouensa's powdered wig at the 1701 Great Peace as the mirror image.
    - _Religion:_ "lived Catholicism" (Pigeon) without priests — lay baptisms/marriages/burials; long pilgrimages to Michilimackinac for sacraments (Du Jaunay, Lamorinie baptizing Rainy Lake and Kaministiquia children and slaves; Bourassa and Langlade families as godparents knitting the watershed to the Lakes); syncretism at manidoo sites (voyageur "baptisms" at Pointe aux Baptêmes below Oiseau Rock, where tobacco-tipped arrows blackened the cliff; muttered prayers to the Mackinac manidoo); the 1908 St Boniface excavation of Fort Saint-Charles finding rosary beads (and keys) with the 1736 dead; miracle folklore (thunderclap at Aulneau's death; prayers to Father Marquette on Lake Michigan); Saint Anne and the Virgin as voyageur patrons; Harmon later meeting Indigenous people on the Assiniboine-Souris reciting French prayers taught "fifty years prior" — surely fur-trade children.
    - _Food:_ "fusion cuisine" — pemmican + brandy + half a biscuit at Jérôme's table; sagamité in the Lakes but corn only via Mandan trade in the watershed; Cree-HBC goose-hunt feasts (3–4,000 geese/yr provisioned); Eucharistic _pemmican_ hosts in later North Dakota tradition; and the papal classification of beaver as _fish_ for Lent (Kalm).
- **Marriage à la façon du pays:** bona fide marriages (Van Kirk), Indigenous-initiated and Indigenous-regulated: parental consent + bride price (blankets, cloth, kettles, alcohol); women as "negotiators of change" (Brown, Van Kirk, Sleeper-Smith) managing food, clothing, snowshoes, canoes, and access to kin trade networks. Official France condemned intermarriage by the early 1700s (mésalliance discourse, "never mixing a bad blood with a good one," mixed children as "idlers, libertines and rascals") — and Canadiens ignored it. Anishinaabe doodem system detail: exogamy rules, patrilineal clan identity, the French-father problem solved by maternal-grandfather adoption (Bohaker) or by treating the French as a nascent doodem of their own (St-Onge) — shared origin (Saint Lawrence), shared manidoo (trade goods), shared religion. Grand Portage Ojibwe as gatekeepers converting portage access into marriage alliance. Not romanticized: abuses, abductions, abandonment (Small, McDonald of Garth, Nelson, Connolly), Dene women seized for debts in the later Athabasca rivalry (Rushforth on slave-concubinage as sexual violence; Bourassa's slave as the ambiguous case — loyalty within radical power asymmetry).
- Conclusion: by the late 1750s the French-Indigenous social world no longer needs the empire. Métissage + the officer vacuum = the seedbed.

## Ch. 6 — From Métissage to Métis, 1760–1782

- Against the "vanishing Frenchman" myth: the Conquest removes the _elite_, not the Canadiens. When Montreal pedlars (Alexander Henry the elder, Finlay, Pond, the Frobishers) arrive from 1767, they find cohesive French-Indigenous communities already in place and are forced to hire Canadiens because "the English did not know the way" (Pink's line about Jérôme's crew). Between 1754 and 1775 the HBC makes 56 inland wintering trips and keeps bumping into resident Frenchmen: Tomison's 1769 voyageur who had _forgotten French_; Cocking's "poor forlorn French-man" of 1772, ten years with a Nakoda band. Named continuities: Henri Janot dit Bourguignon (post at Ball Lake, remembered decades later by an old Cree woman; Sutherland souveniring a crumbling log in 1793), Jean-Baptiste Proulx (on the lower Saskatchewan from 1754, soliciting HBC men for tobacco, shoes, and "a fiddle with some strings"), Maurice-Régis Blondeau (Fort à Blondeau, 1767), Charles Boyer ("Paquatick," Rainy Lake since 1744, canoe-builder). Langlade family (French-Odawa, Michilimackinac/Green Bay) as the pays d'en haut parallel. British officialdom (Carleton, Gage) concedes total dependence on Canadien skills.
- **François Jérôme dit Latour, career study:** voyageur from La Prairie (1743) → independent négociant (1757, hiring his own voyageurs via Michilimackinac) → post-Conquest partner of Baxter, then **James Finlay** (the François-Finlay Fort below the Saskatchewan Forks near Nipawin, 1768–73 — excavated by Kehoe & Meyer: arrowheads and Indigenous pottery beside clay pipes, beads, gun parts = hybridity in the dirt), then Paterson & Holmes (Fort à la Corne, 1774, within a wider Frobisher-Todd-McGill risk-pooling web). Cree names: "Saswee"/"Shash"/"Franceway" (either from _saswekittew_, "to scatter/sow," or a Cree phonetic rendering of "François"). Married à la façon du pays to a Cree woman, children on the Saskatchewan; recruits his nephew **Pierre Jérôme** (b. 1740, La Prairie) in 1769 — parish-and-kin recruitment (Greer, St-Onge, Englebert) bridging the Saint Lawrence and the Northwest; Cree parallel-cousin reckoning would have made Pierre a "brother" to his half-Cree cousins. Hospitality as strategy: feasts Smith & Waggoner (1757), Cocking (1773), Hearne (1774); rescues and re-clothes the robbed HBC man Robert Flatt and refuses payment — entangling newcomers in reciprocity, the Indigenous grammar of power. Hutchins's grudging diagnosis: Canadiens succeed by living as "Indianized Frenchmen," drinking, singing, conjuring with the Natives "like one of themselves" — egalitarianism that unnerved class-bound HBC men.
- Scale shift: from ~30 voyageurs/yr under La Vérendrye to ~300/yr by 1780 (McTavish/McGill petition) and 400+ resident by Tomison's 1786 estimate; 60 canoes past Grand Portage in 1774; a 120-man blockade ruining Hudson House trade on the frozen North Saskatchewan (1778); York Factory returns 33,000 MB (1760) → 18,000 (1768). NWC coalescing from 1779 (McTavish, Frobisher). Berthelette's demographic argument: without this influx of fresh Canadiens absorbed as kin by the resident métisé families, the tiny French-Indigenous world would likely have been absorbed into Indigenous communities; with it, an independent people became demographically possible. Red River census back-projections (1827–35 data) show Métis births climbing decade over decade from the 1760s — undercounting anyone who died early or stayed at Pembina, Qu'Appelle, Île-à-la-Crosse, Carlton, Fort-des-Prairies (the Jérômes themselves are missing, being at Pembina).
- **Louis Primeau ("Nick'a'thu'tin"), career study:** voyageur (Nipigon contract, 1749, age 22) → six years living with his Cree family → walks into York Factory in 1766 seeking work. Jacobs re-Europeanizes him with gifts of his own clothes (Sophie White's sartorial threshold-crossing) and beds him _inside_ the factory; Graham calls him "an able & serviceable Man for your Interest Inland." 1766–72: winters annually with Cree/Nakoda, pushes the HBC to build inland (→ Cumberland House, 1774), brings down 20-canoe flotillas. But Cocking never trusts him ("a secret kindness for his old Masters"), and in 1773 Primeau deserts to the pedlars — forfeiting his wages, souring the HBC on Canadien hires until the Pemmican War era. With the Frobishers he strangles the Churchill River trade (Dene arriving at Churchill already clothed by pedlars), gets "Primo's Lake" onto Turnor's maps ("the farthest house in this Country"), and by 1776 winters at **Île-à-la-Crosse** — the strategic hinge between the Saskatchewan, the Churchill, and (via Methye Portage) the Athabasca "El Dorado." His son Joseph (b. 1785, Cree mother, raised at NWC Cumberland House where Louis was master into the Thompson era) becomes an interpreter at Fort Edmonton; descendant Jean-Baptiste Primot of St Norbert likely helps build Riel's La Barrière across the Pembina Trail in 1869.
- **Smallpox 1780–82 as demographic pivot:** the continental epidemic (out of Mexico City, 1779) kills one-half to two-thirds of many bands — the Basquia Cree of Opāskweyāw effectively "extinct except one child" (Tomison); "not one in fifty" surviving among Pegogamaw Cree and Nakoda on the Saskatchewan; Rainy Lake and "the assineybols country... depopulated." Thompson and Franklin record the permanent diminution. Consequences: a power vacuum along the whole Saskatchewan trade corridor; Cree/Nakoda formally invite Ojibwes west into emptied lands ("to eat out of the same dish"); Canadien-Ojibwe marriages (some to epidemic widows) seed many Métis genealogies (Ens & Sawchuk: the plains Métis "emerged in the shadow of smallpox epidemics"). Métisé Canadiens + Indigenous wives + their children fill the vacuum → permanent communities (Île-à-la-Crosse foremost — Macdougall's "proto-generation": children bearing their fathers' surnames but living in their mothers' homelands, intermarrying with each other). The demographic _balance_ (Cree collapse + Canadien influx) is what lets a distinct people, rather than absorption in either direction, emerge.
- Conclusion: the Saskatchewan valley as a "road to Red River" — the crucible in which Jérôme, Primeau, and other families' Métis identities formed before the 19th-century move to the Red River Valley for the bison/pemmican economy.

## Epilogue — A Métis Homeland

- Closes the Jérôme thread across four generations: Pierre Jérôme dies at Fort Carlton, autumn 1821, "upwards of 80," longtime NWC Cree interpreter (Fort Edmonton 1799–1804); married à la façon du pays to an Oji-Cree woman, Virginia. Son Martin (père): Alexander Henry the Younger's interpreter at Fort Vermilion (1809–10), trading en dérouine in Cree camps. Grandson Martin (fils): born at Fort Edmonton, called by Henry a "half Breed young man" and "Native of Fort des Prairie," rated one of the two best guides for Robertson's 1822 Edmonton trip — a man with _no remaining ties_ to the Saint Lawrence. In spring 1825 Martin (fils) follows his mentor Jean-Baptiste Letendre dit Batoche down the 1,450-km Carlton Trail by Red River cart to the colony (a few hundred people, the new Catholic mission's white steeple, Fort Garry, herds of a continental population of ~50 million bison sometimes within sight), and that June marries Letendre's sister Angelique. Fur-trade work as intergenerational vocation; migration to Red River as the typical Métis family trajectory.
- Restates the thesis: the ambivalent empire = the standing tension between the metropole's _rêve fou_ (Western Sea, mines, Northwest Passage — a dream that later killed Franklin's crews too) and the local interests of Canadiens and Indigenous peoples. Every stated imperial goal failed; the unintended one — a new Indigenous nation — endured. The watershed was never a middle ground: power was never balanced; the French were incorporated, not accommodated. The 18th-century crucible made the people; the 19th-century "hammer and anvil" (Selkirk settlement, HBC monopoly, Dominion of Canada) forged the national consciousness.

## Appendix — The HBC and the Anglo-Métis

- Acknowledges the other stream of the Métis Nation: the Anglo-Métis ("half-breed," "country-born") — Protestant, English/Bungi-speaking, settled in St Andrews and St Clements parishes, more often farmers/merchants though still hunting.
- Periodization: (1) **1670–1770, official celibacy** — London Committee forbade fraternization; only ~15 documented country marriages, almost all post heads (Pilgrim, the Nortons, Isham, Marten) whose wives lived _in_ the factories; sons like Charles Price Isham schooled in England and absorbed into company ranks, daughters absorbed into Cree kin — no distinct ethnic group yet. Cree bands themselves pushed these marriages to bind the posts into reciprocity. (2) **Late 1770s, the inland move** — servants trading en dérouine from Cumberland House, Nipawin, Hudson House, Manchester House marry widely; their children live mostly in Indigenous bands, working informally as provisioners. (3) **By 1800** — celibacy policy dead; labour hunger makes Native-born youths the apprentice pool and Native-born daughters (skilled, kin-connected) the preferred wives, linking new apprentices to veteran officers by "mutual ties of sentiment." (4) **1821 merger** — Simpson closes posts, sacks over half the workforce, and the leaner hierarchy racializes; displaced country-born families move to Red River for land, schools, churches.
- Against Pannekoek's Catholic-vs-Protestant hostility thesis: community tradition and non-clerical sources show cooperation — shared buffalo hunts, cart trains and York boat brigades, intermarriage, and joint political action at the Sayer trial (1849) and the Red River Resistance (1869–70). The Anglo-Métis are "an integral part of the Métis Nation and its national narrative."

## Cross-cutting themes worth remembering

- **Ability vs nobility** is the book's engine: whenever the state promoted rank (Beauharnois → La Vérendrye, Saint-Pierre) it failed; whenever it swallowed its pride and used backcountry specialists (Radisson, Jérémie, Bourassa, Galloudek, Primeau) it got results — and whenever it got results, the specialists' own agendas fragmented imperial control anyway. Hence "ambivalent agents of empire."
- **Kinship is the constitution of the watershed.** Adoption, marriage à la façon du pays, gift reciprocity, "the pot," bride price, doodem and thióšpaye incorporation — every successful French actor entered through these doors; every disaster (the 1712 killings, Martigny, Saint-Pierre's humiliations, the Dakota rupture) came from ignoring them. Incorporation, not accommodation: the post-middle-ground claim.
- **Information as currency:** the reporting hierarchy, Delisle/Bobé's cabinet geography, Auchagah's map institutionalized into fantasy, mineral rumours on official maps — imperfect knowledge didn't hinder empire; it _powered_ it (and misdirected it).
- **War as Indigenous statecraft with French pawns:** the 1674 massacre, the "It is the Frenchman!" ruse, the adoption of Jean-Baptiste and the vengeance demands, La Colle's slave gifts as alliance-lock — Indigenous coalitions consistently steered French policy, not vice versa.
- **The chronology of ethnogenesis** as Berthelette builds it: métissage practices entrenched (1690s–1750s) → officer vacuum and Canadien ascendancy (1740s–50s) → post-Conquest continuity + pedlar influx multiplying métisé numbers (1760s–70s) → 1780–82 smallpox creating the demographic space → proto-generation communities (Île-à-la-Crosse, Saskatchewan posts, Pembina) → 19th-century migration to Red River and the bison/pemmican niche → national consciousness under external threat. Crucible, then hammer and anvil.
- **Connection to Ray (_Indians in the Fur Trade_):** the two books interlock — Ray's Cree/Nakoda middleman system is the Indigenous trading world these Frenchmen married into; Ray's post-1763 pedlar invasion and provisioning economy is the stage for Berthelette's chapter 6; both treat the 1780–82 smallpox as the great demographic hinge; and Berthelette's Métis take over exactly the pemmican niche whose rise Ray charts (and whose capture _from_ the plains tribes Ray describes from the other side).
